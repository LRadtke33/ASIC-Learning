Lesson 26 / 84 · Part IV, Loads, timing and interconnect

Setup and Hold

The two checks that decide whether a flip-flop captures the bit it was meant to capture. Both checks are lookup tables, the same kind of table as a gate delay.

Where the numbers come from. The cell is sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1 at tt_025C_1v80. Delay tables are lesson 23. The short version of these two numbers is lesson 25. The transistors inside the flop are lesson 20.

What you will learn

  • State setup and hold as signed constraints measured at the capturing clock edge.
  • Interpolate the 3 × 3 setup and hold tables of dfxtp_1 between slews.
  • Compute setup and hold slack, and read WNS and TNS in a timing report.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Setup is checked from step 3, synthesis on; hold becomes real after step 6, clock-tree synthesis; both are signed off at every corner in step 8, extraction and timing.
Tools
OpenSTA reports both; OpenROAD’s repair_timing fixes them. Commercial: PrimeTime, Tempus.
Why it matters
A setup failure limits the clock speed; a hold failure breaks the chip at any speed.

Source scope. All four 3×3 constraint tables were checked against the archived dfxtp_1 TT data. The worked path below now propagates the real output slew through every lookup. It is a calculation with explicit loads, not an extracted design.

1. Two questions, asked at the capturing clock edge

A flip-flop copies D onto Q when the clock rises. The copy is reliable only if D has been sitting still for long enough before that edge, and if D keeps sitting still long enough after it. Those two waits are setup and hold.

Setup is how long D must already be the new value before the clock edge. On this cell, with both the clock edge and the data edge at 0.01 ns, the requirement is 0.1033184 ns when D is falling and 0.0508281 ns when D is rising. Falling data is the harder check. Lesson 25 rounds those two cells to 0.103 ns and 0.051 ns.

Hold constrains the earliest following data transition relative to the capture clock reference crossing. At 0.01 ns slews it is −0.0468281 ns for falling D and −0.0285176 ns for rising D. A negative hold permits that pin transition before the clock reference crossing while preserving the captured value, under the characterized conditions. In a simplified internal model, hold_external ≈ clock_delay + hold_internal − data_delay. Thus a longer internal data delay can make the external hold requirement negative. The published tables, not that simple model, set the actual limits.

In the Liberty file the arcs are timing_type : setup_rising and hold_rising on pin D, with related_pin : CLK. The rising in the name is the clock edge. rise_constraint is the check while D is rising. fall_constraint is the check while D is falling. There is no recovery or removal arc: this cell has no asynchronous set or reset pin.

The template is vio_3_3_1. variable_1 is the related-pin transition, the clock slew. variable_2 is the constrained-pin transition, the data slew. Both axes are 0.01, 0.5, and 1.5 ns. There is no load column on a constraint table. Load shows up in the clock-to-Q delay, which is still a del_1_7_7 grid on pin Q, the same shape as lesson 23.

A slow data edge needs more setup. With the clock still at 0.01 ns, falling-D setup grows from 0.1033184 ns at a 0.01 ns data edge to 0.6206849 ns at a 1.5 ns data edge. A slow clock edge moves the other way: at data slew 0.01 ns the falling-D setup is already negative at a 0.50 ns clock (−0.0108092 ns). A negative setup means the new data may arrive that long after the clock pin and the check still passes. The slow data columns stay positive across the whole clock axis.

Hold has the same sign change in the other corner of its table. At clock slew 1.50 ns and data slew 0.01 ns, falling-D hold is +0.1409486 ns. A slow clock and a sharp data edge ask D to stay valid after the clock. The shaded −0.047 ns is the easy cell of that table, the one with both edges fast. It is the wrong cell to quote for a path whose clock edge has slowed down.

3. A data slew between two columns

The same straight line as lesson 23 applies here. Hold the clock at the first row, 0.01 ns, and put the data slew at 0.20 ns. That slew sits between 0.01 ns and 0.50 ns on the falling-D setup row.

(0.20 − 0.01) / (0.50 − 0.01) = 0.19 / 0.49 = 0.387755.

0.1033184 + 0.387755 × (0.3187643 − 0.1033184) = 0.1033184 + 0.387755 × 0.2154459 = 0.186859 ns.

0.186859 ns is the line between two table cells. The cells themselves are 0.1033184 ns and 0.3187643 ns.

When both slews lie between grid points, use the four surrounding cells, load-style bilinear from lesson 23. For falling-D setup at clock slew 0.20 ns and data slew 0.20 ns the fraction on each axis is the same 0.387755. The four corners are 0.1033184, 0.3187643, −0.0108092, and 0.1997539.

Along clock slew 0.01 ns the line is the 0.186859 ns just computed. Along clock slew 0.50 ns: −0.0108092 + 0.387755 × (0.1997539 − (−0.0108092)) = 0.070838 ns. Between those two results: 0.186859 + 0.387755 × (0.070838 − 0.186859) = 0.141871 ns. Walking the axes in the other order gives the same 0.141871 ns.

4. Slack uses a propagated data edge

For a single-cycle, same-edge register path, define capture and launch clock arrival times relative to their nominal edges. Include wire delay in Tcomb. A late-data setup analysis uses late launch/data and early capture timing; hold uses early launch/data and late capture timing, with the applicable correlation and pessimism removal.

Setup slack = Tperiod + Tcapture,early − Tsetup − Usetup − (Tlaunch,late + Tcq,late + Tcomb,late)
Hold slack = Tlaunch,early + Tcq,early + Tcomb,early − (Tcapture,late + Thold + Uhold)

Here is a complete nominal teaching calculation, not a frequency guarantee. Both clock slews are 0.01 ns; clock skew, uncertainty and wire resistance are zero; the period is 1 ns. Launch and capture cells are dfxtp_1 with one inv_1 between them. Total load on launch Q is 9.00162 fF, and total load on inverter Y is 9.52062 fF. These chosen loads include the actual next pin plus extra ideal capacitance: 6.69962 fF at Q and 7.84262 fF at Y, using the published lumped pin caps. This keeps the example physically consistent with the library load columns.

For the falling-D case: arrival = 0.3279992 + 0.0776193 = 0.4056185 ns. Required time is 1 − 0.1233702 = 0.8766298 ns. Setup slack is 0.8766298 − 0.4056185 = 0.4710113 ns. That transition’s setup budget is 0.5289887 ns. Hold slack is 0.4056185 − (−0.0658574) = 0.4714759 ns.

Reading every stage at the 0.01 ns row would incorrectly give a 0.476817 ns setup budget. The actual output slew is slower, so the subsequent delays and setup requirement change. The nominal hold calculation above is not the earliest arrival across process, voltage, temperature, interconnect and variation. A real report evaluates the required early and late scenarios, both polarities and all paths.

Delaying capture by 0.050 ns improves setup slack by 0.050 ns and worsens hold slack by 0.050 ns, with other quantities fixed. A longer period improves this single-cycle setup budget; it does not repair a same-edge hold violation. Real flows also check minimum pulse width, clock gating, recovery/removal and any multicycle or generated-clock relationships.

Checkpoint: why does a slower clock not fix hold?

Hold protects data captured on the same reference edge. Its equation has clock skew and minimum data delay but no full clock-period term. Add suitable data-path delay, reduce harmful skew or change the implementation and verify all corners; do not hide the check with an unjustified timing exception.

5. The clock pulse has its own table

Pin CLK also carries min_pulse_width. The template is constraint_3_0_1: one axis, the clock slew, at 0.01, 0.50, and 1.50 ns. The rise constraint specifies the high pulse width (rising to falling); the fall constraint specifies the low width (falling to rising). Check both widths after duty-cycle distortion and clock gating.

1.50 ns2.500000 ns2.500000 ns

The two slower rows are the clock slew divided by 0.6: 0.50 / 0.6 = 0.833333 and 1.50 / 0.6 = 2.5. The 0.6 is the 20-to-80 fraction already in the library header (80% − 20% = 60% of a full swing). The file does not print that division. It is the arithmetic that matches those two rows. At a 0.01 ns clock, 0.01 / 0.6 = 0.0167 ns, and the table instead says 0.1686861 ns and 0.2082325 ns. On a sharp clock the cell's own limit is the number that binds.

The scan cell's minimum pulse width is a different pair. At slew 0.01 ns, sdfxtp_1 has rise constraint 0.1675876 ns and fall constraint 0.2400893 ns. Those belong to lesson 40.

6. PVT changes both checks

The same 0.01 ns / 0.01 ns cell, read from three library files. Setup grows in the slow file. Slow-cell/late-data scenarios often constrain setup, and fast-cell/early-data scenarios often constrain hold. These are useful starting points, not universal signoff rules. Evaluate every approved PVT and interconnect corner, early/late clock variation, temperature effects, both data polarities and all operating modes. The selected three table entries do not prove which scenario is worst for a complete path.

7. One path has a slack. The report has WNS and TNS.

For the falling-D case in section 4, arrival is 0.4056185 ns, setup required time is 0.8766298 ns and setup slack is +0.4710113 ns. For setup, slack is required minus arrival. For hold, slack is arrival minus required. Keep the check type attached to the number.

WNS summarizes the worst violation in the chosen analysis set (commonly reported as zero when there is no negative slack). TNS (total negative slack) typically sums each violating endpoint’s worst negative slack; it does not add every enumerated path to that endpoint. For endpoint slacks −0.080, −0.030 and +0.050 ns, WNS is −0.080 ns and TNS is −0.110 ns. Confirm the reporting convention and whether the report is for setup or hold. Shared-clock pessimism removal accounts for correlated common clock segments rather than treating the same segment independently early and late.

Check your understanding — five paths have setup slacks of +0.10, −0.02, −0.05, +0.30 and −0.01 ns. What are WNS and TNS?

WNS, the worst negative slack, is −0.05 ns. TNS sums the negative slacks: −0.02 − 0.05 − 0.01 = −0.08 ns. Positive slack on other paths does not offset it.

In the open SKY130 flow. In OpenSTA, report_checks -path_delay max lists the worst setup paths and -path_delay min the worst hold paths; report_wns and report_tns give the summary numbers. LibreLane stops with an error if its SetupViolations or HoldViolations checks find negative slack at signoff, and OpenROAD’s repair_timing -hold inserts delay buffers to fix hold.

Key takeaways

  • Setup and hold are signed external constraints referenced to the clock edge; positive setup precedes it and positive hold follows it. On dfxtp_1 both are 3×3 tables of clock slew and data slew.
  • At 0.01 ns edges, typical corner, falling-D setup is 0.1033184 ns and falling-D hold is −0.0468281 ns. Negative hold means D may change that early before the clock.
  • A data slew of 0.20 ns on the first clock row interpolates the falling-D setup to 0.186859 ns. Both slews at 0.20 ns interpolate to 0.141871 ns.
  • The period is in single-cycle setup slack and absent from same-edge hold slack. The slew-propagated falling-D example has a nominal setup budget of 0.528989 ns, before wire, uncertainty and variation.
  • At a sharp clock the minimum pulse width is 0.1686861 ns and 0.2082325 ns. At a slow clock the table matches the slew divided by 0.6.
  • The slow file stretches setup to 0.2601787 ns. The fast file ff_n40C_1v76 moves rising-D hold to −0.018752 ns, closer to zero.
  • Setup slack is required minus arrival; hold slack is arrival minus required. WNS reports the worst violation; TNS typically sums the worst negative slack at each endpoint.
  • Level-sensitive latches, and the time-borrowing checks they add to this edge-triggered picture, are lesson 27.
