Lesson 59 / 84 · Part VI, Physical cells and the floorplan

Routing & Timing Closure

Global and detailed routing on SKY130 met1–met5, the DRC iterations that closed three utilizations, and how a routed design feeds multi-corner STA.

Where the numbers come from. Congestion, layer wirelength, via counts, DRC iterations and longest-net figures are OpenROAD global-route and detailed-route logs, plus Odb.ReportWireLength, from the same three LibreLane 3.0.17 SPM runs described in lesson 58. Setup and hold slacks are the post-route, post-extraction OpenSTA 2.7.0 results of the 45% run, already reported in lesson 45; they are not re-derived here. Non-default rules and shielding are discussed conceptually and labelled as such. Recorded files live in labs/analysis_examples/rtl_to_gds/.

What you will learn

  • Read a GCell congestion report by layer, and say what a zero-overflow result does and does not guarantee.
  • Follow detailed-routing DRC from the first iteration to a clean result, including a run that got worse before it got better.
  • Account for wirelength by layer and for via ladders, with SKY130 pin access on li1.
  • Connect a routed database to timing closure without repeating the nine-corner STA lesson.

Why it matters
A placement that looks fine can still be unroutable, and a route that is DRC-clean can still miss timing. The logs in this lesson are how you see both.

1. Two problems: corridors, then shapes

Routing is solved twice. Global routing assigns each net to a sequence of GCells — coarse tiles on each metal layer — and reports whether demand exceeds the tracks in any tile (overflow). Detailed routing then draws actual wires on the manufacturing tracks and drops vias, iterating on design-rule errors until they reach zero, or until it gives up. LibreLane’s 45% SPM used RT_MIN_LAYER=met1 and RT_MAX_LAYER=met5, GRT_ADJUSTMENT=0.3 (about 30% of resources reserved) and GRT_OVERFLOW_ITERS=50.

li1 is the local interconnect that standard-cell pins sit on (lesson 19). In these runs the global router reports li1 resource and demand as zero: signal routing starts at met1, and li1 is used for pin access and the cell’s own paint. The track pitches the router used were li1 0.46, met1 0.34, met2 0.46, met3 0.68, met4 0.92 and met5 3.40 µm.

All three runs finished global route with zero overflow. That is necessary for a comfortable detailed route, but it is not a DRC result and it is not a timing result. The 60% run is already using 43% of met2 and has started to climb into met4 (12%); its GRT wirelength is 12,323 µm against 10,695 µm at 45%. Zero overflow means “the GCell budget closed,” not “the detailed router will have an easy time.”

3. Detailed routing and the DRC loop

The 45% detailed router started at 19 errors and 6,559 µm, then 4 errors and 6,527 µm, then 0 errors and 6,508 µm. The 30% run was 43 → 16 → 0 (final 6,613 µm). The 60% run was 95 → 15 → 21 → 0 (final 7,613 µm): iteration 2 introduced errors that iteration 1 had cleared, and a fourth iteration finished the job. Final route__drc_errors was 0 on every run. All vias were single-cut (1,991 at 45%, 1,980 at 30%, 2,136 at 60%). Antenna violations after route were 0; LibreLane had RUN_ANTENNA_REPAIR on. The antenna mechanism itself is lesson 51.

The logs also carried ten DRT-0349 warnings, “LEF58_ENCLOSURE with no CUTCLASS is not supported,” for via through via4 / mcon. They were warnings, not errors, and they appeared on all three runs. Checker.TrDRC ran after detailed routing on the 45% flow (step directory 46-checker-trdrc in that run; the number is a step index, not a lesson number).

4. Layer usage and via ladders

Final 45% detailed-route layer totals: li1 0, met1 2,524, met2 3,846, met3 29, met4 108, met5 0 µm (sum 6,507, agreeing with the 6,508 µm metric). The longest net was net60 at 173.355 µm, from a 359-net wire_lengths.csv. At 60%, met3 and met4 together carry 700 µm — a via-ladder cost as well as a length cost, which is why that run has 2,136 vias against 1,991 at 45%.

Pin access from an HD cell starts on li1 and steps up through mcon to met1 (lesson 19). A net that ends on met4 has climbed the whole via stack: mcon, via, via2, via3. Each cut has its own enclosure and resistance, which is why a timing closure that only looks at routed length is incomplete — extraction and STA, in lesson 45, see those vias.

5. NDRs and shielding — labelled conceptual

A non-default rule (NDR) assigns a net a wider width or larger spacing than the layer minimum: a clock, a sensitive analog net, or a high-current rail. Shielding runs a grounded neighbour along a victim to cut coupled noise. Neither was measured in these SPM runs — the clock used the default routing rule, and no shields were requested. The idea, and a coupling estimate, is in lesson 83. What this run did measure is default-rule layer usage and a clean antenna result.

6. Timing closure after the route

Once the wires exist, timing closure is a loop: extract parasitics, run multi-corner STA, repair the paths that fail, and re-route the repairs. On this SPM the 45% post-route, post-extraction OpenSTA result — the subject of lesson 45, not a new measurement — was:

  • Worst setup slack 3.590931 ns at max_ss_100C_1v60, MET, with WNS 0 and TNS 0 at every corner.
  • Worst hold slack 0.253127 ns at min_ff_n40C_1v95, MET, same clean WNS/TNS.
  • Implied fmax 249.11 MHz at that slow-slow max-RC corner, against a 10 ns constraint.
  • Zero max-slew, max-capacitance and max-fanout violations at every corner; design__violations 0.

Hold had already been repaired with 33 dlygate4sd3_1 cells after CTS; setup never needed a setup buffer (design__instance__count__setup_buffer: 0). For a design that does not close, the usual levers are: another repair_timing pass, a slightly slower constraint, a less aggressive utilization (the 60% WL and met3/met4 climb is the warning), or an ECO of the critical path. Those repairs were not required here and were not run as an experiment.

Wirelength feeds STA through extraction: the 6,508 µm of routed metal, and the 1,991 vias, become SPEF. TrDRC confirms the route is geometrically legal before you trust that SPEF. Filling the rows (803 fill cells in the final 45% metrics) and metal fill (lesson 61) come next, and then physical signoff in lesson 62.

Zero GRT overflow and zero DRT DRC: are they the same check?

No. Global-route overflow is a GCell capacity check — demand versus tracks in a coarse tile. Detailed-route DRC is a shape check — spacing, width, enclosure, shorts — on the actual wires. The 60% SPM had zero GRT overflow and still opened with 95 detailed-route DRC errors. Both ended at zero; you still need both reports.
