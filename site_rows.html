Site Rows & Standard Cell Placement

Why standard cells live in rows, why row height matters, and how cells snap together by abutment.

What you will learn

  • Explain sites, rows and abutment, and why ordinary cells share one height.
  • Convert a placement — site, row, orientation — into coordinates without confusing the LEF origin.
  • Check where a double-height cell may start, using the SKY130 unithd and unithddbl sites.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

step 4, floorplan and power grid creates the rows; step 5, placement puts every cell on a site.

Why it matters
Every standard cell must sit on a legal site, and the row structure decides how cells share power and wells.
Reading the evidence. Colored layouts and switch states are teaching models unless a caption explicitly identifies LEF geometry. SKY130 HD file values on this page were checked against source revision ac7fb61f06e6 on 2026-11-06; nominal Liberty values use tt_025C_1v80. Source files and scope.

1. The placement grid: sites and rows

The standard-cell placement area is divided into site rows. Each site defines a legal placement width and row height. The placer aligns a cell’s placement bounding rectangle to the row’s site grid and checks its orientation and row compatibility. This is a placement rule; it is distinct from the much finer manufacturing grid that constrains individual polygons.

2. One height, many widths

In a fixed-height row-based standard-cell library, ordinary cells have the row height and widths quantized to its site grid. Some libraries also contain special or multiheight cells with additional placement restrictions. A conceptual small inverter might occupy three sites and a flip-flop eleven; actual SKY130 HD values are listed later. Matching outlines make mixed cell types tile without vertical gaps when their rail, well and boundary requirements are compatible.

The 3-site inverter and the 11-site flip-flop in that sentence are the sizes used in these drawings. On the SKY130 high-density library the smallest inverter is also 3 sites, and the plain flip-flop is 16 sites. Section 6 has the outlines.

3. Placement by abutment

Compatible cells can be placed by abutment, with their placement edges touching. In the illustrated upright template, VDD lies along the top and VSS along the bottom. Matching rail and well shapes meet at the boundary. This joins neighboring rail segments; the row still needs connections to the power grid and correctly spaced well/substrate taps. Abutment does not supply current or guarantee that arbitrary library families are compatible.

In flipped (FS) rows the cell is mirrored top-to-bottom so its VDD rail meets the neighboring VDD rail. In an alternating stack of N rows there are N+1 boundary stripes rather than 2N separate stripes. Rail compatibility, well rules and the library’s allowed symmetries determine legal orientations; alternation is a common template, not a universal law.

4. Placement coordinates and the LEF origin

The figures mark the lower-left corner of the placed bounding rectangle. That is the convenient placement reference used by DEF after rotation or mirroring. A cell’s native drawing origin is not universally that corner: LEF can specify an ORIGIN offset, and database APIs may expose a transform origin instead. Read the format/API before converting coordinates.

x = row_x_origin + site_index × site_width
y = row_y_origin + row_index × row_height
The placement orientation is a separate N/FS/etc. attribute, not an addition to y. These expressions describe the uniform row array shown here.

In Figure 2, NAND2_X1 occupies (site 5, row 0, N), while AOI21_X1 occupies (site 20, row 1, FS). The coordinate labels describe the placed bounding boxes in this drawing. When translating them into a DEF record or a database transform, apply the format’s placement-point and LEF ORIGIN conventions rather than assuming every API uses the same origin.

5. Double-height and multi-height cells

Not every cell is one row tall. Libraries also contain cells that are an integer multiple of the row height — double-height, triple-height, and so on. They exist because some functions don't fit comfortably in one row: very high drive-strength buffers and clock drivers need large transistors, and complex cells (multi-bit flip-flops, level shifters) route better with the extra vertical space.

For multiheight cells compatible with the illustrated row architecture, placement must check the following:

  • The placed bounding rectangle uses the same site-grid reference; apply any library origin offset and orientation transform.
  • Their footprints occupy the supported site grid and row heights. A library can define a separate multiheight site or additional placement restrictions.
  • They must land where the rail pattern matches their design. The double-height BUF_X16 in Figure 2 is built with VSS at its bottom and top and VDD through its middle, so it can only start on rows whose bottom boundary is VSS (rows 0 and 2 here). Starting it on row 1 would short VDD to VSS.
  • Single-height neighbors may abut their sides only when the rail, well and edge rules permit that pairing.

The placer checks the applicable site, row, orientation and boundary constraints for the chosen library. A rectangular outline that fits geometrically is only one part of legality.

6. The SKY130 site, and the cells that sit on it

The cited sky130_fd_sc_hd technology LEF defines two placement sites: unithd, SIZE 0.46 BY 2.72 µm, and unithddbl, SIZE 0.46 BY 5.44 µm. The latter is two ordinary row heights. A cell’s own LEF SITE, symmetry and boundary shapes determine how it is placed. The double-height BUF_X16 in Figure 2 is a teaching cell, not an HD cell name.

Widths of the cells the later lessons keep using, taken from each cell's LEF SIZE and divided by 0.46 µm:

Every one of those cells is SITE unithd and CLASS CORE, except the fill cells (lesson 47), which are CORE SPACER. The power rails are met1, 0.48 µm tall, centered on the top and bottom edges. Neighboring rows draw the same rectangle on the shared edge, so the rail in the finished row is one stripe. That is the abutment in section 3, with a measured stripe instead of a colored bar.

Areas in the Liberty files match the LEF outline. inv_1 is 1.38 × 2.72 = 3.7536 µm². inv_4 is 2.30 × 2.72 = 6.256 µm². dfxtp_1 is 7.36 × 2.72 = 20.0192 µm². The placer does not need the transistors to know whether the cell fits. It needs the site, the width in sites, and the orientation.

met1 pitch in the same tech LEF is 0.34 µm, and 2.72 / 0.34 = 8 exactly. Lesson 17 counts that height as tracks and shows where the rails cover the outer ones. Vertical li1 pitch is 0.46 µm, the same number as the site width, so one local-interconnect pitch lines up with each site.

7. A two-row cell has to start on the matching rail

In the alternating-row arrangement used in these figures, a row is followed by its top-to-bottom mirror, making matching supply-rail rectangles coincide at the shared boundary. This describes the chosen architecture; actual row orientations come from the implementation’s row definitions and library restrictions.

A two-row cell covers two adjacent row heights. The HD level shifter example is 5.44 µm tall (lesson 54). For a fixed orientation and the rail pattern drawn here, compatible starting rows repeat every two rows. Other legal orientations or cell-specific rail arrangements must be checked from that cell’s abstract; height alone does not establish legal placement.

Worked example — rows, sites and utilization on the Lab 6 SPM

The HD placement site is 0.46 × 2.72 µm (section 6). Lab 6’s 45% SPM, run RUN_2026-11-11_16-09-06, has a recorded core bounding box of (5.52, 10.88) to (91.08, 95.2) µm, so the core is 85.56 × 84.32 µm:

  • Rows: 84.32 / 2.72 = 31 rows, exactly. The run’s metrics also report 31 rows.
  • Sites per row: 85.56 / 0.46 = 186 sites, exactly.
  • Core area: 31 × 186 × 0.46 × 2.72 = 7,214.4192 µm², matching the recorded core_area_um2 of 7,214.42 (rounded).
  • Standard-cell area after the flow: 4,546.86 µm² (423 cells). Utilization against the core is 4,546.86 / 7,214.42 = 0.63025 (63.0%).

The flow was asked for FP_CORE_UTIL = 45. The 63% figure is what was actually sitting in the core after placement, buffering and hold repair — fill is additional (Lab 6: 803 fill cells, 2,667.56 µm², which together with the standard cells occupy essentially the whole core). The lesson from the arithmetic is that a utilization target is an input to the floorplan, not a measurement of the finished netlist. The numbers are from Lab 6 and labs/analysis_examples/rtl_to_gds/utilization_sweep.json.

Worked checkpoint — How much row width do three cells occupy?

Place one inv_1, one inv_4 and one dfxtp_1 in a compatible single-height row. Their outlines occupy 3 + 5 + 16 = 24 sites, or 24 × 0.46 = 11.04 µm. This is the geometric width before any required placement padding. It does not prove that the surrounding pin access, taps or routing are legal.

Check your understanding — one dfxtp_1 and four inv_4 share a single row. How many sites and how many micrometers do they occupy?

dfxtp_1 is 16 sites and each inv_4 is 5, so 16 + 4 × 5 = 36 sites, or 36 × 0.46 = 16.56 µm, before any padding, taps or fill.

Key takeaways

  • A placement site defines a width and height; ordinary cells in the chosen row architecture use compatible site-quantized footprints.
  • Abutment joins compatible boundary shapes. The power grid and well/substrate taps must still connect and bias the row.
  • N/FS alternation is a common way to share rails, not a requirement for every library or row pattern.
  • Keep placement coordinates, orientation transforms and native LEF ORIGIN offsets distinct.
  • Multiheight legality depends on the complete rail/well pattern and permitted neighbors, not height alone.
  • The cited HD sites are unithd = 0.46 × 2.72 µm and unithddbl = 0.46 × 5.44 µm. inv_1 occupies 3 ordinary sites; dfxtp_1 occupies 16.
