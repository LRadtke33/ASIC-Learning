Lesson 60 / 84 · Part VI, Physical cells and the floorplan

Digital Signal Integrity: Crosstalk Delay & Glitch

Two min-width met1 wires, 100 µm long at minimum spacing, have a teaching sidewall coupling of 8.6 fF. With this course’s incremental inv_1 resistance, an opposite-direction aggressor adds about 38 ps of delay — a hand estimate, not an extracted STA delta.

Where the numbers come from. Width, spacing, thickness and sheet resistance are from the HD nom tech LEF already used in lesson 29 (met1 W=0.14 µm, minimum spacing 0.14 µm, thickness 0.35 µm, R□=0.125 Ω). The coupling used in the worked example is a parallel-plate sidewall estimate C′ = εox t / s with εox = 3.9 ε0, labelled as such; it is not an OpenRCX extraction and it is not the LEF EDGECAPACITANCE (that number is a to-ground fringe for a lone wire). The driver resistance is the incremental delay of lesson 29’s inv_1 (“about 10 ps per inv_1 load”) divided by the course-wide pin-A capacitance 2.302 fF. Waveforms are teaching drawings. Analog-victim crosstalk and return paths are lesson 83; this lesson is the digital-STA companion.

What you will learn

  • Separate a timing delta (delay pushed or pulled by a switching aggressor) from a functional glitch (a quiet victim kicked through a threshold).
  • Apply the Miller factor 0 / 1 / 2 to a hand coupling estimate.
  • State when STA will charge a delta: the aggressor’s switching window must overlap the victim’s.
  • Name the usual physical mitigations: spacing, shielding, layer change, and non-default rules (NDRs).

1. Two ways a neighbour can hurt you

A victim net and an aggressor net share a coupling capacitance Cc. When the aggressor switches, charge flows through Cc. Two consequences matter for digital logic:

  • Delta delay. The victim is itself transitioning. The aggressor either fights it (opposite direction — the victim slows) or helps it (same direction — the victim speeds up). Setup cares about the slowdown; hold cares about the speedup. This is what STA’s crosstalk-aware delay calculation is for.
  • Functional glitch. The victim is supposed to be quiet, held by a driver. The aggressor kicks a pulse. If that pulse crosses the receiver’s threshold and lasts long enough to be latched, the function is wrong, even if every setup and hold check on the intended transitions still passed.

The two are not interchangeable: a small, fast glitch can be a functional fail and almost invisible to a setup check; a slow opposite-edge overlap can be a setup fail with no quiet-net glitch at all.

2. The Miller factor is 0, 1, or 2

The current through Cc is Cc d(Vagg − Vvic)/dt. For equal, aligned ramps, the effective capacitance the victim’s driver must charge is M × Cc, with a textbook Miller factor:

Aggressor during the victim’s edgeMEffect on the victim
Quiet (held)1Cc looks like a ground capacitor
Switching in the same direction, aligned0No voltage across Cc if the ramps match; the victim is faster than the quiet case
Switching in the opposite direction, aligned2The voltage across Cc changes by 2 VDD; the victim is slower

Partial overlap, unequal slew, and a weak aggressor driver produce factors between these ideals. STA’s timing windows decide which M is even possible: if the aggressor cannot switch in the victim’s window, the tool should not charge you for M = 2. Windows that are computed too loosely (or an analysis that assumes a worst aggressor always) over-predict delta delay and you “fix” a ghost; windows that are too tight miss a real one.

3. A hand estimate on parallel met1

From the HD nom tech LEF: met1 minimum width 0.14 µm, minimum spacing 0.14 µm (the routing pitch is 0.34 µm, so a default track pair is 0.20 µm apart — looser than the manufacturing minimum). Thickness 0.35 µm. A teaching sidewall plate at the manufacturing minimum is

C′ = εox t / s = 3.9 ε0 × 0.35 / 0.14 = 86.3 fF/mm

For a 100 µm parallel run, Cc ≈ 8.63 fF. This ignores fringe, the real dielectric stack, and any grounded shape nearby; it is a plate, not a field solver. (At the default 0.34 µm pitch the same plate would be 60.4 fF/mm, because s = 0.20 µm.)

Lesson 29’s incremental delay for inv_1 is about 10 ps per inv_1 load. With Cin(A) = 2.302 fF,

Req ≈ 10 ps / 2.302 fF = 4.34 kΩ

Treating Cc as lumped at the driver (an optimistic place to put it for delay, a simple place for a hand sum), the extra delay from M = 1 to M = 2 is

Δt ≈ Req × Cc = 4.34 kΩ × 8.63 fF ≈ 38 ps

That is about half of the course FO4 (72.15 ps) for a short, minimum-spaced pair — large enough to take seriously, and also large enough that you should not treat 38 ps as a signoff number. A distributed 100 µm line has only 89 Ω of metal (R = 0.125 × 100/0.14), so the driver, not the wire, dominates this particular estimate. Longer parallels, weaker drivers, and two-sided aggressors (a victim between two wires) all make it worse. A grounded shield in between replaces Cc with a larger to-ground cap: delay becomes more predictable and usually a bit slower, and the glitch goes down.

Check your understanding — STA reports a 0 ps crosstalk delta on a net you know runs parallel to a busy bus for 200 µm. What might be going on?

The windows may not overlap: the aggressors are constrained not to switch when this victim does, so M = 2 is not in the analysis. The coupling may have been filtered as a small fraction of the total cap. The net may have been routed on a quieter layer than you remember. Or the tool is not running a crosstalk-aware delay at all. Before you relax, confirm which of those it is — “0 ps” is not the same as “no Cc.”

4. What you change in the netlist and in the rules

Physical mitigations, in the usual order of cost:

  • Spacing — Cc falls as s grows (the plate says 1/s; fringe falls more slowly). A non-default rule (NDR) can ask the router for extra gap on clock nets or on a sensitive bus.
  • Shielding — a grounded (or supply) wire between aggressor and victim. The NDR can require a shield. Cost is tracks; the benefit is a much smaller Cc and a quieter glitch budget.
  • Layer promotion — thicker, wider upper metals have different Cc and much lower R; a long victim is often happier one layer up even at the same spacing.
  • Slew and drive — a stronger victim driver reduces glitch height (it holds the quiet net more firmly) and reduces delta delay. A slower aggressor reduces both, at the cost of that aggressor’s own timing.
  • Timing windows — staggering when things switch, or tightening an aggressor’s window, can legally remove an M = 2 case. This is a constraint change, not a routing change, and it must remain true after ECO.

Clocks are the usual first clients of an NDR: a clock that is both a victim (it can glitch into a false edge) and an aggressor (it switches every cycle, so its window is wide) is worth a shield. Lesson 35’s tree is where that budget starts; this lesson is why the router is asked for the extra space.

Check your understanding — same-direction switching makes M = 0, which speeds the victim. Can you use that as a hold fix?

Only if the alignment is guaranteed. Hold cares about the fastest victim, which is exactly this case when it happens. Relying on a helpful aggressor as your hold margin is fragile: a different vector, a quieter neighbour after an ECO, or a slightly shifted window takes the gift away and can leave you short. STA should be allowed to assume the helpful case for hold (and the harmful case for setup) when the windows allow it; you should not take the speedup to the bank as a design technique.

Check your understanding — why is this not the same lesson as lesson 83?

Lesson 83 is written from the analog-victim side: return paths, substrate, and isolation of a quiet analog node. This lesson is the digital-STA side: Miller factors, timing windows, glitch into a CMOS threshold, and router NDRs on HD met1. The physics is one capacitance; the questions and the tools differ. A shield that satisfies STA may still be a poor return path for an analog block, and the reverse.

Key takeaways

  • Crosstalk is a delta-delay problem when both edges overlap, and a glitch problem when the victim was quiet. STA windows decide which Miller factor is possible (0, 1, or 2 in the ideal aligned case).
  • A teaching plate on HD met1 at minimum spacing gives 86.3 fF/mm. For 100 µm and this course’s incremental inv_1 resistance, M = 2 versus M = 1 is about 38 ps. That is a hand estimate, not an OpenRCX or STA result.
  • Mitigations: extra spacing, shields, upper layers, stronger victim drive, and honest timing windows, often as NDRs on clocks and sensitive buses.
  • Lesson 83 is the analog-victim companion; do not collapse the two.
