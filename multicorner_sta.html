Lesson 45 / 84 · Part V, Digital implementation

Multi-Corner STA: OCV, CRPR & Hold Cells

One 10 ns SKY130 design, timed at every LibreLane corner, with a real OpenSTA path at the slowest setup and the fastest hold, on-chip variation, clock reconvergence, and the delay cells that repaired hold.

Where the numbers come from. The nine-corner slack table, the annotated setup and hold paths, the clock latency and CRPR figures, and the post-route fmax are OpenSTA 2.7.0 reports from LibreLane 3.0.17 run RUN_2026-11-11_16-09-06 of the bundled 32-bit SPM multiplier, after RC extraction, on sky130_fd_sc_hd. The post-CTS three-corner and 10% OCV numbers are a separate OpenSTA run on the same design’s post-CTS netlist, without SPEF. Delay-cell times are Liberty lookups at 0.05 ns slew and 5 fF. Signal-integrity delta-delay was not reported in this run and is labelled where the discussion is conceptual. Recorded files live in labs/analysis_examples/multicorner_sta/.

What you will learn

  • Read a nine-corner SKY130 STA table and say why setup is worst at max-RC slow-slow and hold is worst at min-RC fast-fast.
  • Walk a real annotated OpenSTA path, including a hold-delay cell that costs setup slack.
  • Apply on-chip variation derates and clock-reconvergence pessimism (CRPR), and say what POCV would add.
  • Choose a HD delay cell for hold repair and estimate what it costs at ss versus ff.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

Why it matters
A chip that is clean at typical 25 °C 1.8 V can still fail in the cold, in the heat, or on a fast lot. Signoff is a matrix of corners, not a single number.

1. One corner is not a chip

A Liberty file is characterised at one process, voltage and temperature. The SKY130 HD library used in this course ships (among others) a typical file at 25 °C and 1.80 V (tt_025C_1v80), a slow file at 100 °C and 1.60 V (ss_100C_1v60), and a fast file at −40 °C and 1.95 V (ff_n40C_1v95). After routing, extraction adds three interconnect corners — min, nom and max RC — so LibreLane’s signoff view is a three-by-three matrix: nine corners.

That matrix is called multi-corner, multi-mode STA (MCMM) when you also have more than one functional mode (scan shift, a slow test clock, a gated-clock sleep mode). This lesson uses a single 10 ns functional mode and varies only the corners. The teaching pattern is stable across tools:

  • Setup is a maximum-delay check. It is almost always worst at slow cells and maximum interconnect (here, max_ss).
  • Hold is a minimum-delay check. It is almost always worst at fast cells and minimum interconnect (here, min_ff).

Before extraction, the min, nom and max RC views are identical — there is no SPEF yet, so LibreLane skips the duplicates. After RCX they differ, usually by tens of picoseconds on a small block and by much more on a long routed net.

2. Nine corners on a real SKY130 block

The design is LibreLane’s bundled 32-bit SPM multiplier, the same block whose clock tree was measured in lesson 35. The period is 10 ns; signoff clock uncertainty is 0.10 ns; the clock is propagated through the tree; parasitics come from OpenROAD RCX. Every corner met (worst negative slack is 0, total negative slack is 0). The number that changes is the worst slack — how much margin is left.

3. The worst setup path, annotated

The worst setup path at max_ss is not a flop-to-flop path. It starts at input port x (2.00 ns of external input delay) and ends at flop _290_, a sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfrtp_2. Arrival is 6.641 ns; required time is 10.232 ns; slack is 3.591 ns, MET. Two things on that path are worth sitting with.

First, three clkdlybuf4s25_1 cells sit on the data input — they are used here as delay/slew cells on an input cone, not as the clock tree. Together they contribute more than 2 ns at ss. Second, right in front of the capturing flop’s D pin is a hold-repair cell:

hold66/A  sky130_fd_sc_hd__dlygate4sd3_1
hold66/X  delay 1.187 ns    arrival 6.641 ns    →  _290_/D

That 1.187 ns is there because earlier in the flow, OpenROAD inserted 33 dlygate4sd3_1 cells to repair hold. Hold is now comfortable; this particular cell is paying for that comfort on the worst setup path. The rest of the required-time side is ordinary: propagated clock 0.623 ns to the capture flop, 0.100 ns of uncertainty, 0.290 ns of library setup, and — because this is an input path, not two flops sharing a tree — CRPR of 0.

4. The worst hold path, and the delay cells that fixed it

At min_ff the worst hold path is flop _265_ to flop _264_, both dfrtp_2. Data arrives at 0.585 ns; the required time is 0.332 ns (capture clock 0.273 ns, plus 0.100 ns uncertainty, minus 0.016 ns of CRPR, minus 0.025 ns of library hold). Slack is 0.253 ns, MET. Launch and capture share the root of the tree (clkbuf_0_clk, a clkbuf_16) and then split into different leaves — that shared root is what CRPR will credit, in section 6.

Hold was not always this comfortable. The same design, before place-and-route, at the ff Liberty corner with an ideal clock, had only 46 ps of hold slack. After clock-tree synthesis OpenROAD’s repair_timing inserted 33 sky130_fd_sc_hd__dlygate4sd3_1 cells on short data paths. Post-CTS OpenSTA (no SPEF) then reported 98 ps of ff hold slack; after routing and extraction, min-ff hold is 253 ps. Setup, over the same three stages, went the other way: pre-PNR ss 5.22 ns, post-CTS 3.97 ns, post-route max-ss 3.59 ns.

Two practical observations. The dlygate4sd3 that OpenROAD chose is the slowest of the gate-delay family: about 0.55 ns at tt and more than a nanosecond at ss — which is exactly the 1.187 ns we just saw on the worst setup path (ss, with a real load, is in that range). The clkdlybuf4s* family is intended for the clock network (balanced rise and fall, designed as a clock buffer); putting one on a data path, as the input cone of SPM does, still delays the signal, but it is a different cell for a different job. A plain buf_1 is a poor hold fixer: at ff, where hold is tight, it only adds about 70 ps, and at ss it still adds 171 ps that setup has to pay for.

Useful skew is the other hold (and setup) lever: deliberately lengthening the capture clock or shortening the launch clock. That moves slack from one check to the other without adding data delay. The 33 cells on this design were data-path delay cells, not a useful-skew campaign. Useful skew is worth considering when a hold path and a setup path share a flop and you would rather slide the clock than pile delay on the data.

5. On-chip variation, derates, and a measured 10% OCV

A Liberty corner is a global PVT. Two gates on the same die, at the same nominal ss, are not identical: one may sit a little slower, one a little faster. On-chip variation (OCV) is how STA accounts for that without a new characterisation. The simplest form is a timing derate: late paths are multiplied by a factor greater than one, early paths by a factor less than one.

set_timing_derate -late  1.10
set_timing_derate -early 0.90

For a setup check, data and the launch clock are late (slower — less slack), and the capture clock is early (faster — also less slack). For a hold check the assignments flip: data and launch are early, capture is late. OpenSTA applies those factors to cell (and, if asked, net) delays.

On this SPM, a post-CTS OpenSTA run without SPEF, at tt, reported setup 5.864 ns and hold 0.302 ns with no derate. The same run with a 10% derate (-early 0.90 -late 1.10) gave setup 5.749 ns and hold 0.260 ns — 115 ps and 42 ps of slack consumed by OCV, respectively. A 5% derate was also attempted; it produced slacks identical to the no-derate run, so it is not reported as a measured 5% result. The 10% figures are the ones that clearly applied.

Clock uncertainty is a coarser knob sitting in the SDC (set_clock_uncertainty). It is a single margin, in nanoseconds, subtracted from every setup required time and added to every hold required time. It is the usual home for PLL jitter, plus a little extra conservatism. It is not a substitute for a derate: uncertainty does not scale with path depth, and a long data path has more opportunity for on-chip variation than a short one. Signoff on this run used 0.10 ns of uncertainty; you can see it as a line of its own in both annotated paths.

6. CRPR: taking back shared pessimism

Look at that 10% derate on a flop-to-flop path. The launch clock is derated late and the capture clock is derated early — even the buffers those two clocks share, at the root of the tree. Those shared buffers cannot be both slow and fast at the same instant. Clock reconvergence pessimism removal (CRPR, also called CPPR) finds the common part of the two clock paths and credits the contradictory derate back.

The OpenSTA setup-skew report at nom_tt writes this explicitly:

Clock clk
  0.399 ns   source latency
− 0.356 ns   target latency
  0.100 ns   clock uncertainty
− 0.022 ns   CRPR
────────────
  0.121 ns   setup skew

That −0.022 ns is 22.4 ps of reconvergence credit. On the annotated min-ff hold path, the same idea appears as “clock reconvergence pessimism −0.0156 ns.” A larger tree with a longer shared trunk credits more; two clocks that never meet (a generated clock, a muxed clock, a hard macro pin) credit nothing.

Advanced OCV, POCV and LVF replace a single derate with a per-cell statistical width. Liberty Variation Format (LVF) tables give a delay distribution at each slew/load point; parametric OCV (POCV) walks that distribution through the path and combines stages as a root-sum-square rather than a stacked worst case. OpenSTA can consume LVF when the Liberty file has it. The public SKY130 HD Liberty used in this course is NLDM — no LVF tables — so this run did not do POCV. The idea is still the one you need for a denser node: a 10% flat derate on a 40-stage path is a lot of stacked pessimism, and a statistical model gives some of it back.

7. Signal integrity — what this run did not measure

A neighbour net that switches at the same moment as your victim adds (or subtracts) charge through the coupling capacitance. STA calls the result a delta-delay: setup uses the direction that makes the victim slower, hold the direction that makes it faster. The coupling is in the SPEF (CC capacitors); a SI-aware timer turns those into a worst-case delta given the aggressor’s switching window.

LibreLane’s STAPostPNR reports for this SPM run did not include an SI delta-delay. The numbers in Table 1 are therefore cell and interconnect delay without a crosstalk adjustment. On a small, sparse multiplier that may be a small omission; on a wide bus over a long metal-1 or metal-2 run it is not. If you need a number, turn SI on in the timer you sign off with and read the delta, or treat this paragraph as a checklist item, not a measurement.

8. A worked derate and CRPR calculation

This calculation is a self-contained example of the arithmetic, not a reconstruction of the SPM slack. Take a flop-to-flop path at a 10 ns period:

  • Data path (cell + net, underrated) 4.00 ns
  • Launch clock network 0.40 ns, capture clock network 0.35 ns, of which 0.30 ns is shared
  • Library setup 0.12 ns, clock uncertainty 0.10 ns
  • OCV late 1.10, early 0.90

Without CRPR, setup arrival is the late launch plus late data: 1.10 × (0.40 + 4.00) = 4.84 ns. Required time is the period, plus early capture, minus uncertainty, minus setup: 10.00 + 0.90 × 0.35 − 0.10 − 0.12 = 10.095 ns. Slack is 10.095 − 4.84 = 5.255 ns.

CRPR looks at the shared 0.30 ns, which was derated late on the launch side (+0.10 × 0.30 = +30 ps) and early on the capture side (−0.10 × 0.30 = −30 ps) — 60 ps of contradictory pessimism. Crediting all 60 ps raises slack to 5.315 ns. (OpenSTA’s reported CRPR is exactly this credit, with a sign convention that depends on whether it is folded into skew or listed on its own; match the report, don’t force the sign.)

For hold, swap late and early, drop the period, and use the library hold time. The same shared 0.30 ns is again the CRPR term. A short data path with a large derate is where CRPR matters most: without it you will insert delay cells you do not need, and you will pay for them at ss as section 3 showed.

9. A signoff checklist

  • Every Liberty PVT you claim, crossed with every extracted RC corner. On LibreLane sky130A that is the nine in Table 1. Do not sign off only at tt.
  • A propagated clock, not an ideal one, once the tree exists. Ideal-clock hold is optimistic; ideal-clock setup can be optimistic or pessimistic depending on the skew.
  • An OCV derate or a POCV/LVF model, plus CRPR. Record the factors. If a derate doesn’t move slack, treat it as unapplied, not as “OCV had no effect.”
  • Clock uncertainty that matches the PLL and the board, not a leftover default.
  • Recovery and removal on asynchronous resets (the same Liberty arcs already used in lesson 39), and a minimum pulse width.
  • Hold repair you can account for: which cell, how many, and what they cost at the slow corner. Prefer a delay cell with similar rise and fall, and consider useful skew before stacking dlygate4sd3 on a path that is already tight at ss.
  • SI if the tool and the SPEF support it; if not, say so.
  • The SDF you will hand to gate-level simulation (lesson 46) should come from the same corner you care about — typically max-ss for setup-style delay, min-ff when you are hunting hold races.
Check your understanding — after a clean pre-PNR STA, post-CTS hold is violated at ff and setup slack has shrunk at ss. What happened, and what is the first thing to inspect?

The clock is no longer ideal: real insertion delay and skew have appeared, and short data paths that used to have leftover hold margin no longer do. Inspect the hold paths and the cells repair_timing wants to insert. Then look at the worst ss setup path — every delay cell you accept will show up there, at roughly the ss column of Table 2.

Check your understanding — a 10% late/early derate is applied, but the two flops share 200 ps of clock tree. Roughly how much setup slack does CRPR give back?

The shared 200 ps was derated 10% late on launch (+20 ps) and 10% early on capture (−20 ps), a contradictory 40 ps. CRPR credits that 40 ps, raising setup slack by about 0.040 ns. The exact credit is in the OpenSTA skew report’s CRPR line.

Check your understanding — you need about 400 ps more hold slack at ff, and the same path has 800 ps of setup slack at ss. Which HD cell in Table 2 is a reasonable first choice, and why might dlygate4sd3_1 be the wrong one?

dlygate4sd3_1 adds about 0.38 ns at ff — close to the 400 ps you need — but more than a nanosecond at ss, which would consume the 800 ps of setup slack and then some. A dlygate4sd1_1 (0.14 ns ff, 0.39–0.49 ns ss) or a dlymetal6s4s_1 is a smaller, safer first step; two smaller cells, or a useful-skew move, may be better than one large one.
