Analog Bias, gm & Headroom

What digital switching models leave out, and what an analog operating point must establish.

What you will learn
  • Identify MOS operating regions from terminal voltages rather than logic labels.
  • Estimate gain from gm, output resistance and voltage headroom.
  • Explain mirror errors, startup risk and the information needed at an analog/digital boundary.

Why it matters
The operating point sets the gain, speed and noise of every analog block.

1. A MOSFET has an operating point, not only an ON/OFF state

The same transistor can act as a digital switch, a voltage-controlled current source, or a nonlinear resistor. A logic designer chooses input levels that restore a reliable 0 or 1. An analog designer chooses a DC operating point around which a small signal can move without clipping or losing gain. Even a digital inverter passes through analog operating regions during every transition.

For an NMOS with source at the lower potential, define VOV = VGS − VTH, the gate overdrive. The familiar region boundaries below are strong-inversion, long-channel approximations. For a PMOS, use the positive magnitudes VSG, VSD and |VTH|. Threshold itself changes with body bias, process and temperature. MIT's device-model notes derive the incremental model.

Operating regionApproximate NMOS conditionUseful intuitionNonideality to remember
Weak inversion / nominally offVGS below thresholdVery small, strongly bias-dependent currentSubthreshold current and junction leakage remain; this region is also useful for low-current analog design.
Triode / linearVOV > 0; 0 ≤ VDS < VOVVoltage-controlled resistance at small VDSResistance depends on signal voltage; a sampling switch can distort.
SaturationVOV > 0; VDS ≥ VOVDrain current mainly controlled by gate voltageCurrent still depends on drain voltage; output resistance is finite.

“Saturation” in a MOS amplifier does not mean that its output is at a supply rail. A saturated device is normally what provides useful voltage gain. An amplifier whose output has clipped at a rail may instead have a device in triode or cutoff.

2. Choose bias before calculating gain

A common-source stage needs the desired drain current, a valid input bias, an output bias that leaves room in both directions, and suitable well/body connections. A current-source load usually gives more gain than a small resistor, but consumes voltage headroom. Increasing device length often improves output resistance while adding area and capacitance; the actual tradeoff must come from the selected models.

For the teaching stage below, assume a 1.8 V supply, 20 µA branch current, VOV,N = VOV,P = 0.20 V and an output near 0.9 V. A threshold assumption of 0.45 V would suggest VGS,N ≈ 0.65 V. These are design assumptions for arithmetic, not extracted SKY130 threshold or bias values. Device operating-point results decide whether a candidate implementation achieves them. See MIT's common-source amplifier notes.

3. Worked example: gm, ro and gain

Transconductance measures how much current changes for a small gate-voltage change: gm = ∂ID/∂VGS, with other terminal biases held fixed. Output conductance is gds = ∂ID/∂VDS; ro = 1/gds. These are derivatives at the bias point, not resistances measured from V/I.

With the first-order strong-inversion model ID ≈ ½βVOV²(1 + λVDS), useful approximations are gm ≈ 2ID/VOV and ro ≈ 1/(λID). The latter neglects the factor (1 + λVDS) in the exact derivative of this simple model. Assume λ = 0.10 V⁻¹ for each transistor:

gm,N ≈ 2 × 20 µA / 0.20 V = 0.20 mS
ro,N ≈ ro,P ≈ 1 / (0.10 V⁻¹ × 20 µA) = 500 kΩ
Av ≈ −gm,N(ro,N ∥ ro,P) = −50 V/V ≈ 34 dB magnitude

The negative sign means inversion. A small +1 mV input change predicts about −50 mV at the output while the linear approximation holds. A 100 mV change would predict −5 V, which the 1.8 V circuit cannot deliver. That contradiction identifies the limit of small-signal reasoning. This example also neglects body transconductance, additional loading and frequency-dependent capacitances.

Solved checkpoint — Does doubling current double the gain?

Keep VOV and λ unchanged by also changing device width appropriately, and raise each branch current to 40 µA. Then gm ≈ 0.40 mS and each ro ≈ 250 kΩ. Their parallel combination is 125 kΩ, so the estimated gain is still −50 V/V. Bandwidth, noise, capacitance and power change. If geometry is held fixed instead, overdrive changes and this comparison no longer applies.

4. Current mirrors need compliance and matching

A diode-connected reference device ties its drain to its gate. A second device shares that gate voltage. In an ideal matched pair with both devices saturated, equal source/body potentials and equal effective channel conditions, current scales approximately as the W/L ratio. The output must have sufficient compliance voltage to keep the output device in the intended region. MIT's current-source notes discuss mirrors and their limitations.

Worked error estimate: for equal geometry, assume λ = 0.10 V⁻¹, reference VDS = 0.65 V and output VDS = 0.95 V. The simple channel-length-modulation model gives IOUT/IREF ≈ (1 + 0.10 × 0.95)/(1 + 0.10 × 0.65) = 1.0282: about +2.8% error before mismatch. At 0.20 V overdrive, a small +10 mV threshold offset in the output device gives a first-order current error near −2ΔVTH/VOV = −10%, with other effects held fixed. This assumed offset is not a SKY130 mismatch specification.

Longer devices, cascoding and feedback can reduce some errors, with costs in headroom, bandwidth and complexity. Common-centroid placement can help cancel certain spatial gradients; it cannot guarantee equal devices or remove random mismatch. Read Noise, Mismatch & Variation before assigning a yield number.

5. A valid bias point is not a startup proof

A self-biased reference may have more than one equilibrium, including an unwanted near-zero-current state. A DC operating-point solver can find the desired state even when the physical circuit will not reliably start there. A startup circuit must push the core out of the unwanted state, then stop disturbing the final bias.

Check supply ramps, enable sequencing, power interruption, temperature, process, leakage, external loading and relevant mismatch. Use realistic initial conditions and supply impedance. An initial-condition command that forces a reference node to its final voltage can hide the very failure being investigated. A simple externally forced mirror, such as Figure 3, and a self-biased reference have different startup obligations.

6. Put this into a SKY130 workflow

The open SKY130 PDK includes distinct NMOS/PMOS and threshold-voltage options. Choose devices by their model names and documented operating ranges; nominal “1.8 V” is not permission to put an arbitrary transient or differential voltage across any terminal. Model validity limits also do not, by themselves, define product reliability limits. Use the official device details and the actual installed model revision.

QuestionDigital designer should provideAnalog designer should provide
Can this block be enabled safely?Reset/enable sequence, possible glitches and clock stateAllowed ramp/sequence and reference startup/ready behavior
What loads the output?Fanout, sampling events, pin capacitance and routing estimateSettling/error limit, output compliance and allowed load range
When is data valid?Sampling edge and synchronization schemeWorst-case settling/ready timing and invalid intervals
Does layout preserve the design?Switching activity, nearby clocks, power and return pathsMatching groups, well ties, guard strategy and sensitive nodes

A practical review reads the DC terminal voltages, ID, gm, gds and capacitances from SPICE, then repeats the relevant DC, AC, noise and transient analyses over conditions. Extracted parasitics are needed before concluding that the physical implementation behaves like the schematic. BSIM (Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model) operating-point definitions and simulator syntax vary: use the simulator's documented device-instance probes.

Check your understanding — a transistor has gm = 1 mS and ro = 50 kΩ. What is its intrinsic gain?

gm × ro = 1 mS × 50 kΩ = 50, about 34 dB. Any load resistance in parallel with ro lowers it.

In the open SKY130 flow. Draw the circuit in xschem, which writes a SPICE netlist that includes the SKY130 model library. Run it in ngspice with .op for the operating point, .dc for sweeps and .ac for small-signal response, and repeat at each process corner by changing the library section. The same steps in a commercial flow use Virtuoso and Spectre.

Key takeaways

  • Bias establishes headroom and the small-signal parameters on which gain, noise and speed depend.
  • Triode resistance and saturated current are both voltage dependent; neither is an ideal element.
  • Current mirrors require adequate drain voltage and matching conditions, and self-biased references need startup analysis.
  • Use square-law equations to reason about direction and scale; use validated PDK models and extracted circuits to establish performance.
  • A digital “ready” signal must represent a defined analog condition and cross clock domains safely.
