Lesson 55 / 84 · Part VI, Physical cells and the floorplan

Floorplan

A floorplan decides the size and shape of the core, where large blocks and pins go, and how power reaches every row — before a single standard cell is placed. Caravel’s 2920 × 3520 µm user area is the worked example.

What you will learn

  • Size a core from cell area, a utilization target and an aspect ratio, in whole SKY130 sites and rows.
  • Place macros with halos and routing channels, and plan logic around fixed boundary pins.
  • Plan the power structure and the physical cells — taps, endcaps, fill — that every row needs.
  • Connect a dropped-in SKY130 macro to the power grid, through met5 straps onto its own ring or through a ring you draw, and size that ring for IR drop, electromigration and via count.
  • Keep an analog block on its own supply ring, away from the digital grid.

Why it matters

1. The floorplan is the first physical decision

Before a single standard cell is placed, the floorplan fixes the shape the rest of the flow works inside: the block outline and its pins, the core where rows of cells will go, the positions of large blocks, and the power structure that feeds every row. Placement, clock-tree synthesis and routing (lesson 35) all assume it. Changing a floorplan late usually means redoing everything after it.

On a Caravel shuttle the outer boundary is already decided. A user design is the contents of user_project_wrapper, whose DEF gives a die area of 2920 × 3520 µm (lesson 5). The floorplan question is how to use that rectangle.

2. The core is a whole number of sites and rows

Standard cells land only on placement sites (lesson 13). In the SKY130 HD library a site is 0.46 µm wide and a row is 2.72 µm tall, so a complete rectangular array of rows is a whole number of sites wide and a whole number of rows tall. A core may also include margins, cut rows and blockages; what must be legal is every row and every cell origin.

A small example: 100 sites by 20 rows is 100 × 0.46 = 46.0 µm by 20 × 2.72 = 54.4 µm. That rectangle is a core, the region the rows occupy — not a die size.

In the open SKY130 flow. OpenROAD’s initialize_floorplan sets the die and core areas and creates the rows, and place_pins puts the block’s pins on its boundary. LibreLane wraps them in its Floorplan and IOPlacement steps and sizes the core from a utilization target unless you give explicit dimensions.

initialize_floorplan -die_area "0 0 300 300" -core_area "10 10 290 290" -site unithd
place_pins -hor_layers met3 -ver_layers met2

3. Size the core from cell area and utilization

Synthesis reports the total area of the cells in the netlist. Utilization is the fraction of the core those cells occupy; the rest is left for routing access, clock buffers, timing repair, taps, fill and later changes. The core area is therefore the cell area divided by the target utilization, and its shape follows from a chosen aspect ratio (height divided by width).

core area = total cell area / utilization · width = √(core area / aspect ratio)

Worked example (assumed design). 40,000 cells averaging 6 µm² occupy 0.24 mm². At 50% utilization the core needs 0.48 mm²: a square of 692.8 µm. Rounding up to whole sites and rows gives 1,507 sites (693.2 µm) by 255 rows (693.6 µm). That fits easily inside the 2920 × 3520 µm wrapper, leaving room for margins, rings and macros. The 6 µm² average and the 50% target are assumptions; real targets depend on the design’s routing density and on the flow.

4. The core sits inside a boundary with fixed pins

At chip level the core sits inside the pad ring, which uses its own, much coarser grid (lesson 64). Inside a Caravel wrapper the boundary is a block edge instead, and its pins are fixed by the harness. The wrapper DEF lists 637 signal pins and 8 supply pins: three 128-bit logic-analyzer buses (la_data_in, la_data_out, la_oenb), three 38-bit GPIO buses (io_in, io_out, io_oeb), a 32-bit Wishbone bus with its control signals, 29 analog_io pins, three interrupt lines and two clocks.

The wrapper’s perimeter is 2 × (2920 + 3520) = 12,880 µm, so the signal pins average about 20 µm apart. Logic that talks to a group of pins belongs near that group; a floorplan that ignores fixed pins forces long routes across the whole block.

5. Macros, halos and routing channels

A macro — a memory, an analog block, a pre-built processor — is placed before the standard cells. The usual choice is near an edge of the core with its pins facing inward: a macro in the middle splits the rows and forces routes around it on every side. A halo (keep-out ring) around the macro keeps standard cells from crowding its pins, and a channel between two macros must carry every signal that passes between them.

Worked example. Suppose 100 signals must pass vertically through a channel, using met2 (0.46 µm pitch) and met4 (0.92 µm pitch) from the HD tech LEF. Together they offer 1/0.46 + 1/0.92 ≈ 3.26 tracks per micrometer, so the channel must be at least 100 / 3.26 ≈ 31 µm wide — before power straps, spacing to the macros and any track the router cannot use.

6. Power: rings, straps and rails

Every row carries met1 supply rails along its edges; in the open_pdks LibreLane configuration for the HD library the rail width is 0.48 µm. The rails alone are far too resistive to feed a large core, so the floorplan adds straps on upper metals that cross the rows and connect them to rings around the core, which connect to the supply pins (lesson 14). Caravel’s empty wrapper already contains power rings for its supply domains; place and route adds the strap grid and joins it to the rows. Strap width and spacing trade voltage drop (lesson 68) against the routing tracks they occupy. A macro dropped into the core needs its own connection to this grid, often through a ring; section 7 shows how.

7. A power ring for a dropped-in macro

A hard macro is a block that arrives already laid out: an SRAM from OpenRAM, an analog block such as an ADC, or bought IP. The floorplan sees only its LEF abstract: a boundary, its pins and its obstructions (OBS), the areas on each metal layer that the macro’s own wiring already uses. Supply has to reach the macro’s power pins without crossing that wiring and without breaking the core grid around it. There are two ways to do that. In the first, straps on a metal the macro does not use pass over it and drop vias onto power shapes the macro already has; LibreLane’s integration guide calls this the hierarchical method and makes it the default. In the second, a power ring is drawn around the macro and the core straps end on it; the guide calls this the ring method, and it costs area but leaves the macro free to use every metal layer.

What the macro brings: the SKY130 SRAM’s abstract

The OpenRAM macro sky130_sram_1kbyte_1rw1r_32x256_8 (1 KB, one read-write port and one read port, 32-bit words) is a good real example. Its LEF already contains a two-loop ring: ground outside, power inside. Each loop has met4 vertical sides and met3 horizontal sides. Inside the rings, met1 to met4 are almost entirely obstruction, and there is no met5 at all.

Joining the grid at the macro edge

Figure 4 puts the SRAM into the default HD grid at its real size and pitches. Core met4 straps run vertically every 153.6 µm, met5 straps horizontally every 153.18 µm. Around the macro is a halo, 10 µm by default in LibreLane on every side, where nothing else is placed. The rows are cut at the halo and an endcap closes each row fragment, so the met1 rails stop there too. Any met4 strap that would run into the macro stops at the halo, because met4 inside is obstruction and belongs to the macro’s own wiring. The met5 straps keep going straight across: the macro has no met5. Wherever a met5 power strap crosses a met4 side of the vccd1 ring, pdngen drops a via4, and the ground strap does the same on the vssd1 ring.

Figure 5 cuts through the same edge along one met5 power strap. In the core, a via4 joins the strap to each met4 strap of the same net, and a via stack (via3, via2, via, mcon) joins the met4 strap to the met1 rail. At the macro the strap simply continues; its only connection is the via4 onto the ring, 4.76–6.5 µm in from the macro edge.

How many via4 cuts fit at one crossing? The met5 strap is 1.6 µm wide and the ring side 1.74 µm. A via4 cut is 0.8 µm square, cuts are 0.8 µm apart, and met5 must enclose a cut by 0.31 µm, so one cut needs 0.8 + 2 × 0.31 = 1.42 µm of met5 width and two side by side need 3.02 µm. One cut per crossing is all that fits. That one cut is the narrowest point of the whole path, so it is the first thing to check.

Check your understanding — why do the core met4 straps stop at the SRAM, while the met5 straps cross it?

Inside the macro, met4 is 96.9% obstruction: the macro’s own wiring is there, and a core met4 strap would short to it or break spacing rules. met5 is empty inside the macro, so a met5 strap can cross freely, and wherever it passes over a met4 ring side a via4 connects them. That is the hierarchical method: each level down gives up the top metal of the level above.

Sizing a ring you draw yourself

With the ring method, or for a block whose pins are scattered round its edge, you draw the ring. Its width and how often straps feed it set both the drop and the current density. Treat the ring as a loop of perimeter P drawing the block current I evenly along its length, fed at N evenly spaced points. Between two feeds a distance s = P / N apart, the current enters at both ends and the worst point is midway:

ΔVmax = (I / P) · (Rs / W) · s² / 8 · peak ring current = I / (2N) · current per feed = I / N

The drop grows with the square of the spacing but only inversely with the width. Doubling the feeds cuts the drop by four; doubling the width cuts it by two and costs routing space all the way round the block.

Worked example 2 — a ring for an illustrative block (met4 sheet resistance and EM from the HD LEF; block size and current assumed). The block is 400 × 300 µm and draws 20 mA. A ring 6 µm outside it has P = 2 × (412 + 312) = 1448 µm, so the load is 20 mA / 1448 µm = 13.8 µA per µm.

A single feed would need a ring six times wider to meet a 1% budget. Ten feeds, one every 145 µm (roughly one per core strap), bring the drop to about 1 mV on the minimum default width. The per-feed column matters too: if the feed is a met5 strap landing on a met4 ring, it goes through a via4, and one cut carries only 2.49 mA. Ten feeds at 2 mA fit within one cut each; four feeds at 5 mA would each need more cuts than a 1.6 µm crossing can hold.

Check your understanding — the 20 mA block keeps its 1.6 µm met4 ring. How many feeds meet an 18 mV budget, and does that answer survive the vias?

N = 2 gives 106 / 4 = 26.6 mV, too much; N = 3 gives 106 / 9 = 11.8 mV, which meets it. But each of the three feeds then carries 6.7 mA. Through met5 onto the met4 ring, that is 2.7 times one via4 cut’s 2.49 mA, and a 1.6 × 1.6 µm crossing holds only one cut. Either widen the crossing (with a 3.2 µm strap and ring, 2 × 2 cuts fit, 10 mA) or use at least nine feeds (2.2 mA each). The via, not the drop, sets the answer.

A separate ring for an analog block

An analog block such as an ADC is dropped in the same way but powered differently. In Caravel the user area has two kinds of supply: vccd1/vssd1, the 1.8 V digital pair, and vdda1/vssa1, the 3.3 V analog pair, with a second set (vccd2, vssd2, vdda2, vssa2) beside them. The LibreLane guide says to use the first pair for digital macros and the second for analog ones. An analog block therefore gets its own ring on its own nets, fed by its own straps straight from the wrapper’s analog rings, never by tapping the digital grid. Digital switching current then never flows through the analog supply wiring and cannot shift the block’s reference or bias (lesson 83).

Three things keep the two supplies apart on the floorplan. A routing blockage around the block keeps digital straps, rails and cells out, so digital wiring does not cross the block or its ring. The block’s ring and feeds are on the analog nets only. Any guard ring inside the block is a substrate or well tie, not a supply ring, and it is part of the block’s own layout (lesson 48). Where the analog and digital grounds finally meet, and what lies between them, is a chip-level decision made in the harness and package, not in the block floorplan (lesson 69). Signals that cross between the domains need level shifting (lesson 54), and the whole arrangement is checked at bring-up (lesson 84).

Check your understanding — why not connect the ADC with PDN_MACRO_CONNECTIONS “adc0 vccd1 vssd1 VDDA VSSA” and save a ring?

Two reasons. In Caravel vdda1 is the 3.3 V analog supply and vccd1 is 1.8 V, so the block would get the wrong voltage. Even at the right voltage, the block would share the digital grid’s resistance, so every digital current spike would appear on its supply and ground. The hook should name the analog nets (“adc0 vdda1 vssa1 VDDA VSSA”), and the block needs its own feed from the analog rings.

In the open SKY130 flow. LibreLane’s default PDN script already contains a macro grid. It cuts rails and straps around each macro by the halo, then joins the macro’s power pins to the core grid on the two strap layers:

define_pdn_grid -macro -default -name macro -starts_with POWER \
    -halo "$::env(PDN_HORIZONTAL_HALO) $::env(PDN_VERTICAL_HALO)"
add_pdn_connect -grid macro -layers "$::env(PDN_VERTICAL_LAYER) $::env(PDN_HORIZONTAL_LAYER)"

In the design configuration, name each macro’s supply connection explicitly, and keep the placement and PDN halos equal:

"PDN_MACRO_CONNECTIONS": ["sram0 vccd1 vssd1 vccd1 vssd1", "adc0 vdda1 vssa1 VDDA VSSA"],
"VDD_NETS": ["vccd1", "vdda1"], "GND_NETS": ["vssd1", "vssa1"],
"PDN_HORIZONTAL_HALO": 10, "PDN_VERTICAL_HALO": 10,
"FP_MACRO_HORIZONTAL_HALO": 10, "FP_MACRO_VERTICAL_HALO": 10

The format is <instance regex> <vdd net> <gnd net> <vdd pin> <gnd pin>; older configurations call it FP_PDN_MACRO_HOOKS. To harden your own digital macro for the hierarchical method, set PDN_MULTILAYER to false so its grid stays on met4; for the ring method, set PDN_CORE_RING to true instead. A block ring on its own nets, as in Figure 7, needs a custom script (PDN_CFG). The lines below are a sketch, not a tested script; check them against your OpenROAD pdn documentation:

# sketch: a met4/met5 ring around one analog instance
add_global_connection -net vdda1 -inst_pattern adc0 -pin_pattern VDDA -power
add_global_connection -net vssa1 -inst_pattern adc0 -pin_pattern VSSA -ground
set_voltage_domain -name CORE -power vccd1 -ground vssd1 -secondary_power "vdda1 vssa1"
define_pdn_grid -macro -instances adc0 -name adc_ring -halo "10 10"
add_pdn_ring -grid adc_ring -layers "met4 met5" -widths "3.2 3.2" \
    -spacings "1.7 1.7" -core_offsets "4 4 4 4" -nets "vdda1 vssa1"
add_pdn_connect -grid adc_ring -layers "met4 met5"

OpenROAD extends straps inside the area a ring encloses until they reach it, and cuts the standard-cell rows around placed macros.

8. Taps, endcaps and the other physical cells

The same configuration names the cells every row needs: tapvpwrvgnd_1 as the well tap, inserted along each row at intervals no larger than the foundry’s tap-spacing rule (lesson 48), and decap_3 as the endcap at both ends of every row and row fragment (lesson 49). After routing, fill and decap cells close the remaining gaps (lesson 47). Special cells constrain the floorplan too: the HD level shifter is two rows tall, so it needs a legal double-row slot (lesson 54).

9. Reserve area for the real implementation

In the 100 × 20 example there are 2,000 site slots. If logic occupies 1,200 of them, gross utilization is 60%. Suppose taps, endcaps and blockages take another 200: only 600 slots remain, before clock buffers, timing repair, decaps and any later change. The accounting is illustrative, not a recommended utilization for every design.

Check your understanding — is an empty-looking region free routing space?

Not necessarily. It may contain a macro obstruction, power strap, keepout, guard ring, sensitive analog net or reserved channel. Check the physical abstract and full verification context before using it.

Check your understanding — a design’s cells total 0.30 mm² and the target utilization is 60%. Does a 600 × 600 µm core suffice?

No. The core needs 0.30 / 0.60 = 0.50 mm², but 600 × 600 µm is only 0.36 mm². A square core needs about 707 µm per side: 1,538 sites (707.5 µm) by 260 rows (707.2 µm).

Check your understanding — why place a memory macro against the core edge with its pins facing inward?

In the middle it would split every row it crosses and force routes around it on all sides. At the edge it blocks less routing and leaves the rows continuous, its pins face the logic that uses them, and its halo keeps cells from crowding those pins.

Key takeaways

  • A floorplan fixes the core, the rows, macro positions, pins and the power structure before placement.
  • Size the core as cell area ÷ target utilization, then round to whole 0.46 µm sites and 2.72 µm rows.
  • Caravel’s wrapper is a fixed 2920 × 3520 µm boundary with 637 signal pins and 8 supply pins in fixed positions.
  • Put macros near edges with halos; size channels from the tracks the passing signals need.
  • Every row needs taps (tapvpwrvgnd_1), endcaps (decap_3) and later fill; supply reaches the rows through rings, straps and met1 rails.
  • A dropped-in macro is fed either by met5 straps that cross it and drop one via4 per crossing onto its met4 ring (the SRAM: about 9 mV) or by a ring around it; rows, rails and met4 straps stop at its 10 µm halo.
  • A ring’s drop grows with the square of its feed spacing, so add feeds before width, and check the via4 cut at every feed (2.49 mA each).
  • An analog block gets its own ring on its own nets (vdda1/vssa1 in Caravel), its own feeds and a blockage that keeps the digital grid out.
