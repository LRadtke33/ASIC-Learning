Lesson 84 / 84 · Part IX, Mixed-signal

Mixed-Signal Verification & Bring-Up

Turn analog requirements, digital behavior, and physical effects into reviewable tests.

What you will learn
  • Choose the analysis that can establish a requirement.
  • Write an interface contract and a numerical settling test.
  • Build a reproducible verification matrix across circuits, logic, and layout.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Runs alongside the whole flow, from the plan in step 1, specification to the final checks before step 10, tapeout.
Tools
Verilator or Icarus Verilog with behavioral models, cocotb testbenches, and ngspice for transistor-level checks. Commercial: Xcelium with Spectre, or VCS with PrimeSim.
Why it matters
Many first-silicon failures in mixed-signal chips sit at the boundary between analog and digital.

1. A passing test proves only what it observed

A digital functional simulation can correctly implement every register and still drive an analog block before its reference is ready. A transistor simulation can show excellent gain while using an impossible ideal supply. DRC can pass while a circuit has the wrong connections; LVS can pass while extracted wiring destroys settling time. The question is always: which requirement does this result establish, under which assumptions?

CheckWhat it establishes within its modelWhat it does not establish alone
RTL / assertions / formal propertiesSpecified states, transactions, and logical propertiesAnalog settling, transistor noise, physical metastability probability
STA with Liberty, constraints, parasiticsTiming against specified arcs and path constraintsReference accuracy, analog startup, protocol correctness
DC / operating pointBias solution and headroom at a conditionWhether startup reaches that solution
AC / loop analysisLinearized response around a bias solutionLarge-signal slewing, startup, saturation recovery

See the SKY130 file-type map, OpenSTA's inputs, and the ngspice analysis descriptions. The table is an engineering interpretation of each check's scope, not a claim that one tool covers all of them.

2. Write the interface contract before the testbench

Consider a teaching subsystem: digital trim registers control a nominal 1.2 V analog reference powered from a 1.8 V domain, and a 12-bit converter uses that reference. These nominal voltages are design assumptions, not a provided SKY130 macro. The analog team owns valid bias/range and settling behavior. The digital team owns legal updates, sequencing, and result interpretation. Both own the boundary.

Contract itemConcrete question
Power statesCan a control be high while either supply is off? Which cells provide isolation or level conversion?
Trim updateDo bits change atomically? Is there a shadow register plus a commit event?
Ready signalWhat analog accuracy makes ready true, and how is it synchronized?
SamplingHow long after a trim/channel change must acquisition wait?
Result validityWhich sample does the word represent, and which samples must be discarded?
Failure behaviorWhat happens on brownout, lost clock, out-of-range input, or calibration timeout?

A multi-bit control word should not be “synchronized” by placing an unrelated two-flop chain on every bit. Use a coherent transfer mechanism, such as a held word with a correctly designed handshake, and implement the analog update at a safe boundary. See lesson 39. A comparator-generated ready flag needs both an electrical specification and a digital crossing strategy.

3. Give the test a numerical pass/fail condition

For a 1.2 V full-scale, ideal 12-bit converter, 1 LSB = 1.2/4096 = 292.97 µV and half an LSB is 146.48 µV. Suppose the reference requirement allocates 80 µV to residual settling error after a worst-case trim change. Measure the error relative to the intended final value over the whole specified observation window; a single lucky crossing does not mean settled.

For an illustrative one-pole response with τ=100 ns and a 20 mV change, |e(t)| = 20 mV·e−t/100 ns. The 80 µV target requires t > 100 ns·ln(20 mV/80 µV) = 552.15 ns. Waiting 600 ns gives 49.6 µV in this model. A real waveform may ring or slew; use extracted transient behavior and the actual stimulus.

The remaining 66.48 µV of the half-LSB number is not automatically an RMS-noise allowance. Deterministic worst-case errors, random noise, gain error, and reference variation combine according to different system specifications. Define the allowed RMS noise or error probability separately. For the ideal ratio model code ≈ 2NVin/Vref, a small reference error also produces input-dependent gain error.

4. Test conditions that cross team boundaries

Cold / warm startupSupply ramps, initial-state variations, valid PVT rangeCorrect operating point reached; bounded startup time

Largest trim transitionUpdate many bits together through the actual protocolGlitch, recovery, stale or prematurely valid samples

Statistical variationSupported mismatch/process model with recorded seedsOffset/range distributions, calibration coverage, uncertainty

Process corners and statistical samples are not interchangeable. Neither is a substitute for missing parasitics. Confirm which mechanisms your installed SKY130 deck and extractor include. OpenRCX describes a LEF/DEF interconnect extractor; it is not a transistor-level substrate or package field solver. A tool can be working as documented while omitting the effect your test needs.

5. Build a ladder of models

Use fast behavioral models for large sequences, protocols, and rare state combinations. Give those models the required delays, saturation/range limits, ready behavior, power states, and error flags. Correlate them with transistor-level results at a small set of meaningful operating points. A real-valued signal alone does not provide electrical loading, current conservation, or a noise spectrum unless the model explicitly supplies that behavior.

Use transistor-level benches to characterize bias, stability, settling, noise, and startup; use extracted views to quantify the layout's effect. Preserve the same measurements across schematic and extracted runs. Probe names can change when extraction flattens hierarchy or splits nets: verify the correspondence and electrical location of each saved node. A familiar net name is not proof that the probe still measures the intended point.

The analog and digital clocks in a co-simulation also need a clear time relationship. Define when a continuous voltage becomes a logic level and what happens in the threshold region. An ideal comparator model that flips instantly with no hysteresis, delay, or unknown-state policy may hide a real boundary failure.

In the open SKY130 flow. A typical open-source ladder: behavioral Verilog models of the analog blocks, simulated with Verilator or Icarus Verilog and driven by cocotb testbenches; transistor-level ngspice runs of each analog block; and a few slow, combined checks of the critical interfaces. Commercial flows run both worlds in one mixed-signal simulator, such as Cadence Xcelium with Spectre or Synopsys VCS with PrimeSim.

6. Make a result reproducible

Record the design/netlist revision, device-library revision, simulator and relevant options, model corner, supplies, temperature, extracted view and RC corner, package model, source/load impedances, activity scenario, measurement expression, observation window, and random seeds. Save units with results. Reporting “settling = 0.6” without a unit, target band, and stimulus is not a useful verification result.

SKY130 example: this course pins standard-cell facts to the HD source revision identified in the review notes. A design project must additionally record the installed primitive-model and extraction-deck revisions. A cell name such as inv_1 does not uniquely identify all those views.

Check your understanding: DRC, LVS, and TT transient all pass. What can still fail?

Examples include hold timing at another corner, local mismatch offset, supply sequencing, a reference glitch during scan activity, package-induced droop, an unsafe CDC handshake, or a load not present in the testbench. The next test should follow an uncovered requirement or missing physical mechanism, not simply repeat TT with more plot points.

Check your understanding — a test shows an ADC output that “looks right” on a waveform plot. What turns it into a verification check?

A numerical pass/fail condition over a stated interval: which signal, what tolerance, measured when, under which corner, temperature and supply, recorded so it can be reproduced. A plot that looks right proves only what someone happened to notice.

Check the supply plan too. Before tape-out, confirm that each analog macro’s PDN_MACRO_CONNECTIONS entry names the analog nets (vdda1/vssa1 in Caravel), not the digital ones, and that LVS finds its ring on those nets (lesson 50, section 7).

Digital test practice — wafer sort, shmoos, Caravel SPI and Tiny Tapeout’s microcotb — is lesson 71. This lesson stays with mixed-signal contracts.

Key takeaways

  • Define the analog/digital interface as voltages, currents, time windows, and states.
  • Use explicit numerical pass/fail measurements over a specified interval.
  • DRC, LVS, STA, simulation, and extraction establish different things.
  • Correlate fast behavioral models with transistor and extracted results.
  • Keep revisions, units, conditions, and statistical seeds with each result.
