Reliability & Aging: HCI, BTI, TDDB & Self-Heating

A chip that closes timing on day one can still slow, leak, or puncture over years. The usual aging mechanisms have different drivers — field, current, heat — and different knobs.

Where this sits. Lesson 30 is metal lifetime (EM). Lesson 48 is latch-up. This lesson is device aging: hot-carrier injection (HCI), bias-temperature instability (BTI), time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB), and self-heating. The ΔVth curve and the table are teaching summaries of well-known mechanisms. This course has not run an aging model, has not extracted a SKY130 lifetime parameter, and does not quote a years-to-fail number for a SKY130 device.

What you will learn

  • Name HCI, BTI, TDDB and self-heating, and say what each does to a MOSFET.
  • Read a qualitative ΔVth versus time sketch, including recovery.
  • Match each mechanism to a driver and a mitigation, without treating the table as a foundry spec.

1. Four mechanisms, four drivers

SKY130’s core MOSFET is a 1.8 V bulk device with a foundry-allowed use range; I/O devices are thicker-oxide and sit at a higher voltage. That split exists in part because oxide field and hot carriers do not scale politely. This lesson does not replace the PDK’s recommended operating conditions.

Check your understanding — a PMOS bias transistor has been on, at 1.80 V, for a year. You then start the block. Is FO4 still the day-one number?

Not necessarily. NBTI can have shifted that PMOS’s Vth during the long “on” interval. Some of the shift may recover after the bias changes, some may not. A timing closure that used only a fresh tt or ss corner has not asked this question. If the foundry provides an aged library or a recommended derate, that is the right place to look; this course does not invent one.

2. How this shows up in a digital flow

Digital STA normally uses process corners (ss, tt, ff) and RC corners, as in lesson 45. Aging is a further shift that depends on what the chip actually did: duty cycle, temperature history, supply. A path that toggles rarely may age mostly by BTI (DC bias); a hot, busy path may add HCI and self-heating. Treating “ss 125 °C” as a complete end-of-life model is a methodology choice, and only as good as the foundry data behind it.

Analog blocks can be more sensitive than a digital FO4: a current mirror whose two devices aged differently is an offset, which is lesson 75 plus time. Self-heating next to a matched pair is a layout problem as well as a reliability one.

Check your understanding — if you raise VDD to buy speed, which mechanisms do you feed?

Almost all of them. Higher VDS feeds HCI; higher |VGS| feeds BTI and TDDB; higher power feeds self-heating and EM (lesson 30). The speed you buy on day one is paid for in field, current and heat. That is one reason a product may ship at a lower advertised voltage than the PDK’s absolute maximum.

Key takeaways

  • HCI is a high-field, high-current, mostly permanent delay and gm loss. BTI is a bias-and-temperature Vth shift with partial recovery. TDDB is oxide breakdown — a fail, not a slowdown. Self-heating accelerates the others.
  • The ΔVth sketch and the table are teaching. This course has not extracted a SKY130 aging parameter.
  • EM (lesson 30) is the metal companion; latch-up (lesson 48) is a different sudden fail. Design-for-manufacturing and yield are lesson 32.
