Comparators & Power Management: StrongARM, LDO & POR

Three analog blocks every digital chip still needs: a clocked comparator that regenerates a decision, a regulator that holds a clean supply, and a power-on-reset that releases the digital only after that supply is legal.

Where the facts come from. The StrongARM schematic, the regeneration waveform, the LDO loop and the POR threshold-and-delay picture are teaching diagrams. They use the language already in lesson 74, lesson 73 and lesson 76. This lesson does not publish a SKY130 StrongARM, LDO or POR characterisation, and it does not claim a measured trip point or dropout. Caravel’s housekeeping power-on behaviour is described in earlier Caravel lessons from documentation, not from a board measurement we ran.

What you will learn

  • Sketch a StrongARM latch comparator and say what happens in precharge versus regeneration.
  • Draw an LDO as a loop — reference, error amplifier, pass device, feedback — and name dropout and dropout’s cost.
  • Describe a POR as a threshold plus a delay, and say why releasing reset too early is a digital failure.

1. The StrongARM is a precharged latch

A clocked comparator does not amplify continuously. In precharge (clock low, in the usual drawing) its output nodes are pulled to a known level, often the supply. When the clock rises, a tail current is steered by the input pair; a small differential at the inputs becomes a small differential at a pair of intermediate nodes; a cross-coupled pair then regenerates — the same positive feedback as two inverters in a loop — and the outputs run to the rails. The decision is a regenerative latch, not a linear amplifier, which is why a StrongARM is fast and why its input-referred offset and kickback matter.

Kickback is the clock-edge charge that couples back through the input-pair gates into the source that is being compared. Offset is a mismatch of the pair and of the latch (Pelgrom’s law, lesson 75). Neither is quantified here; the lesson-75 Pelgrom numbers remain the course’s SKY130 mismatch facts, and they are for devices, not for a finished comparator.

Check your understanding — why not just use a high-gain op-amp as a comparator and skip the latch?

You can, and some continuous-time comparators do. A regenerative latch is usually faster for a given current, because positive feedback grows an exponential, and it consumes current mainly in the decision window. The costs are kickback, a clock, and a decision that is not valid until regeneration has finished — so the following logic has to wait, just as a flop’s clk-to-Q has to wait.

2. An LDO is a loop that holds a voltage

A low-dropout regulator (LDO) takes a dirty or higher supply and holds a quieter, specified output. The loop is:

  • a reference (bandgap or otherwise — lesson 76) that does not move with the input;
  • an error amplifier that looks at a fed-back fraction of the output;
  • a pass device, usually a large PMOS, between the input supply and the output;
  • a load, including the digital current that jumps when a clock tree fires, and a capacitor that has to be stable with that load.

Dropout is the smallest input-to-output voltage at which the pass device can still regulate. Below dropout the pass device is just a resistor, and the output follows the input down. The “low” in LDO means that number is designed to be small (a few hundred millivolts in many chips), which is why a PMOS pass device is common: it can sit just a VDS,sat above the output, without an extra VGS stacked on top. Stability is a feedback problem (lesson 73): the load cap, the pass device’s resistance, and the error-amp compensation have to keep the loop from ringing when the digital current steps.

Check your understanding — the digital current jumps 10× at a clock edge. What does the LDO have to do in that instant?

The pass device cannot slew instantly. The output capacitor supplies the step for a moment, the output sags, the feedback notices, and the loop restores VOUT. If the cap is too small, or the loop is slow or poorly compensated, the sag (or a ring) can be large enough to corrupt a SRAM bit or shrink a setup margin — which is why an LDO is a stability problem, not only a DC voltage problem.

3. Power-on reset is a threshold and a delay

A power-on-reset (POR) watches the supply (and often the regulator output) and holds the digital in reset until two things are true: the voltage is above a threshold at which flops, memories and isolation actually work, and enough time has passed for that voltage to be stable and for clocks or references to start. Releasing reset while VDD is still climbing through a flop’s legal range is a classic bring-up failure: some registers wake in a one, some in a zero, some metastable, and a “clean” RTL reset never happened.

A simple POR is a reference compared against a fraction of VDD, plus a delay (an RC, a counter, a second threshold). A brown-out detector is the same idea on the way down. Lesson 76’s startup discussion is the analog sibling: a bandgap itself needs a kick, and a POR should not trust a reference that has not started. This lesson does not assign a SKY130 trip voltage.

Check your understanding — the supply is already at 1.8 V when you attach a programmer and release a chip-level reset. Do you still need a POR?

You still need something that is legal at the first moment VDD is applied, including on a board that is powered from a supply that ramps, brown-outs, or is plugged in live. A debugger reset is a later, software-visible event. The POR is the hardware guarantee that the first instruction, and the first flop, see a defined state. Bring-up (lesson 71) still checks that this happened, because a POR that trips too low, or that glitches, is a common “the chip is dead” cause.

Key takeaways

  • A StrongARM precharges, then regenerates a decision with a cross-coupled pair. Offset and kickback are the analog costs; no SKY130 comparator is characterised here.
  • An LDO is a feedback loop: reference, error amp, pass device, divider. Dropout is the headroom it needs; a load step is a stability problem.
  • A POR is a threshold plus a delay. Releasing the digital before the supply (and the reference) are legal is a reset that never happened.
