Lesson 65 / 84 · Part VII, The chip boundary

ESD and Clamps

A discharge on a pin has to be caught at the ring. This library puts the clamp in the power and ground pads, and it rates that clamp at 3.3 kV in a human-body test. That rating is not the supply voltage.

Where the numbers come from. The scheme, the 150 ohm resistor, the 3.3 kV note, and the outer-guard table are the sky130_fd_io user guide. Pin names and sizes are the LEF files. Availability statements refer to the pinned upstream fd_io release, not every later wrapper or extension package.

What you will learn

  • Follow an ESD current path from a pin through the pad ring to a supply clamp.
  • Read a human-body-model rating and keep it separate from operating voltages.
  • Identify which SKY130 pad cells contain the clamps.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Planned with the pad ring in step 4, floorplan and power grid and reviewed at signoff.
Tools
Mostly commercial: reliability checkers such as Calibre PERC trace discharge paths. The SKY130 I/O cells carry their own protection.
Why it matters
One static discharge during handling can destroy an unprotected chip.

1. The clamp sits on the supply, not on every signal pin

ESD is electrostatic discharge: a short, large current into a pin. The user guide calls this library's scheme rail-based and grouped. Grouped means the clamp is not repeated as its own device on every signal pad. The power and ground pads contain an RC-triggered clamp: the RC network detects the transient and turns on a large discharge device. The small trigger capacitor is not itself the main current path. The guide specifies a clamp for each external power domain, together with the prescribed steering and inter-domain connections. [I/O ESD scheme] Diodes go from each derived supply to the source supply. The guide names two clamp kinds, HV and LV. The guide does not state a supply voltage for either name.

The same guide says the HV and LV clamps are robust up to 3.3 kV HBM. HBM is a human-body-model discharge test. The 3.3 kV number is the guide’s stated clamp robustness in that test. It is not a guaranteed HBM qualification of every finished chip assembled from the cells: the entire discharge path, pad combinations, rail resistance, and integration conditions matter. The I/O operating range in the same document is 1.71 V to 5.5 V. Read those as two different sentences.

2. Which clamp cells are public, and where the clamps sit

The guide says an HV pad holds one HV clamp under the pad, with terminals that can be tied without disturbing the power connection. An LV pad holds two LV clamps and a back-to-back diode. The diode's ground pin in the guide is bdy_b2b. The LEF name on the opened LV cells is BDY2_B2B.

The guide's design notes for the HV clamp are a single trigger with a 2 µs time constant, a driver width of 2880 µm, and a cell height the same as the gpio. Those notes describe a clamp drawn without its own pad. The guide marks that cell, and the pad-only metal cells, as not publicly available. They are not in the 26-cell directory. The four pad cells above are public, and they are the ones with the clamps inside.

On top_gpiov2 the three core-side pad pins are PAD_A_NOESD_H, PAD_A_ESD_0_H, and PAD_A_ESD_1_H. The guide's lowercase names are pad_no_esd_h, pad_a_esd_0_h, and pad_a_esd_1_h. It says the first is connected directly to the pad, and the other two go through a 150 ohm ESD resistor. That 150 ohm is the guide's number. It was not re-measured as a drawn resistor.

OGC is the outer guard ring connection. The guide says it ties the deep n-well, the buried n-type region under the pad, so that region can be isolated from the rest of the chip. The recommended tie is an HV supply that is present most of the time. For the four opened power and ground pads, the guide's table says OGC is already connected inside the cell to VDDIO, and the end user does not need to connect it. The rows of that table for the clamp-without-a-pad are the cells marked not public.

3. The human-body test is a capacitor and a resistor

The simplified human-body-model network used to explain ANSI/ESDA/JEDEC JS-001 charges 100 pF and discharges it through 1.5 kΩ. Real standardized testing also specifies the waveform, pin combinations, and acceptance checks. This 1.5 kΩ belongs to the tester; it is not the 150 Ω resistor inside the pad path. [EOS/ESD Association]

On the part, diodes steer a high pin into the rails and a low pin into the rails, and the clamp between the rails takes the current. The guide’s 3.3 kV statement concerns the clamps; the 150 Ω entries concern the analog access paths. These are different parts of the protection network. The charged-device model is a different test, in which the part itself holds the charge. A CDM rating, if a design needs one, must come from the I/O library documentation or qualification data.

4. Test voltage, clamp voltage, and normal signal range differ

Quantity/testMeaningDo not infer
Normal operating voltageAllowed powered signal/supply conditions for a specified cell and modeThat a core pin tolerates the maximum I/O voltage
HBMComponent handling-discharge test using a charged source networkThat the pad node itself remains at the source’s kilovolts
CDMDischarge of charge stored on the deviceA CDM rating from an HBM rating
IEC 61000-4-2 system testSystem-level discharge conditions with a different waveformSystem qualification from the bare chip’s HBM result

Worked HBM scale estimate: with a 3.3 kV initial charge, the ideal 1.5 kΩ discharge resistor suggests I ≈ V/R = 2.2 A. The 100 pF capacitor initially stores E = ½CV² = 0.545 mJ. Actual peak current and device voltage depend on the standardized waveform, parasitics, and clamp behavior. The source energy is not all dissipated inside the chip. The important lesson is the ampere-scale transient through a path whose normal current may be tiny.

Analog ↔ digital: the pad protection path also loads ordinary signals. If an analog path contains 150 Ω and a downstream load is an assumed 2 pF, its simple RC time constant is 0.30 ns; ideal first-order settling to 0.1% takes ln(1000)τ ≈ 2.07 ns. At an assumed 1 mA DC load the same resistor drops 150 mV. Actual pin capacitance, switch resistance, leakage, and voltage dependence must be included. Bypassing the resistor to improve settling changes the protection path and needs an approved circuit, not merely a faster schematic.

An RC clamp also has a normal-operation requirement: it must release after a discharge and avoid unintended conduction on valid supply ramps. Review the intended ramp range and powered/unpowered pin cases with the library guidance. A surge, sustained overvoltage, and a standardized ESD pulse are different stresses.

Check your understanding — Does a 3.3 kV HBM clamp rating allow a 3.3 kV input in normal operation?

No. It describes a short standardized source discharge under specified conditions. The normal input limits remain those of the selected I/O cell, supply state, and mode; it does not imply CDM or system-level ESD qualification.

Check your understanding — what peak current does a 2 kV human-body-model discharge push into a pin?

The test network is 100 pF through 1.5 kΩ, so the peak is about 2000 V / 1500 Ω ≈ 1.3 A, decaying with a 150 ns time constant (1.5 kΩ × 100 pF). The protection path must carry that current while keeping thin oxides below breakdown.

Key takeaways

  • The scheme is rail-based and grouped. The RC clamp is inside the power and ground pads. Each external domain gets its own.
  • 3.3 kV HBM is a discharge-test rating. The operating range in the same guide is 1.71 V to 5.5 V.
  • PAD_A_NOESD_H is direct. The two PAD_A_ESD pins go through 150 ohm, per the guide.
  • HV pads list one clamp's pins. LV pads list two drains, BDY2_B2B, and OGC_LVC.
  • The clamp-without-a-pad is not in the public cell list. The public cells are the pads that contain the clamps.
  • The usual human-body network is 100 pF through 1.5 kΩ. This library's guide rating is 3.3 kV. No CDM voltage is on this page.
