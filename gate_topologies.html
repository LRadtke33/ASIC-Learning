More Gate Topologies

Beyond NAND and NOR: complex gates, transmission gates, tristates, and how a latch and flip-flop store a bit — with the netlist signatures to spot each one.

What you will learn

  • Recognize AOI and OAI gates, transmission gates and tristate inverters by their netlist signatures.
  • Explain how a latch stores a bit, and how two latches of opposite phase make an edge-triggered flip-flop.
  • Compare wide NAND and NOR gates using logical effort.

1. Complex gates: AOI and OAI

The series/parallel recipe isn't limited to two inputs of the same kind — you can mix series and parallel in one pull-down network and get a whole logic expression in a single stage. An AOI21 (AND-OR-INVERT, 2-input AND + 1 more input) computes out = !((A·B) + C): the pull-down puts A and B in series (the AND) and C in parallel with that pair (the OR); the pull-up is the exact dual. OAI gates are the same idea with series/parallel swapped.

An AND followed by a NOR uses about ten transistors in a simple complementary implementation; AOI21 uses six and removes one logical stage. This can save area and delay, but the actual benefit depends on transistor stacks, the switching input, pin capacitance, load and routing. Compare characterized arcs rather than assuming every complex gate is faster or lower-power.

2. The transmission gate: a switch, not a driver

A transmission gate connects two signal nets through an NMOS and PMOS in parallel, with complementary gate controls. The NMOS conducts low signal voltages well and the PMOS conducts high signal voltages well; together they pass a wider signal range than either alone. Their ON resistance remains voltage dependent and their OFF state has leakage and capacitive feedthrough. Allowed signal voltages and body connections matter; the pair is not an ideal short or an ideal open.

The recognition trick: in a netlist, a t-gate is the pair whose source and drain sit on the same two nets — and neither net is a supply. Devices not anchored to VDD/VSS are your tip-off that you've left plain pull-up/pull-down logic. T-gates are everywhere in multiplexers, latches, and flip-flops.

3. The tristate inverter: 1, 0, or disconnected

Add enable transistors inside an inverter's stacks and you get a gate that can also let go of its output: a tristate. With EN = 1 it inverts normally; with EN = 0 both networks are cut and the output floats — high-impedance, "Z". That third state is what lets several drivers share one net (a bus or a mux node): exactly one is enabled at a time, the rest go quiet. Click A and EN:

4. Storing a bit: the latch

Storage comes from positive feedback: two inverters in a loop reinforce one of two stable states while the supply, gain and noise margins remain adequate. A gated-feedback D latch uses an input transmission gate and a feedback transmission gate to choose between accepting data and holding it. Supply loss, excessive noise or metastability can defeat storage; “forever” is an idealization.

  • CLK = 1 (transparent): the input t-gate is closed, the feedback t-gate is open — D flows straight through to Q.
  • CLK = 0 (opaque): the input t-gate opens, the feedback t-gate closes the loop — the last value circulates and D is ignored.

5. The flip-flop: two latches, opposite phases

A common positive-edge D flip-flop combines a low-transparent master with a high-transparent slave. The master stops accepting data around the rising edge, and the slave transfers the stored state. Q changes after a clock-to-Q delay, provided D meets setup and hold and the clock meets pulse-width requirements. The edge is not an infinitely narrow analog sampling event; violations can cause metastability.

7. A wide NAND stays cheaper than a wide NOR, in the textbook count

The same catalog, for a gate with n inputs, gives a NAND logical effort (n+2)/3 and a NOR (2n+1)/3. Two inputs: 4/3 and 5/3. Four inputs: 2 and 3. The series transistors sit on the NMOS side in a NAND, and on the PMOS side in a NOR. The NOR number climbs faster because the wide devices are the ones in series.

That is the textbook PMOS-twice-NMOS count from lesson 11. It is the reason a wide function is usually written as a NAND with inversions around it, when the library delay tables agree. When they disagree, the table wins.

Analog bridge — Why does a switched node jump when its switch turns off?

The switch has channel charge and parasitic gate-to-channel capacitance. Changing its enable injects charge and couples a clock edge onto the held node. A transmission gate reduces some errors but does not make them vanish; source resistance, signal level, switch size, complementary-clock mismatch and hold capacitance all matter. For an assumed injected charge of 1 fC on 100 fF, ΔV = ΔQ/C = 10 mV. This is an illustrative charge budget, not a SKY130 device measurement.

Check your understanding — Why can an AOI21 be cheaper than an AND2 followed by a NOR2?

The AND-then-NOR cascade builds a complete AND2 (4 transistors) and a complete NOR2 (4 transistors), then wires them together — 8 transistors, plus the intermediate node. An AOI21 merges the AND into the NOR’s pull-down (two series NMOS) and the complementary pull-up, for 6 transistors and no intermediate output. Section 1 is that count. The Boolean function is the same; the complementary recipe is applied once, to the whole expression.

Check your understanding — A transmission gate can pass a 1 or a 0. Why is it still “a switch, not a driver”?

It has no path from a supply to the output that is turned on by the data. It connects two nodes when enabled, and those nodes then share charge and follow each other through the switch resistance. A heavy load is charged through that resistance, so the edge is slow and the driving gate on the other side of the switch is doing the work. A static CMOS inverter, by contrast, pulls Y to VDD or VSS with its own transistors. Section 2 is the distinction; the analog-bridge note below is what happens at the instant the switch opens.

Check your understanding — How many latches are in a flip-flop, and what must be true of their clocks?

Two, in series, on opposite clock phases (a master and a slave). Section 5: the master is open while the clock is low (or high, depending on polarity) and the slave is closed; they swap at the edge. That is why a flop captures on an edge rather than remaining transparent for a whole level. If both latches were open on the same phase, a path would exist from D to Q for that whole phase and the cell would be a latch, not a flop.

Key takeaways

  • AOI/OAI can reduce transistor count and logic stages; actual delay depends on input arc, load and implementation.
  • A transmission gate is a bidirectional switch with finite, signal-dependent ON resistance and OFF leakage/feedthrough.
  • A tristate adds enable devices in series inside the stacks; EN = 0 cuts both networks → output Z; that's how buses and mux nodes share a wire.
  • Storage = two inverters in a feedback ring; a latch adds t-gates so the loop either listens (transparent) or holds (opaque).
  • A common positive-edge flip-flop uses master/slave latches of opposite phases. Q updates after clock-to-Q delay when setup, hold and clock requirements are met.
  • Many sequential cells combine these building blocks; other topologies require their own connectivity, timing and state analysis.
  • In the textbook count a NAND4 has g = 2 and a NOR4 has g = 3. The series stack wants to be the NMOS stack.
