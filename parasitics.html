Lesson 28 / 84 · Part IV, Loads, timing and interconnect

Routing Resistance and Parasitics

A routed wire is a resistor and a capacitor. On SKY130 the short metal is mostly the capacitor. A long run of local interconnect is both.

Where the numbers come from. The numbers are from sky130_fd_sc_hd.tlef. Capacitance in that file is in picofarads, resistance in ohms, lengths in microns. 0.001 pF is 1 fF.

What you will learn

  • Derive first-order resistance and capacitance per micrometer from the tech LEF.
  • Decide when a wire is effectively a capacitor and when its resistance matters.
  • Use the pi model and an Elmore estimate for a resistive route.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
step 8, extraction and timing for digital blocks and step A4, verification for analog blocks.
Tools

Why it matters
On long nets, wire resistance and capacitance can exceed the delay of the gates.

1. A wire is distributed resistance and capacitance, not one load number

A timer looks up delay from one capacitance on the output (lesson 21, lesson 23). That number is real, and it is incomplete. The metal that connects the driver to the next gate has resistance along its length and capacitance to everything around it. Those are the parasitics: pieces of R and C that were not in the schematic, added by the route.

Draw the wire as a chain. Each short piece has a resistor in series and a capacitor to the AC ground of the chip. A gate input hangs on the end as one more capacitor, the pin cap from the Liberty file. If the resistors are tiny, the chain collapses back into the single capacitor lesson 21 used. If a resistor is large, the far end of the wire moves later than the near end, and the edge slows down on the way (lesson 25).

2. Derive first-order R and C per micrometer from the tech LEF

You do not start from a field solver to get a first number. For a long, straight, uniform wire, three technology-LEF fields give a first estimate. They do not describe the full surrounding geometry:

  • RESISTANCE RPERSQ — ohms per square. A square is a length of wire equal to its own width.
  • CAPACITANCE CPERSQDIST — picofarads per square micrometer of drawn metal. That is the plate, the area term.
  • EDGECAPACITANCE — picofarads per micrometer of edge. A wire has two edges, so count it twice. That is the sidewall, the fringe.

Minimum width is the WIDTH on the same layer. Per micrometer of a minimum wire:

C′ ≈ CPERSQDIST × width + 2 × EDGECAPACITANCE (pF/µm)
R′ = RPERSQ / width (Ω/µm); C = C′L, R = R′L

met1 is the one to check by hand, because lesson 21 already used it. WIDTH 0.14, CPERSQDIST 25.7784e-6 pF/µm², EDGECAPACITANCE 40.567e-6 pF/µm, RPERSQ 0.125. The area term is 25.7784e-6 × 0.14 = 0.00361 fF/µm. The two edges are 0.0811 fF/µm. Almost all of the capacitance is the edge, not the plate. Together, 0.0847 fF/µm. Resistance is 0.125 / 0.14 = 0.893 Ω/µm.

Thickness is quoted from the same LEF so you can see why the sheet resistance falls. The file does not give a resistivity, so this table does not derive one.

li1 worked the same way: width 0.17 µm, CPERSQDIST 36.9866e-6, EDGECAPACITANCE 40.697e-6, RPERSQ 12.2. Area cap 0.00629 fF/µm, two edges 0.0814 fF/µm, total 0.0877 fF/µm. Resistance 12.2 / 0.17 = 71.8 Ω/µm. The pitch 0.46 0.34 on a vertical layer is the x pitch, then the y pitch. 0.46 µm is also the standard-cell site width, so one vertical li1 pitch lines up with each site.

The area-plus-two-edges number is a wire alone. It does not know the neighbor on the next track. A sign-off extraction adds coupling capacitors to those neighbors. The tech-LEF number is the right size for a lesson and for a router's first estimate. It is not the capacitor an analog net gets after the wires beside it are known.

UNITS in the file: capacitance picofarads, resistance ohms, database 1000 units per micron. There is no current unit in that block. Current limits are the next lesson, and the unit there is the comment on each line.

3. A short met1 route is a capacitor. A long li1 route is not.

One inv_1 input is 2.30 fF. Divided by 0.0847 fF/µm, that is 27 µm of minimum met1. At 0.893 Ω/µm the resistance is about 24 Ω. Multiply 24 Ω by 2.30 fF and the time is under a tenth of a picosecond. The gate delay in lesson 25 is tens of picoseconds. On a hop this short, adding the wire means adding femtofarads to the load column and ignoring the resistance.

A longer li1 route is a different object. Two hundred micrometers is 200 × 71.8 Ω/µm = 14.35 kΩ, and 200 × 0.0877 fF/µm = 17.5 fF. The product is 252 ps. Lesson 21 rounded that same wire to about 14 kΩ, 18 fF, and 250 ps. Either way it is longer than the inv_1 rise delay of 0.073 ns into 9.5 fF (lesson 25). A route that stays on local interconnect for a long run is a resistor and a capacitor. The single load number misses the resistor.

For an ideal source driving a uniform RC wire with no end load, the Elmore first moment is RC/2, about 126 ps here. It is not the 50% propagation delay: the distributed-wire 50% step delay is approximately 0.38RC under those assumptions. With driver resistance Rd, wire Rw/Cw, and end load CL, a useful Elmore estimate is τE = Rd(Cw+CL) + Rw(Cw/2+CL). The end load is charged through both the driver and the wire. The distributed-wire propagation model distinguishes the 50% delay from the Elmore first moment. A timer uses its own waveform/delay model; do not simply add this complete estimate to a Liberty delay, which would count driver loading twice.

The same product on longer wires, minimum width, lumped R×C of the whole run: 1 mm of li1 is 71.8 kΩ and 88 fF, product 6.3 ns. 1 mm of met1 is 893 Ω and 85 fF, product 76 ps. 1 mm of met5 is 18 Ω and 88 fF, product 1.6 ps. Upper metal reduces distributed resistance, but driver impedance, vias, coupling, and edge rate still decide whether a lumped capacitor is adequate. Very fast or package-scale paths may also require inductance and transmission-line models. These three products use the table above. They are not extracted nets.

4. What you hand the timer

For a lumped load, pin caps plus wire cap form the load used with a total_output_net_capacitance lookup dimension. Resistive interconnect can require an effective load calculation and waveform propagation; the sum alone is not always what the driver dynamically sees. That is the whole job when the route is short metal. When the route is resistive, the timer needs the chain from Figure 1, not a fatter capacitor. Extraction writes that chain (a SPEF file is the usual name). The Liberty table still answers "how does this cell behave into a load." It does not contain the wire.

Slew travels with the wire. A resistive run stretches the edge, and the next gate is looked up at that slower input slew, not at the driver's own 20–80 time. Lesson 25 already did that handoff from one inverter to the next with no wire in between. A long li1 wire is a third stage in that handoff, with no cell name on it.

Vias have resistance too, but this tech LEF does not publish a resistance on the cut layers. The layer list above is the routing layers only. A via array between met1 and met2 is a current question as well as a resistance question; the current side is lesson 30.

Analog and digital readers split the same file differently. The digital timer wants the extra femtofarads, and the resistance only once it moves the arrival. The analog reader often needs coupling and DC voltage error; the digital reader also needs coupling-induced noise and delay changes when aggressors switch. Both start from the three LEF lines. Neither should treat 0.085 fF/µm as the finished capacitor of a wire that has neighbors.

In the open SKY130 flow. After routing, OpenRCX computes each net’s resistance and capacitance from the routed shapes and writes a SPEF file, which OpenSTA reads before it reports timing. LibreLane does this in its RCX and STAPostPNR steps. For analog blocks, Magic extracts a SPICE netlist with the parasitics instead.

extract_parasitics -ext_model_file <rcx_rules_file>
write_spef top.spef
read_spef top.spef
report_checks

5. The pi model splits the wire capacitance in half

A simple reduced representation for a straight wire is the pi model: half the capacitance at the near end, the resistance in the middle, half the capacitance at the far end. Elmore delay at the far end, with no extra load, is the resistance times the far half. For a uniform wire that is RC/2.

Use the table in section 2 on 100 µm of minimum li1: 71.8 Ω/µm and 0.088 fF/µm. Resistance is 7180 Ω. Capacitance is 8.8 fF, so each half is 4.4 fF. Elmore delay is 7180 Ω × 4.4 fF = 31.6 ps. That is the sheet estimate for a straight wire. A real route adds neighbors, vias, and the gate load, and final timing combines the extracted network with the library timing model, source slew, corners, and timing constraints.

6. Worked example: the same wire fails two different budgets

For 100 µm of minimum met1, the table gives Rw = 89.3 Ω and Cw = 8.47 fF. Assume a 1 kΩ linear driver and a 10 fF end load, purely for calculation. Then τE ≈ 18.47 ps + 1.27 ps = 19.74 ps. This first moment is a screening metric; it is not a characterized cell delay.

If the same geometry instead carries 100 µA of analog bias current, its DC drop is IR = 8.93 mV. That current is an additional assumption: a CMOS input load does not continuously draw 100 µA. A wire can be unimportant for a digital timing budget and important for an analog voltage budget.

OpenRCX extracts resistance, ground capacitance, and coupling based on calibrated rules and wire context. SPEF is common for digital timing; a simulator-compatible extracted SPICE or DSPF (Detailed Standard Parasitic Format) view is common for transistor-level work. File format alone does not guarantee preservation of hierarchical probe names. Inspect the extraction options and name map, then check connectivity against the source design.

Check your understanding: if wire length doubles, what scales by four?

For unchanged width and surroundings, wire R and wire C each double, so the unloaded wire's RC product and Elmore RC/2 term become four times larger. The term RwCL only doubles when the end load stays fixed. Driver loading also changes, so total path delay does not generally scale by four.

Check your understanding — is 200 µm of minimum-width li1 just a capacitive load?

No. This lesson’s numbers give that wire 14.35 kΩ and 17.5 fF, an RC product of about 252 ps — larger than typical gate delays. It needs a resistive, extracted model, or a move to a metal layer.

Lesson 29 carries these per-micrometer values into a complete delay budget for one net: SKY130 device current, contact and via cuts, the distributed wire and the receiving gate’s capacitance, followed by fanout, FO4 and repeaters.

Key takeaways

  • A wire is series resistance plus capacitance along it. The timer's one capacitor is that picture after the resistance stopped mattering.
  • Per micrometer, C ≈ (area × width) + two edges, and R ≈ sheet resistance / width. met1 minimum is 0.085 fF/µm and 0.893 Ω/µm.
  • Capacitance per micrometer is similar on every layer here. Resistance is not. li1 is 71.8 Ω/µm. met5 is 0.018 Ω/µm.
  • 27 µm of met1 is one inv_1 input and about 24 Ω. 200 µm of li1 is 14.35 kΩ and 17.5 fF, product 252 ps.
  • Short metal updates the Liberty load column. A resistive route needs the extracted chain. The table does not contain the wire.
  • The pi model puts C/2 at each end of R. On 100 µm of minimum li1 the Elmore estimate is 31.6 ps.
