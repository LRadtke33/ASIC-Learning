Pin Access & Porosity

Why a DRC-clean cell can still be a bad citizen — what makes pins reachable and cells routable-over.

What you will learn

  • Explain why pin access, not only DRC cleanliness, decides whether a cell is routable.
  • Recognize how neighboring pins, via enclosure and blockages compete for access points.
  • Describe porosity, and why SKY130 HD signal pins sit on li1.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
step 5, placement leaves room around pins; step 7, routing must reach every one of them.
Tools
OpenROAD’s detailed router, detailed_route, computes access points for every pin. Commercial: Innovus, IC Compiler II.
Why it matters
A pin the router cannot reach becomes a routing violation, however good the placement is.

1. A pin is a promise

Inside a cell, routing is hidden behind its abstract: pin shapes, power shapes and obstructions. In the teaching M1/M2 example an access point is a legal way to place a via and connecting wire on the pin while meeting enclosure, spacing and other rules. Count both the number and the diversity of usable access patterns; several points that all conflict with the same neighbor offer little resilience.

2. Pins compete with each other

Access points are counted per pin, but they're spent together. A via is a chunky object: once it lands, spacing rules push other vias and wires away. In a dense cell — an AOI or a flip-flop with many pins packed between the poly fingers — the pins' access points overlap and start stealing from each other:

  • Two nearby pin accesses can violate via-to-via or wire spacing if chosen together. Staggering the vias, choosing another access point or using another layer may resolve the conflict; the actual rule geometry decides.
  • A track over a pin can already be occupied by a through-route — a net just passing by — and that access point is gone.
  • The combination matters: each pin may look fine alone, yet there may be no legal assignment that connects all of them at once.

3. Porosity: routing over the cell

Keeping cell internals on lower layers can leave more upper-layer routing capacity. It does not make every upper track automatically free: pin-access vias, power routes, neighboring shapes and spacing rules also constrain it. An internal M2 jumper blocks its actual geometry and spacing halo, not necessarily an entire track across the whole cell. LEF obstruction shapes communicate these restrictions; router behavior depends on their exact shape and configuration.

4. Why this matters (and who fixes it)

Poor pin access doesn't fail loudly at first — the placer happily packs the cells, global route looks fine, and the pain surfaces late, as stubborn detail-route DRCs and unroutable pins in dense clusters. Typical mitigations, in order of preference:

  • In the library: lengthen pins to span more tracks, stagger pin positions so neighbors' access points don't collide, avoid internal M2 (or add explicit blockage shapes so the router at least knows).
  • In the tools: pin-access-aware placement (don't pack access-hungry cells side by side), cell padding/keep-out margins around known offenders.
  • In the design: lower utilization in hot regions — lesson 58's whitespace again, this time buying access points instead of tracks.

This is also the practical reason behind lesson 17's height trade-off: a 6T cell has so little internal room that its pins are tiny and few tracks pass over it — pin access is often the real limiter on how dense a library can go.

5. On SKY130 the signal pin is on local interconnect

The drawings in this lesson put the pin on met1, because that is the layer a first router story uses. In sky130_fd_sc_hd the signal pins in the LEF are li1 rectangles. inv_1 pin A is one of them, 0.33 µm by 0.24 µm, with gate antenna area 0.2475 µm². Pin Y is three li1 rectangles. The met1 shapes on that cell are the power rails. The router still has to hit the pin from the routing grid. The last step is the mcon cut from met1 down onto li1, instead of a via that lands on a met1 pin.

inv_1 and nand2_1 have no OBS block at all. Their abstracts tell the router about pins and rails, and they do not declare a blockage on met2. dfxtp_1 does have an OBS, and every shape in it is li1, mcon, or met1. The met1 straps run for several micrometers inside the 7.36 µm cell, so they do occupy met1 tracks. They do not occupy a met2 track. Figure 3's walled-off cell is what an internal met2 jumper does. This flop's LEF does not contain that jumper.

mcon is a 0.17 µm cut. met2 is the first vertical routing layer above met1, pitch 0.46 µm, the same pitch as the site. A legal connection is still "a track, a via, room for the enclosure." The track in question can be met2, with a short met1 jog and an mcon onto the li1 pin. Counting access points from the LEF rectangles alone, without the via enclosure and the neighboring pin, over-counts. The number of legal access points for pin A comes from the router’s pin-access analysis, not from the LEF alone.

Antenna area is the other promise on the pin. For these cells it matches W × L of the gates the pin touches, which is why inv_8 is eight times inv_1 and why the flip-flop's clock pin is 0.159 µm². A long route onto a small pin is the antenna problem. The LEF records the gate area the router is accumulating against. It is not, by itself, the diode the library may have added. That is a separate cell, and it is not drawn in this lesson.

6. A via wants both tracks at once

met2 is vertical with pitch 0.46 µm. met1 is horizontal with pitch 0.34 µm. A via from one to the other sits where a vertical track crosses a horizontal track. On SkyWater the signal pin is usually li1, and the same idea holds one layer down: the pin has to cover the spot the via will land.

A pin drawn between tracks can still be reached. The router jogs on the metal that is off grid, then hits the crossing. That jog uses a track the neighboring cell may have wanted. A pin that already covers the crossing spends no jog.

Worked example — counting access rectangles on inv_1

The HD LEF for sky130_fd_sc_hd__inv_1 (SIZE 1.380 × 2.720 µm, three sites) declares the following signal-pin ports, all on li1:

PinDirectionli1 RECT countGeometry (µm), from the LEF
Ainput1(0.320, 1.075) to (0.650, 1.315) — 0.33 × 0.24, area 0.0792 µm²
Youtput3A vertical spine: (0.720, 1.485)–(1.050, 2.465); (0.820, 0.885)–(1.050, 1.485); (0.720, 0.255)–(1.050, 0.885)

A router that wants a li1–met1 via onto A has one 0.33 × 0.24 µm window. Y is easier: three rectangles stacked up the cell, so more than one track can land a via. Power and ground pins (VPWR, VGND) are abutment rails on met1 and are not counted as signal access. The 0.0792 µm² input rectangle is the same area lesson 16 uses when it compares pin area to gate area — the two lessons are reading the same LEF.

The counting rule for a “pin-access count” is therefore: number of signal PORT rectangles on the preferred access layer, per pin, taken from the LEF, not from a drawing. A via also has to sit on a routing track (section 6); having a rectangle is necessary and not always sufficient. The worked checkpoint below is the assignment problem that follows from such a count.

Worked checkpoint — Two pins, two candidate positions

In a hypothetical access assignment, pin A can use positions p1 or p2, while pin B can use only p2. Assume one connection fits at each position and the p1/p2 pair is mutually legal. Choosing A → p2 blocks B. Choosing A → p1 and B → p2 connects both. Counting A’s two options was not enough; the router needed a compatible assignment across the pins. The positions are abstract candidates, not SKY130 spacing values.

Check your understanding — a cell’s pins are all easy to reach, but it contains internal met2 shapes. Why can it still hurt routing?

Internal met2 geometry, and the spacing halo around it, blocks tracks that other nets could use to pass over the cell. That lowers the cell’s porosity, so nearby nets must detour around it even though its own pins are accessible.

Key takeaways

  • Count both the number and diversity of legal access patterns, including via enclosure and spacing to neighboring shapes.
  • A pin between preferred tracks may need a legal jog or another access layer; it is not automatically unreachable.
  • Access choices interact. Via spacing and through-routes can prevent an otherwise valid set of individual candidates from being used together.
  • Porosity is available through-routing capacity. Internal M2 geometry and its spacing halo consume local resources, not necessarily full tracks across the cell.
  • Pin access can constrain utilization and compact cell architectures; inspect detailed-route behavior before attributing congestion only to logic density.
  • The checked inv_1 and nand2_1 have li1 signal pins and no OBS block; dfxtp_1 declares li1/mcon/met1 obstructions. These abstracts are not complete internal layouts.
