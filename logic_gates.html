Logic Gates at the Transistor Level

What's inside an inverter and a 2-input gate — and how to recognize those structures in a messy, connected-by-name schematic.

What you will learn

  • Read a logic level as a voltage range at the receiver, not an exact rail.
  • Build complementary pull-up and pull-down networks for an inverter, a NAND2 and a NOR2.
  • Recognize gate topologies in a connected-by-name netlist, including SKY130’s nand2_1.

1. What a logic gate is

A digital signal is an analog voltage interpreted as a logic level. A receiver accepts a low below its specified VIL limit and a high above its VIH limit; the region between them has no guaranteed Boolean interpretation. VSS and VDD are nominal output targets, but resistance, loading, noise and supply movement keep real voltages from being ideal rails. The switch networks below describe settled valid logic states.

2. MOSFETs as switches

CMOS logic uses two flavors of MOSFET. Each has a gate (the control), a drain and source (the two ends of the switch), and a body (bulk) terminal. Two rules cover almost everything:

  • NMOS: a channel forms as VGS rises above threshold. With its source near VSS, a high gate enables a pull-down. A pass NMOS driven only to VDD does not deliver an ideal rail-high output.
  • PMOS: a channel forms as VSG exceeds |VTP|. With its source near VDD, a low gate enables a pull-up. The controlling quantity is gate-to-source voltage, not the gate’s absolute voltage.

3. The CMOS inverter

The simplest gate has one PMOS and one NMOS, their gates tied to the input and their channels meeting at the output. At settled, valid rail-level inputs, one intended network conducts strongly while the other is off in the switch model. During an input transition both may conduct, and “off” devices still leak. Toggle the input below to inspect the ideal settled states.

Where the current flows: sourcing and sinking

After a purely capacitive load settles, its charging current falls to zero. Real static CMOS still has subthreshold, junction and gate leakage; a DC load or an intermediate input can produce additional current. During transitions the supply delivers both load-charging current and, for part of the edge, short-circuit current. Arrows below show conventional current.

4. The complementary static-CMOS recipe

A complementary static-CMOS combinational gate has a PMOS pull-up network and an NMOS pull-down network. For valid settled Boolean inputs these implement complementary conduction conditions. This is a powerful design family, not a description of every CMOS circuit: transmission-gate logic, dynamic logic, latches and analog circuits use other arrangements.

  • Series switches conduct only if all of them are ON — an AND-shaped condition.
  • Parallel switches conduct if any of them is ON — an OR-shaped condition.

With the same uncomplemented inputs applied to the two complementary networks, the usual single-stage series/parallel gates are inverting (negative-unate): NAND, NOR, AOI and OAI. AND/OR typically require an additional inverter. Functions using complemented inputs or different logic styles need separate analysis.

5. Two-input gates: NAND2 and NOR2

NAND2: output is 0 only when A and B are 1 — so the pull-down is two NMOS in series, and (by duality) the pull-up is two PMOS in parallel. Either input at 0 is enough to pull the output up. Click A and B:

NOR2 is the mirror image: output is 1 only when A and B are both 0 — series PMOS pull-up, parallel NMOS pull-down. Either input at 1 pulls the output down.

Need an AND or an OR? In CMOS they are built as NAND + inverter and NOR + inverter — that's why the standard-cell library is full of NAND2/NOR2 cells and why inverting logic is "free" while non-inverting logic costs a stage.

6. Finding the topology in a messy schematic

Real schematics and netlists rarely look like Figures 5 and 6. Devices are scattered across the page, nothing is drawn touching, and connections exist only because two pins carry the same net name. The circuit is there — you just have to reassemble it. This method applies to the complementary static gates covered here:

  1. Anchor on the supplies. Every PMOS with source on VDD starts a pull-up path; every NMOS with source on VSS starts a pull-down path.
  2. Find the output. It's the net touched by both PMOS drain(s) and NMOS drain(s) — where pull-up meets pull-down.
  3. Classify the connections. Two devices sharing a net that appears nowhere else form a series stack (that net is internal). Two devices spanning the same pair of nets are in parallel.
  4. Match the pattern. 1P + 1N sharing gate and drain = inverter. 2 series NMOS + 2 parallel PMOS = NAND2. 2 parallel NMOS + 2 series PMOS = NOR2. (Check the body terminals go to the expected supply, then ignore them.)

7. The SkyWater NAND2 uses those four transistors

Section 5's NAND2 is a real cell in sky130_fd_sc_hd: nand2_1. The SPICE netlist is two series NMOS and two parallel PMOS, which is the dual in section 4. Each NMOS is 0.65 µm wide. Each PMOS is a high-Vt device, 1.0 µm wide. All four lengths are 0.15 µm. Those are the same widths as inv_1, so each input of the NAND is about the same capacitance as the inverter's input: 2.32 fF against 2.30 fF. The series NMOS stack is two of the inverter's pull-downs, one after the other. That is the extra resistance lesson 11 pointed at, with the drawn widths filled in.

The Liberty function is (!A) | (!B). The node between the two NMOS exists in the SPICE netlist and does not exist as a pin. Lesson 18 shows that node staying in the silicon. The cell is three sites wide, 1.38 µm by 2.72 µm, the same outline as the smallest inverter.

In the netlist the NMOS gated by B sits on VGND, and the NMOS gated by A sits on Y. The two PMOS both run from VPWR to Y. Body terminals are VNB on the NMOS and VPB on the PMOS, the same four supply pins every cell in this library carries: VGND, VPWR, VNB, VPB. Pin A and pin B each have gate antenna area 0.2475 µm², which is (0.65 + 1.0) × 0.15, one NMOS gate plus one PMOS gate.

8. The output is a curve, and the current is a pulse

Sweep the input of the inverter from the low rail to the high rail and the output falls. The HD library supply those rails sit on is 1.8 V. The curve is steep through the middle. The exact switching threshold comes from simulation at a chosen corner, not from this sketch.

In the intermediate input-voltage region, both devices can conduct and draw current from VPWR to VGND. The path is reduced when the input settles sufficiently near a supply rail, not simply because the output has arrived. An input held at an intermediate voltage can sustain this current. Leakage remains even at settled rail inputs; Liberty can describe characterized leakage, while transistor models are needed for arbitrary analog bias.

Checkpoint — Is 0.9 V a logic 1 on a 1.8 V rail?

You cannot decide from VDD alone. Compare 0.9 V with the receiver’s specified VIL/VIH at its actual supplies, PVT and noise environment. It may be in the undefined region and can leave both input devices conducting. An RTL 0/1/X simulation does not predict that current or the receiver’s analog resolution.

Check your understanding — Why is the NAND2 pull-down two NMOS in series, not in parallel?

A NAND output goes low only when every input is high. Series NMOS implement that AND of conditions: both channels must conduct before Y is tied to VSS. Parallel NMOS would pull Y low if either input were high, which is a NOR. The complementary PMOS pair is in parallel, so a single low input is enough to pull Y up — the De Morgan dual of the series NMOS. The SkyWater HD nand2_1 in section 7 is exactly those four transistors.

Check your understanding — A static CMOS gate holds a 1 without a clock. What is doing the holding?

The PMOS pull-up. When the inputs demand a 1, the pull-up network is on and the pull-down is off, so Y is connected to VDD through a conducting channel and is not a floating node. A dynamic gate that stored charge on a capacitor would need a clock (or a keeper) to restore that charge; complementary static CMOS does not. Leakage still exists, which is why Figure 8 notes a residual current at the rails, but the logic level is defined by a path to a supply, not by a trapped charge.

Key takeaways

  • Logic states are voltage ranges defined at the receiver; VDD and VSS are nominal output targets, not exact guaranteed voltages.
  • NMOS: ON at gate = 1, pulls down. PMOS: ON at gate = 0, pulls up. Body terminals go to the supplies (technology-dependent — check the PDK).
  • Complementary static CMOS uses dual pull-up/pull-down networks for valid settled inputs. Both can conduct during an edge; leakage and DC loading remain in steady state.
  • Series = AND-shaped, parallel = OR-shaped; the pull-up is the series↔parallel dual of the pull-down. Simple complementary gates with uncomplemented controls are inverting; other CMOS logic styles exist.
  • NAND2 = 2 series N + 2 parallel P; NOR2 = 2 parallel N + 2 series P.
  • To decode a messy schematic: anchor on supply connections, find the net where PMOS and NMOS drains meet (the output), spot series stacks by their private internal nets and parallel devices by their shared net pairs, then match the pattern.
  • nand2_1 is that NAND with 0.65 µm NMOS, 1.0 µm high-Vt PMOS, and 0.15 µm length, on a 3-site cell.
  • At settled rail-level inputs, switching current subsides and leakage remains. A DC output load or intermediate input voltage can sustain additional current.
