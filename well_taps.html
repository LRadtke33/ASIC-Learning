Lesson 48 / 84 · Part VI, Physical cells and the floorplan

Well Taps and Latch-up

Most standard cells do not tie their own well. A one-site tap cell does that, so the n-well stays at the power rail and the wafer stays at ground.

Where the numbers come from. Cell sizes and pins are from the sky130_fd_sc_hd LEF and schematic spice. The foundry note is the SkyWater library page. The flow setting is from the open_pdks LibreLane configuration for this library.

What you will learn

  • Explain why wells and the substrate must be tied to the rails, and how latch-up starts.
  • Identify the SKY130 tap cells and their pins, starting with tapvpwrvgnd_1.
  • Know where the tap-spacing limit comes from: the foundry rules, not the cell LEF.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
step 4, floorplan and power grid, before any logic cell is placed.
Tools
OpenROAD’s tapcell. Commercial: well-tap insertion in Innovus and IC Compiler II.
Why it matters
Without taps the wells float, which risks latch-up: a destructive short between the supplies.

1. The well and the wafer have to sit on the rails

A CMOS cell puts the PMOS in an n-well and the NMOS in the p-type wafer under the chip. That wafer is the substrate. The n-well against the wafer, and each source against its well, is a diode. Those diodes are supposed to stay off.

If the well or the wafer is left floating, a current spike can turn on a pair of parasitic bipolar transistors, one PNP in the well and one NPN in the wafer. Together they are a latch: once both are on, they hold each other on and short VPWR to VGND. That short is latch-up. Contacts that reduce this risk tie the n-well to VPWR and the local p-type body/substrate to VGND. Contacts reduce well/substrate resistance; they do not make arbitrary current injection, overvoltage, or illegal spacing safe. [foundry library guidance]

The SkyWater foundry note says individual cells do not have that tie, with a few exceptions. Tap cells are placed on a staggered grid instead. The same note says those taps are also how a design does body bias and supports latch-up protection.

The parasitic pair sits in the same stack as the transistors. The PMOS source, the n-well, and the p-type wafer form a PNP. The NMOS source, the wafer, and the n-well form an NPN. Each collector is the other's base. Once both are on, current through one keeps the other on, and the supply is in a high-current state while the external circuit sustains the latch. It releases when voltage/current falls below the holding condition, commonly by removing power. The tap holds the n-well at VPWR and the wafer at VGND, so those two junctions stay off during normal operation. The cited library overview does not give a maximum distance between taps. Placement still has to satisfy the process body-contact rules; choosing a pitch is a constrained floorplan decision.

2. The cell the digital flow inserts is tapvpwrvgnd_1

sky130_fd_sc_hd__tapvpwrvgnd_1 is class CORE WELLTAP, site unithd, SIZE 0.460 BY 2.720. That is one site, the same 0.46 µm by 2.72 µm row pitch as lesson 13. The LEF pins are VGND and VPWR. The schematic subcircuit has those two ports and no devices.

The historical open_pdks/OpenLane variable FP_WELLTAP_CELL names this cell; the current LibreLane config uses WELLTAP_CELL for the same choice. Both body terminals are tied to the rails inside it, which is what the name says: VPWR and VGND are the well and the wafer. A flow that is not using a separate body voltage inserts this one.

In the open SKY130 flow. The command is OpenROAD’s tapcell, run by LibreLane’s TapEndcapInsertion step right after the rows are created. It places a tap in every row at a fixed maximum spacing and an endcap at each row end. Commercial place-and-route tools have matching commands.

tapcell -distance <max_tap_spacing> \
        -tapcell_master sky130_fd_sc_hd__tapvpwrvgnd_1 \
        -endcap_master sky130_fd_sc_hd__decap_3

3. Tap variants have different pin lists

The LEF abstract of every tap below lists only the two rails. The schematic is where the body pins show up, and they are not the same cell to cell. An analog block that biases the body needs appropriate body ports and an independently isolated body region; the port list alone does not establish that isolation. The cited conventional digital flow uses tapvpwrvgnd_1, where those pins are not separate schematic ports.

All five are CLASS CORE WELLTAP. tap_1 and tap_2 bring both body nets out. tapvgnd_1 has VPB and does not have VNB. Opening the LEF and expecting to see VNB will fail on every one of these: the body port lives in the schematic, and only on the cells whose port list includes it.

The two bipolars, named the usual way: the PNP is the PMOS source (emitter), the n-well (base), and the p-type wafer (collector). The NPN is the NMOS source (emitter), the wafer (base), and the n-well (collector). Each collector is the other base. That loop is a silicon controlled rectifier. The tap holds the well-to-wafer diode off and gives each base a path to a rail, so a spike has to develop voltage across a real contact instead of across a floating well.

Neither the LEF nor the SPICE netlist states a latch-up spacing; that limit is a foundry design rule. The flow inserts tapvpwrvgnd_1. The floorplan sets their positions, but the maximum allowed tap distance is constrained by the process and the approved library/flow rules, including coverage near macros, row ends, and isolated wells. An absent pitch in a cell LEF does not make spacing arbitrary. [tap placement and row handling] The spice says there is no transistor. It does not say where the contact is drawn.

Logic cells in this library still list VNB and VPB as supply pins (lesson 16). Those pins are the transistor bodies. The tap is the cell that connects the well and the wafer to something. One HD cell is both a level shifter and a well tap; that is lesson 54.

4. A flipped row flips the tap with the rail

The well contact in tapvpwrvgnd_1 is drawn on the VPWR side, and the wafer contact on the VGND side. Flip the row and both contacts flip. They still land on the right rail, because the rails flipped too.

A staggered tap grid puts the next row's tap in a different column, so the wells are tied on a lattice rather than in one vertical stripe. The spacing must satisfy the applicable body-contact rules and the flow’s distance convention. OpenROAD’s distance option describes its checkerboard; a drawn x-pitch alone is not a universal maximum distance to a tap.

5. Body voltage is part of the circuit

In the HD transistor netlists, VNB connects to the NMOS body and VPB to the PMOS body. Follow actual net connectivity; do not infer well polarity from a pin spelling or one Liberty metadata field. A body port in SPICE does not prove that the layout has an independently isolated well. Separate NMOS body bias may require an isolated p-well/deep-n-well structure and suitable guard rings.

Worked example — assumed local body network: an injected 100 µA flowing through an effective 2 kΩ body resistance develops ΔV = IR = 0.20 V. Reducing that effective resistance to 200 Ω reduces the same disturbance to 20 mV. These are teaching assumptions, not a SKY130 latch-up threshold. They explain why a distant tap can allow a local body voltage to move even though the schematic body net has the right name.

Analog ↔ digital: digital switching and I/O injection can disturb an analog device’s body, changing threshold and bias. Taps and guard rings help collect current, but they also have parasitic resistance and capacitance. Connectivity, isolation, contact distance, and injection limits all belong in the mixed-signal review.

Check your understanding — Does a no-device SPICE tap mean the cell does nothing?

No. It is a physical well/substrate contact structure. An empty schematic subcircuit does not model the distributed body resistance or latch-up network. Layout connectivity and the process rules supply that information.

Check your understanding — two well taps sit far apart in a long row. Why does that raise latch-up risk?

The well and substrate between the taps reach the rails only through their own resistance. A current spike injected far from a tap develops more voltage across that resistance, which can forward-bias the parasitic bipolar junctions and trigger latch-up. Closer taps lower the resistance; the foundry rule sets the maximum distance.

Key takeaways

  • Latch-up is a supply short that holds itself on. The n-well has to stay on VPWR and the wafer on VGND.
  • Most HD cells do not contain that tie. The LibreLane configuration inserts tapvpwrvgnd_1.
  • That cell is one site, 0.46 × 2.72 µm, class WELLTAP, and its spice has no transistors.
  • tap_1 exposes VNB and VPB in the schematic. tapvgnd_1 exposes VPB only. The LEF of each tap lists the rails.
  • A cell LEF does not set a universal tap pitch. The floorplan must obey the process and library body-contact rules.
  • Flipping a row flips the tap, and the well contact stays on VPWR. A staggered grid must still provide legal coverage near macros, well boundaries, and row ends.
