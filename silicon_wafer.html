The Silicon Wafer: From Crystal to Dies

Every SKY130 die starts as one rectangle in a grid on a 200 mm disc of single-crystal silicon. The wafer sets how many dies a run produces, how many of them work, and how several designs can share one manufacturing run.

Reading the evidence. Wafer diameter and thickness are SEMI standard values; SkyWater processes 200 mm wafers. Scribe width, edge exclusion and defect density are stated assumptions, not SkyWater data.

What you will learn

  • Describe how a silicon wafer is made and why it is a single crystal.
  • Estimate dies per wafer and yield from a die size, a scribe width and an assumed defect density.
  • Explain scribe lines, seal rings, wafer test, dicing and multi-project wafers.

Why it matters
Die size and defect density decide how many good dies each wafer gives, and so the cost of every chip.

1. Silicon, purified and grown as one crystal

Silicon is a semiconductor. Pure silicon conducts poorly, but adding a tiny fraction of other atoms — doping — makes it conduct in a controlled way. Phosphorus or arsenic atoms donate free electrons, making n-type silicon. Boron atoms accept electrons, leaving mobile “holes” that behave like positive charges, making p-type silicon. Transistors are built by placing n-type and p-type regions next to each other (lesson 4).

A wafer is cut from a single crystal, so the atomic lattice is continuous across the whole disc. In the common Czochralski method a small seed crystal is dipped into molten, highly purified silicon and slowly pulled upward while rotating. The melt freezes onto the seed with the same orientation, growing a cylindrical ingot. A controlled amount of dopant in the melt sets the starting type; the SKY130 process stack is built on a p-type substrate (lesson 3). The ingot is ground to diameter, sliced, and the slices are lapped and polished to a mirror finish.

A 200 mm wafer is nominally 725 µm thick (±20 µm) and has a small notch at its edge so that machines can align to the crystal orientation; 300 mm wafers are 775 µm thick. SkyWater’s Bloomington fab processes 200 mm wafers.

Almost all of that thickness is mechanical support. In the SKY130 stack diagram the top of the finished layer stack sits about 11.9 µm above the silicon surface — under 2% of the wafer’s thickness. Wafers are often ground thinner from the back before packaging.

2. Dies sit in a grid, separated by scribe lines

The wafer is patterned with a repeated grid of identical rectangles: the dies. The lithography tool exposes one rectangular field at a time and steps across the wafer, so one layout is copied hundreds or thousands of times (lesson 3). Between neighboring dies runs a narrow street called the scribe line (also dicing street or kerf), where the wafer will be cut.

The scribe line is not wasted space. Foundries place alignment marks and process-monitor test structures in it — resistors, transistors and capacitors that are measured during manufacture to confirm that each layer came out as intended. These structures are destroyed when the wafer is cut.

The wafer edge costs dies twice. Rectangles that cross the circular edge are incomplete. A narrow ring at the edge, the edge exclusion, is also not guaranteed to meet specifications, because film thickness and patterning are less uniform there.

3. Worked example: Caravel dies on a 200 mm wafer

A common first estimate of gross dies per wafer for wafer diameter d and die-plus-scribe area S is:

dies ≈ π(d/2)² / S − π·d / √(2S)

The first term divides the wafer area by the area of one die step; the second subtracts the partial dies along the edge. With the Caravel die (3.60 × 5.20 mm) and an assumed 80 µm scribe, one step is 3.68 × 5.28 mm, so S = 19.43 mm². For the full 200 mm the estimate is 1516 dies; using a 194 mm usable diameter (3 mm edge exclusion) gives 1424. Counting complete rectangles directly on the grid in Figure 2 gives 1440.

The formula and the direct count differ because the formula averages over grid positions, while a real wafer map has one grid placement. Foundry wafer maps also reserve some sites for test dies.

4. Yield: why large dies cost more than their area

Defects — particles, pattern faults, scratches — land on the wafer more or less at random. The simplest model treats them as independent, with an average density D₀ of killing defects per unit area. The chance that a die of area A receives none is the Poisson yield:

Y = e−A·D₀

Assume D₀ = 0.1 per cm² for illustration. The Caravel die is 0.1872 cm², so Y = e−0.01872 = 98.1%. A 1 cm² die would yield 90.5%, and a 4 cm² die 67.0%. Doubling the area squares the yield fraction, so the cost of a good die grows faster than its area. Real yield models account for clustered defects and for dies that work but miss a specification; the foundry’s data, not this formula, set real expectations.

Before the wafer is cut, an automatic prober can touch each die’s pads with a probe card and run electrical tests. Results are stored in a wafer map so that failing dies are not packaged. This step is called wafer sort or probe test.

Check your understanding — if the defect density doubles to 0.2 per cm², what happens to the yield of the Caravel die and of a 1 cm² die?

Caravel: e−0.0374 = 96.3% (down from 98.1%). The 1 cm² die: e−0.2 = 81.9% (down from 90.5%). The larger die loses about five times as many percentage points.

5. Dicing, the seal ring and thinning

After the wafer is finished and tested, it is mounted on tape, often thinned by grinding the back, and cut along the scribe lines with a diamond saw or a laser. Cutting chips and stresses the silicon at the edge. A seal ring — a continuous wall of stacked metal and vias running all the way around the die, just inside the scribe — stops cracks and moisture from travelling from that edge into the circuitry. The open_pdks distribution includes a seal-ring generator script for SKY130.

The Caravel drawings show the die 12 µm larger than its padframe in each direction: 3.600 × 5.200 mm against 3.588 × 5.188 mm. That leaves a 6 µm band on every side, outside the pads, at the die edge (lesson 5).

6. Many designs, one wafer: multi-project wafers

A full set of masks costs far more than most research, teaching or startup projects can justify. A multi-project wafer (MPW), also called a shuttle, shares one mask set and one wafer run among many designs. Each design occupies part of the repeated field, every wafer carries copies of all of them, and after dicing each team receives its own dies.

The open SKY130 shuttles used Caravel as the shared frame: each slot is a complete Caravel die whose 2.92 × 3.52 mm user area holds one project. Tiny Tapeout divides a single user area further into small tiles, so that hundreds of designs share one slot.

Shuttle availability changes. Efabless, which created Caravel and ran the original Google-sponsored shuttles, shut down in early 2025. ChipFoundry acquired its assets and restarted Caravel-based SKY130 shuttles, and other SKY130 MPW services have been announced. This lesson does not describe any one service’s prices or schedule; check the current provider’s rules before you plan a tapeout.

Check your understanding — why are dies on the wafer edge lost even when most of the die is printed?

A die must be complete and inside the region where the process is guaranteed. A rectangle that crosses the wafer edge is physically missing part of its circuit, and one inside the edge-exclusion ring sits where film thickness and patterning are least uniform.

Key takeaways

  • Wafers are slices of a single silicon crystal; SKY130 is built on 200 mm wafers, 725 µm thick, on a p-type substrate.
  • Dies sit in a grid separated by scribe lines that carry test structures and are removed when the wafer is cut.
  • With an assumed 80 µm scribe, about 1440 complete Caravel-sized dies fit on one 200 mm wafer.
  • Poisson yield Y = e^(−A·D₀): larger dies lose yield faster than their area grows.
  • A seal ring protects the die edge; a multi-project wafer lets many designs share one mask set.
