Lesson 46 / 84 · Part V, Digital implementation

Functional Verification: Testbenches, Assertions, Formal & Gate-Level Sim

A 4-bit Gray counter and a 4-deep FIFO, checked three ways that were actually run: a self-checking Verilog testbench, a cocotb Python test, and SymbiYosys formal — then the counter is mapped to sky130_fd_sc_hd and simulated at gate level, with OpenSTA SDF delays measured in Icarus.

Where the numbers come from. Every PASS, FAIL, cover step, cell count and delay in this lesson was recorded on 11 November 2026. Icarus Verilog 12.0 ran the self-checking testbenches and the gate-level simulations; cocotb 2.1.0 ran the Python tests against the same Icarus; SymbiYosys with Yosys 0.62 and Z3 4.13.3 ran BMC, cover and k-induction; Yosys 0.62 mapped gray4 onto sky130_fd_sc_hd; OpenSTA 2.7.0 wrote the SDF. Sources, scripts and the recorded log are in labs/analysis_examples/verification/. UVM is discussed as an industry idea only — it was not run.

What you will learn

  • Write a self-checking testbench with a require task and a scoreboard, and drive it so it cannot race the DUT clock.
  • Run the same checks from Python with cocotb, and say what coverage would add.
  • Express a property as an assertion, prove it with bounded model checking and k-induction, and watch formal produce a counterexample for a planted bug.
  • Map a design to SKY130 HD, simulate the netlist, and measure a CLK→Q delay that matches an OpenSTA SDF IOPATH.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Functional simulation starts with the RTL at step 3, synthesis and is repeated on the gate netlist after step 8, extraction and timing signoff, using an SDF from the same STA that signed the chip off. Multi-corner STA itself is lesson 45.
Tools

Why it matters
A clean timing report says the chip can meet the clock. It does not say the chip does the right thing. That is this lesson’s job.

1. What has to be true

The two teaching designs are small on purpose. gray4 is a 4-bit Gray-code counter: after reset the output is 0000; each enabled clock advances one Gray step (Hamming distance 1) and wraps from 1000 back to 0000. sync_fifo is a 4-deep, 8-bit synchronous FIFO: writes are ignored when it is full, reads are ignored when it is empty, occupancy is consistent, and data comes out in the order it went in.

Those sentences are the specification. A testbench is a machine that tries to catch the design disagreeing with them. It can do that by walking through a prepared sequence (simulation), by speaking the sentences as assertions and asking a solver to find a counterexample (formal), and by repeating the same checks on the gates the synthesizer actually emitted, with real cell delays (gate-level simulation). The right picture is not a pipeline that you finish once; it is the same properties, checked at three depths.

2. A self-checking testbench, without races

A testbench that prints “done” is not a test. The pattern used here is a require(ok, msg) task that increments an error counter and a $display("FAIL …") when a prediction misses, and a final PASS only when that counter is still zero. For the FIFO, the prediction is a queue scoreboard: every accepted write is pushed, every read is compared with the front of the queue.

The other habit is about time. If the testbench assigns en or din at the same rising edge the DUT samples, the simulator is free to let either side go first — and we watched that race fail, on the first draft of these testbenches, in exactly that way (the first Gray step was skipped; the first FIFO byte was lost). The fix is the one you should copy: drive and sample on the falling edge when the DUT is rising-edge clocked. A 10 ns teaching period gives 5 ns of clear setup on both sides of the edge.

Icarus Verilog 12.0, with those testbenches:

PASS  tb_gray4       32 steps + 8 holds, 0 errors
PASS  tb_sync_fifo   fill/full-ignore/drain/simultaneous, 0 errors

The FIFO test fills four unique bytes, confirms full, proves a write-to-full is ignored, drains against the scoreboard, then does a simultaneous write and read at mid-occupancy and checks that the count stays two and that the bytes still come out in order. The Gray test walks two full wraps (32 steps) checking Hamming distance, the binary decode, and the Gray encoding, then freezes en for eight cycles. You can rerun both with labs/analysis_examples/verification/run.sh.

3. The same checks in Python: cocotb 2.1.0

cocotb drives the same DUT through the same simulator, but the test is a Python coroutine. A Clock object ticks the clock; FallingEdge(dut.clk) waits; dut.en.value = 1 deposits a stimulus; assert is the checker. The Gray and FIFO tests in cocotb/test_gray4.py and test_sync_fifo.py mirror the Verilog testbenches — including falling-edge sampling and a Python list as the FIFO scoreboard.

cocotb 2.1.0, Icarus 12.0
  test_gray4.test_reset_and_sequence         PASS    425.00 ns
  test_sync_fifo.test_fill_drain_scoreboard  PASS    225.00 ns

The payoff is not that Python is prettier. It is that the scoreboard, a file-format model, or a bit-accurate reference can be ordinary Python, and that the same test can be pointed at Icarus today and at a commercial simulator tomorrow. A Makefile in the lab folder is the whole build: make SIM=icarus from cocotb/.

Coverage asks a different question: not “did this test pass?” but “which of the things that can happen did the test actually try?” Code coverage (lines, branches, FSM states) is what the simulator can count for you. Functional coverage is what you write: a coverpoint on FIFO occupancy, a cross of full with a write, a wrap of the Gray counter. We did not collect a coverage database on this run; the formal cover statements in section 5 are the same idea, asked of a solver rather than of a test.

4. Assertions, and the SVA you will see at work

An assertion is a property the design is not allowed to violate, checked continuously rather than at a single require call. SystemVerilog Assertions (SVA) write those properties with a clock and a disabling condition:

property p_hamming;
    @(posedge clk) disable iff (!rst_n)
        en |=> ($countones(gray ^ $past(gray)) == 1);
endproperty
assert property (p_hamming);

That is the concurrent form: “if en was high, the next Gray value is one bit away.” A cover property asks the complementary question: “is there any legal trace that reaches this state?” An assume property restricts the stimulus (for example, “the testbench will never write when the FIFO is full”) — dangerous if you assume something the real world will not honour.

The open-source Yosys frontend used below consumes immediate assertions, written as assert (…) inside an always @(posedge clk) block, guarded by a f_past_valid flag so that $past is not examined on the first cycle. Those are the properties that were actually proved. A concurrent-SVA illustration of the same Gray properties sits in the lab folder as formal/gray4_sva_illustration.sv, for reading; it was not one of the recorded SymbiYosys runs.

5. Formal: BMC, cover, k-induction, and a mutant

SymbiYosys (sby) wraps Yosys and an SMT solver. This run used Yosys 0.62, yosys-smtbmc, and Z3 4.13.3. A system Boolector 1.5 that happened to be on PATH rejected SMT-LIB set-option and is the wrong engine; the .sby files therefore say smtbmc z3.

Three modes, three questions:

  • Bounded model checking (BMC) unrolls the design for k cycles and asks: is there any input sequence of length ≤ k that violates an assertion? A PASS means no bug of that depth — not that the design is correct for ever.
  • Cover asks the solver to find a trace that reaches a state, and writes a VCD when it does. A cover that is reached in two steps, on a property that should take a full Gray wrap, is often a vacuous trace that never went through reset; more on that in a moment.
  • Prove, here by k-induction: if the assertions hold for k cycles from reset (the base case) and any window of k legal cycles can be shown to extend by one more legal cycle (the inductive step), the properties hold for every depth. A PASS here is a real proof, for those assertions.

Recorded results, all with an explicit starting reset (initial assume (rst_n == 0)):

Table 1. SymbiYosys runs, 11 November 2026. Engine: smtbmc z3.
JobModeDepthResult
gray4_bmcBMC24PASS
gray4_covercover24PASS, wrap reached at step 18
gray4_proveprove (k-induction)16PASS, basecase and induction
gray4_bugBMC on a binary counter12FAIL at step 4, Hamming-distance assertion
fifo_bmcBMC20PASS
fifo_covercover20PASS, three covers reached

The mutant is the teaching tool. gray4_bug.v is a plain binary counter pretending to be Gray. BMC finds the Hamming-distance failure at step 4: after reset (0000), one enabled step to 0001 (distance 1, legal), the next step to 0010 (distance 2, illegal). Formal does not need a hand-written test for that case; it searches for it.

The starting-reset assume is not optional decoration. Without it, the first FIFO BMC failed — because formal is free to power the occupancy register up at 3'b111, which is already count > 4, and a combinational assertion fires before any clock. A first cover of the Gray wrap came back at step 2, which is too fast to be a real wrap: the solver had picked a register value that already satisfied the cover, without ever going through reset. After initial assume (rst_n == 0) and a “seen reset, then seen 1000” flag, the wrap is reached at step 18, which is exactly reset plus sixteen increments. Formal explores every initial state you do not rule out. Rule out the ones your hardware will not have.

6. Gate-level simulation on sky130_fd_sc_hd

Yosys 0.62, with the HD tt_025C_1v80 Liberty file, mapped gray4 to 16 standard cells with a chip area of 196.4384 µm²: four dfrtp_1 flops and a handful of inverters, muxes, and XOR/XNOR/AOI gates. The netlist is gl/gray4_gl.v. OpenSTA 2.7.0 linked that netlist against the same Liberty, created a 10 ns clock, and wrote gray4.sdf (290 lines, timescale 1 ns, voltage 1.80, temperature 25 °C). There is no SPEF, so every interconnect delay in that SDF is zero; the cell IOPATHs are real. On flop _21_:

(CELLTYPE "sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfrtp_1")
(INSTANCE _21_)
  (IOPATH CLK Q (0.374::0.374) (0.389::0.389))

That is a 0.374 ns rising and 0.389 ns falling CLK→Q, at the load OpenSTA computed for that instance.

Two Icarus simulations sit on either side of that file.

Functional gate-level, no SDF. The SKY130 functional models (`define FUNCTIONAL, no power pins, UNIT_DELAY #0.001) simulate the netlist as zero-delay logic with a 1 ps placeholder. Icarus 12.0: PASS tb_gray4_gl USE_SDF=0 32 steps, 0 errors, and every captured CLK→Q was 0.001 ns — the unit delay. The Gray properties still hold of the synthesized gates. That is already worth doing: synthesis can drop a reset, invert a polarity, or map a latch you did not ask for, and a RTL testbench will not see it.

OpenSTA delays, measured. Icarus 12.0 accepted a $sdf_annotate of gray4.sdf but did not apply the IOPATH delays: a controlled one-buffer test with a known 0.374 ns SDF left the measured delay at 0. The SKY130 timing models (the ones with specify blocks that SDF would back-annotate) also cannot be used as-is in this Icarus — $setuphold delayed clocks are not driven, and the flops go to X. Both limits are of this simulator, not of the SDF. The delays themselves were compiled as specify numbers taken from that same OpenSTA IOPATH, in gl/tb_ckq_specify.v, and Icarus with -gspecify then measured:

Q=1  CLK→Q = 0.3740 ns
Q=0  CLK→Q = 0.3890 ns
CKQ  n=8  rises=4  falls=4  min=0.3740  mean=0.3815  max=0.3890 ns
PASS  tb_ckq_specify

OpenSTA’s own check of that netlist, at tt and 10 ns, reported a setup worst slack of 8.40 ns (MET) and a datapath hold of 0.10 ns (MET). A removal check on rst_n is violated (−0.31 ns) because the input was left unconstrained; that is a constraint issue, the same class of problem as an unconstrained async reset in lesson 39, not a hold failure of the Gray datapath.

X-propagation. In a unit-delay functional sim, a flop that never sees a clean reset may still power up as 0 in the Verilog. In a timing model, a setup or hold violation can drive the output to X, and X then spreads through the combinational cone. A test that passed at RTL and failed with Xs at gate level is often a real race — or a testbench that is still changing inputs on the active edge. When you move this flow to a simulator that does apply SDF and honour $setuphold, treat an X as a failure until you have explained it. Icarus, on the functional models, will not give you that X; do not take a clean functional-GL PASS as evidence that hold is safe. Hold is a STA question (lesson 45), confirmed in a timing-aware gate sim when the simulator can represent it.

7. Equivalence, and what we did not run

Yosys also ships EQY, an equivalence-checking wrapper: it builds a miter between a “gold” RTL and a “revised” gate netlist and tries to prove the outputs match for every input. That is the right tool when you want to say “synthesis did not change the function,” independently of any test. It was not run on this gray4 netlist — the HD cells would need a functional expansion the lab does not set up. The recorded substitute is the combination of formal properties on the RTL and a passing gate-level sim of the same properties. An EQY PASS would still be a stronger synthesis check, and is the next thing to add if you extend the lab.

8. UVM, briefly

UVM (Universal Verification Methodology) is the SystemVerilog class library most commercial digital teams use to scale the left-hand box of Figure 1. A driver puts transactions on a bus; a monitor watches the pins and turns them back into transactions; a scoreboard — the same idea as the FIFO queue in section 2 — compares those against a reference model; a sequencer and a constraint solver generate the stimulus. None of that was run here. For a block the size of gray4 it would be heavier than the design. For a chip with several buses, several privilege modes and a queue of engineers, it is how the self-checking testbench stops being a single file. The properties in sections 4 and 5 still belong in that world: UVM is a stimulus and scoreboard framework, not a replacement for assertions or formal.

9. A verification checklist

  • A specification you can point at: “Hamming distance 1,” “no drop, no reorder,” not “it looks like it works.”
  • A self-checking testbench with an error counter, a scoreboard or reference, a deliberate failing case (the formal mutant in this lesson; a sabotaged mailbox in Lab 5), and stimulus that does not race the clock.
  • The same checks in a form you are willing to grow — cocotb or UVM — and a coverage question you can answer.
  • Assertions on the properties that must always hold, proved with BMC for depth and with induction (or a commercial formal tool) for unboundedness. Assume a starting reset. Read every FAIL trace; plant a bug on purpose once, so you know the assertion is wired up.
  • A gate-level sim of the SKY130 netlist, with an SDF from the same OpenSTA corner you care about. Know what your simulator actually does with $sdf_annotate and with X. Do not confuse a functional-model PASS with a timing-aware one.
Check your understanding — a FIFO BMC fails on the first cycle, with count == 7, and nobody has even raised wr. What should you look at before you “fix” the RTL?

Formal is using an arbitrary initial value of the occupancy register. A 3-bit counter can power up as 7, which already violates count ≤ 4. Add initial assume (rst_n == 0) (or equivalent) so every trace starts in reset, and only then decide whether the RTL is wrong. This is the failure we saw before adding that assume, and it disappeared afterwards (FIFO BMC PASS at depth 20).

Check your understanding — a Gray-counter testbench, driven on the rising edge, reports Hamming failures starting at the first enabled step. The RTL looks right. What is the more likely bug?

A race: the testbench changed en at the same edge the DUT sampled, and the first increment either happened “for free” or was missed, so the scoreboard and the DUT were one step apart from then on. Drive and sample on the opposite edge. That is the failure the first draft of tb_gray4 had, and the falling-edge rewrite is what produced the recorded PASS.

Check your understanding — functional gate-level simulation of a SKY130 netlist PASSes, with CLK→Q equal to the unit delay. Can you conclude hold is safe at ff?

No. The functional models do not apply SDF IOPATHs or $setuphold, and a unit-delay sim will not expose a hold race. Hold is a STA question at the min-ff corner (lesson 45). A timing-aware gate sim, on a simulator that actually annotates the SDF and propagates X on a violation, is the confirmation — not a functional-model PASS.
