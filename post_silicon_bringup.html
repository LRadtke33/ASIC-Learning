Post-Silicon Bring-Up, Test & Characterization

After tapeout, the first job is not a feature demo. It is to prove that the die is powered, clocked, held out of reset, and reachable for test — and only then to run the same functional checks you already wrote.

Where this sits. Lesson 70 is the path to a GDS and a shuttle. Lesson 84 is the philosophy of mixed-signal bring-up: contracts, sequencing, and brownout. This lesson is digital test practice: wafer sort versus final test, automatic test equipment (ATE), shmoo plots, a debug order, and current-signature checks. Caravel housekeeping-SPI and the Tiny Tapeout demo board with microcotb are the SKY130 anchors. They are described from official documentation. This course has not sat at a tester, has not run microcotb on a board, and has not captured a shmoo from silicon; those figures are teaching diagrams.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish wafer sort from final test, and say what ATE is for.
  • Read a shmoo of VDD versus frequency, and what a wall or a hole in it is telling you.
  • Follow a debug order: power, then clocks, then reset, then scan, then functional.
  • Use Caravel’s housekeeping SPI and Tiny Tapeout’s microcotb as concrete bring-up handles, without treating a document as a run.

1. Two test insertions, and a machine that runs them

Wafer sort (also called wafer probe, or electrical wafer sort) contacts the die while it is still in the wafer, through a probe card on the pads or bumps. It screens gross failures early, before package cost is spent, and it is usually a shorter pattern than the final test. Probe resistance, needle inductance, and the limited number of probes shape what you can even ask at this insertion — high-current or high-frequency tests may be deferred.

Final test is after assembly: the packaged part in a socket (or, for some CSPs, a contact set) on a load board. You now have the package parasitics of lesson 69 in the path, a more complete thermal environment, and typically a richer test list — speed grading, I/O levels, IDDQ or current signatures, and any trim or calibration you kept for production. Parts that pass sort can still fail final test, and the opposite is not a luxury you get: a die that failed sort was never packaged.

ATE (automatic test equipment) is the programmed tester: a timing-accurate pin electronics card per channel, digitally controlled supplies, a pattern memory, and a computer that logs pass, fail, and bin. A characterization lab bench — a board, a supply, a function generator, a scope — is the right tool for a first bring-up and for tests the ATE program does not yet know how to ask. It is not a substitute for a production program that has to finish in seconds and bin consistently.

2. A shmoo is a map of pass and fail

A shmoo plot sweeps two (sometimes more) parameters and records pass or fail at each point. The classic digital pair is core VDD versus clock frequency, for a fixed pattern, temperature, and I/O level. The passing region should be a solid block with a clean wall: as frequency rises, you eventually fail setup; as VDD falls, delay grows and the wall moves in. A hole inside the passing region, a ragged edge, or a wall that moves when you change only the pattern, is a clue — IR drop on a noisy vector, a hold path that prefers a fast corner, a tester-timing artifact — not a number to decorate a datasheet with.

Check your understanding — the part passes at 1.80 V, 50 MHz and fails at 1.80 V, 80 MHz. Is that a process fail?

Not by itself. That is the expected setup wall if 80 MHz is past what this path, at this voltage, temperature, and tester timing, can do. A process or IR problem would be a surprise: passing at 80 MHz and 1.60 V but failing at 80 MHz and 1.80 V, a hole at one pattern only, or a wall that moves when you change only the supply source impedance. Read the whole shmoo, and the pattern, before you blame the wafer.

3. A debug order that does not skip the obvious

First silicon fails in boring ways more often than in interesting ones. A useful order, each step blocking the next, is:

  1. Power. Every rail (core, I/O, analog, and their returns) is at the intended voltage, in the intended sequence, with a plausible current. A short to ground, a swapped 1.8 V and 3.3 V rail, or a missing return will not be fixed by a better test pattern. Current-signature checks belong here: IDDQ-style or simply “does this vector draw what the power analysis of lesson 38 said it should, within a sane window?” A die that draws microamps when you expected milliamps may be unclocked; one that draws amps may be shorted.
  2. Clocks. The edge is present at the pin, at the intended frequency, and — if you can observe it — inside the tree. On Caravel, the startup clock is the external pin; the on-chip DCO or PLL of lesson 36 is a later, deliberate choice. A free-running ring that is enabled too fast for the management CPU is a bring-up foot-gun the housekeeping docs warn about.
  3. Reset. Released in the right domain, for long enough, and not floating. A part that “does nothing” is often still in reset, or was released before the clock ran. See lesson 39.
  4. Scan. If you invested in DFT (lesson 40, lesson 41), a flush, a chain-length check, and a small ATPG sample tell you the digital fabric is reachable before you argue about a functional protocol. A chain that is a bit short is a better first finding than a mysterious UART silence.
  5. Functional. The tests you already wrote, at a conservative voltage and frequency first, then out along the shmoo. For mixed-signal, lesson 84’s contracts still apply: do not enable a converter before its reference has settled.

4. Two SKY130 handles: Caravel SPI and Tiny Tapeout’s microcotb

Caravel. The housekeeping SPI is documented as reachable even when the management CPU is in full reset, and as the path that can enable or trim the DCO, set PLL bypass, and assert CPU reset (lesson 36, lesson 56). That makes it a bring-up instrument, not just a firmware convenience. This course has not run a housekeeping transaction against a chip or a simulation of one; the registers and warnings are from the official page already cited there.

Tiny Tapeout. The official “Getting started with the Tiny Tapeout 04+ demo board” guide says the installed SDK includes microcotb, “a miny version of cocotb that can run right on the demo boards,” so that @cocotb.test() benches written for simulation can be reused, “pretty much as-is, against the actual chip with hardware-in-the-loop.” The MicroPython SDK (RP2040 on the demo board) is what enables a project, clocks it, and exposes the same pin names you used in simulation. This lesson does not run that SDK; the claim is the project’s, and it is the reason writing cocotb tests before tapeout is not only a simulation convenience.

Check your understanding — your Tiny Tapeout tile is silent on the demo board. Where do you start?

At box 1, not at the cocotb log. Confirm the board is powering the chip, the project is the one you enabled, clock and rst_n are doing what you think, and only then rerun the same tests you used in simulation. microcotb can tell you the functional test failed; it cannot tell you a rail is at zero until you look.

Check your understanding — why is a current-signature check useful even if the part already passed a functional pattern?

A functional pass says outputs matched. A current signature says the supply draw is in the expected band. A short between two internals that is not yet exercised, a failing power-gate, or a bus that never stopped toggling can pass a thin functional pattern and still look wrong on the ammeter. It is also a cheap screen at wafer sort, where time is tight.

Key takeaways

  • Wafer sort is on the unsawn wafer; final test is on the packaged part. ATE runs both; a lab bench is for first bring-up, not for production binning.
  • A shmoo of VDD versus frequency should be a solid passing region with a setup wall. Holes and ragged edges are findings.
  • Debug in order: power, clocks, reset, scan, functional. Current signatures belong with power.
  • Caravel’s housekeeping SPI and Tiny Tapeout’s microcotb (same cocotb tests on the demo board) are the documented SKY130 handles. This lesson quotes those documents; it does not report a tester session.
