The MOSFET: SKY130’s Transistor

Every gate, flip-flop, amplifier and pad driver in this course is built from one device, the metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor. This lesson builds it from the silicon up and connects it to the four power pins on every SKY130 standard cell.

What you will learn

  • Identify the gate, source, drain and body of NMOS and PMOS transistors, and the regions they are made of.
  • Explain threshold voltage, and why the gate-to-source voltage is the controlling quantity.
  • Read a SKY130 device line such as sky130_fd_pr__nfet_01v8 w=0.65 l=0.15, and explain the VPWR, VGND, VPB and VNB pins.

Why it matters
Every cell, pad and amplifier is built from these devices; their behavior sets speed, power and accuracy.

1. Four terminals on a slab of silicon

A MOSFET (metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor) is a voltage-controlled switch that can also amplify. In an NMOS (n-channel MOSFET), two heavily doped n-type regions — the source and the drain — sit a short distance apart in p-type silicon. Above the gap is the gate, a conductor separated from the silicon by an insulating layer only a few nanometers thick, the gate oxide. The name records the original materials: metal gate, oxide insulator, semiconductor. SKY130, like most modern processes, uses doped polysilicon for the gate.

The fourth terminal is the body (also called bulk): the silicon under the gate, connected through a heavily doped tap. Diagrams often leave the body out, but the transistor depends on it.

Teaching model with an assumed 0.5 V threshold. Real SKY130 thresholds depend on device type, size, temperature and process corner.

Two dimensions describe the device. The channel length L is the source-to-drain distance under the gate. The channel width W is how far the channel extends into the page. To first order, the current a transistor can carry grows with W/L: wider conducts more, longer conducts less.

2. The gate voltage creates the channel

With the gate at the same voltage as the source, the p-type region between source and drain blocks current: the source and drain each form a p–n junction with the body, and those junctions face each other. Raising the gate voltage attracts electrons toward the surface under the oxide. Above the threshold voltage (Vth), enough electrons gather to form a thin conducting n-type layer — the channel — joining source to drain. A drain-to-source voltage (VDS) then drives current through it.

What matters is the voltage between gate and source, VGS, not the gate voltage measured from ground. An NMOS with its source at 0 V and its gate at 1.8 V is strongly on. The same transistor with its source at 1.5 V and its gate at 1.8 V has VGS = 0.3 V and barely conducts. That is why an NMOS used as a pass switch cannot deliver a full high level (lesson 10).

Below threshold the current is small but not zero. This subthreshold current rises exponentially with VGS. In digital logic it is “leakage” that drains a battery; in low-power analog design it can be a deliberate operating region (lesson 72).

The gate draws no steady current, because it is insulated. It is, however, a capacitor: the gate plate, the oxide, and the channel underneath. Every time an input switches, that capacitance must be charged or discharged. That is the physical origin of a cell’s input capacitance — 2.302 fF for the A pin of inv_1 in the typical-corner Liberty file, the library’s characterization data (lesson 21).

3. NMOS and PMOS are complements

A PMOS (p-channel MOSFET) swaps every type: p+ source and drain in n-type silicon. It turns on when its gate is below its source by more than the threshold magnitude, which gathers holes into a p-type channel. Holes move more slowly than electrons, so a PMOS must be wider than an NMOS to carry the same current. In inv_1 the PMOS is 1.0 µm wide and the NMOS 0.65 µm (lesson 11).

CMOS (complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor) technology builds both types on one die and pairs them so that, in a settled logic state, one network is on while the other is off. The SKY130 wafer is p-type, so each NMOS sits directly in the substrate. Each PMOS needs an n-type region of its own: an n-well.

4. Why every SKY130 cell has four power pins

Each body must be held at a fixed voltage. If it floats, the threshold shifts, and the p–n junctions between diffusion, well and substrate can become forward-biased. In ordinary use the p-substrate (the NMOS body) sits at ground and the n-well (the PMOS body) at the positive supply, so every junction stays reverse biased. SKY130 standard cells expose these as separate pins:

PinConnects toNormally tied to
VPWRPMOS source side: the positive supply railVDD (1.8 V for the HD library)
VGNDNMOS source side: the ground railVSS (0 V)
VPBPMOS body, the n-wellVPWR
VNBNMOS body, the p-substrateVGND

The inv_1 SPICE netlist bundled with lab 1 is the evidence. SPICE (Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis) is the standard format for transistor-level circuits. Its subcircuit pins are A VGND VNB VPB VPWR Y, and its two transistors are:

X0 VGND A Y VNB sky130_fd_pr__nfet_01v8     w=650000u l=150000u
X1 VPWR A Y VPB sky130_fd_pr__pfet_01v8_hvt w=1e+06u  l=150000u

Each line lists the device’s drain, gate, source and body, then its model and size. The NMOS body is VNB and the PMOS body is VPB. The sizes are written in the netlist’s internal units: the lab benches set .option scale=1u, so w=650000u reads as 0.65 µm and l=150000u as 0.15 µm. Source and drain of a MOSFET are physically interchangeable; for the NMOS, whichever terminal is at the lower voltage acts as the source.

Two cautions. Separate body pins exist so that special cells and flows can bias wells differently; in ordinary digital rows the tap cells make the connection (lesson 48). And trust the SPICE netlist for the physical wiring: the bundled Liberty file’s pg_type labels for these two pins read the other way round (VNB marked “nwell”, VPB “pwell”), so do not infer the well type from that label.

Thin-oxide 1.8 V devices form the core logic. Thick-oxide devices tolerate higher voltages and appear in I/O cells, level shifters and some analog circuits (lesson 54). Check the PDK’s device details for each device’s actual operating limits rather than reading them from the name. One point of wording: the SkyWater library comparison table lists the HD PMOS as pfet_01v8. The HD cell netlists, including inv_1 and the clock buffers in lesson 35, instantiate pfet_01v8_hvt. This course follows the netlists.

6. Two views of one device

Digital design treats the MOSFET as a switch with an on-resistance, which is enough to explain logic gates (lesson 10) and delay (lesson 11). Analog design treats the same device as a voltage-controlled current source biased at an operating point, where small changes in VGS produce proportional changes in current (lesson 72). Both views describe the same silicon; they answer different questions.

7. Worked example: how much of a cell is transistor?

The inv_1 NMOS gate area is W × L = 0.65 × 0.15 = 0.0975 µm². The PMOS is 1.0 × 0.15 = 0.15 µm². Together that is 0.2475 µm², against a cell area of 3.7536 µm² — about 6.6%. Most of a standard cell is contacts, spacing, wells, taps, wiring and supply rails. Layout rules, not transistor size, set much of the area of small cells.

Check your understanding — why is the PMOS body tied to VPWR rather than to VGND?

The PMOS source and drain are p+ regions inside the n-well. Holding the n-well at the highest supply keeps those p+/n-well junctions reverse-biased, so they do not conduct, and keeps the PMOS threshold at its intended value. Tied to ground, the junctions would forward-bias whenever the source rose above about 0.6 V.

Check your understanding — an NMOS has gate 1.8 V, source 0 V, drain 1.8 V. Is it on? What if the source rises to 1.5 V?

With the source at 0 V, VGS = 1.8 V: well above threshold, so it conducts strongly. With the source at 1.5 V, VGS = 0.3 V: near or below threshold, so it barely conducts. The gate voltage did not change; VGS did.

Key takeaways

  • A MOSFET has four terminals: gate, source, drain and body. The gate controls the channel through a thin oxide.
  • Above the threshold voltage, VGS forms a channel; below it, only exponentially small subthreshold current flows.
  • CMOS pairs NMOS (in the p-substrate) with PMOS (in an n-well); the PMOS is drawn wider to match current.
  • SKY130 cells expose VPWR, VGND, VPB (n-well) and VNB (substrate); the SPICE netlist is the evidence for the body wiring.
  • sky130_fd_pr__nfet_01v8 is a 1.8 V NMOS primitive; inv_1 uses W = 0.65 µm and L = 0.15 µm.
