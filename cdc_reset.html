Clock-Domain Crossings & Reset

Analog storage physics, digital information delivery, and safe reset release.

What you will learn
  • Connect a flip-flop's analog regeneration to digital clock-domain crossing failures.
  • Use a measured regenerative time constant for dfxtp_1 to compute a model MTBF, and state what that model does not cover.
  • Choose a crossing circuit for a level, an event, or a multi-bit word.
  • Explain reset recovery, removal, and synchronized reset release.
  • Separate voltage compatibility, analog signal quality, and timing-domain safety.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Designed in step 2, RTL and simulation and reviewed again before step 10, tapeout.
Tools
Simulation with Verilator or Icarus Verilog; structural crossing checks in commercial tools such as SpyGlass CDC or Questa CDC.
Why it matters
Crossing bugs pass ordinary simulation and then fail now and then in silicon.

1. An asynchronous signal can arrive at the wrong instant

A clock-domain crossing (CDC) occurs when a signal is sampled by a clock whose relationship to the signal's changes is not guaranteed by the timing model. Two clocks with the same nominal frequency can still be asynchronous. A comparator output, an external interrupt, and a signal from an independently clocked digital block all create this problem.

A flip-flop is a regenerative analog circuit. If its data changes inside a critical aperture near the sampling edge, internal nodes can spend extra time near an unstable equilibrium. The output may settle late to either logic value. This is metastability. It is not a third valid digital code, and there is no finite settling deadline that is guaranteed for every possible event. Setup and hold constraints reduce risk for synchronous paths; asynchronous arrivals inevitably sometimes approach the aperture.

Ordinary RTL simulation does not reproduce that analog trajectory. A clean simulated waveform therefore does not prove that a CDC is safe. See the Intel/Altera design recommendations, metastability chapter, and the setup/hold lesson. The FPGA vendor's quantitative device data do not characterize SKY130.

2. What two flip-flops accomplish

For a single bit that remains stable long enough, use a destination-clock synchronizer chain. The first stage may become metastable; the following stage gives it time to resolve before the rest of the destination logic observes it. Two stages are a common starting point, not a universal reliability guarantee. Some applications require more stages or purpose-characterized synchronizer cells.

Keep the stages adjacent enough to preserve resolution time; avoid combinational logic between them and prevent synthesis from duplicating or retiming the chain. Apply the implementation tool's supported synchronizer attributes and constraints. Register a digital source before the crossing where practical: combinational glitches increase the number of asynchronous transitions. These are implementation requirements as well as RTL structure.

A common fitted model has the form below. Its parameters depend on the cell, loading, voltage, temperature, and characterization method:

MTBF ≈ exp(Tres/τ) / (K · fclock · ftoggle)

Here Tres is the available resolution time, τ is a regenerative time constant, K is a fitted factor with units of time, and the two frequencies describe sampling and input activity. For a two-flop chain on one clock, Tres is taken as the destination period minus the second stage’s setup time: that is the window the first stage has, after the capturing edge, to present a valid level at the second stage’s D pin.

2b. A measured τ for sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1

The public Liberty tables used elsewhere in this course do not contain a metastability characterization. The course therefore measured one, on the published schematic netlist of sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1, with the bundled SKY130 tt models at 25 °C and 1.80 V, in ngspice 44.2. There are no layout parasitics. The load is 1.678 fF, the Liberty D-pin capacitance of the same cell, standing in for a following synchronizer stage. Clock and data 0-to-100% rises are 20 ps. Q starts at 0; D rises near the capturing edge.

The method is standard. First a binary search finds the functional boundary tm, the D-to-clock offset at which the captured result flips from 1 to 0. For this polarity and these edges, tm is −28.45 ps (D’s 50% point 28.45 ps before CLK’s 50% point). Offsets a controlled distance on the resolve-to-1 side then give a resolve time Tr, measured from CLK 50% to Q reaching 90% (and, separately, 50%) of 1.80 V. In the regenerative regime those times follow

Tr = τ · ln(K / |tD − tm|)

so a plot of Tr against ln(1/|tD − tm|) is a straight line whose slope is τ and whose intercept determines K. A least-squares fit on 20 points with Tr from 282 ps to 688 ps (well above the ordinary 190.1 ps clock-to-Q at 90%) gives:

τ44.29 ps44.23 ps
K303.6 ps189.5 ps
R²0.99900.9990

The two thresholds share τ, as they must if they differ only by a constant voltage distance along the same exponential. They do not share K, because K absorbs the extra time from mid-supply to 90%. The numbers used in the MTBF examples below are the 90% fit: the first stage has “resolved” for the second stage once Q is a valid level. The runnable deck is labs/analysis_examples/metastability/.

This characterization is schematic, typical-corner, one polarity, one voltage, one temperature, no mismatch and no extracted parasitics. τ grows at lower supply and usually at high temperature; a real chip will not match 44.29 ps. The numbers below are a model MTBF under those conditions, useful for seeing how Tres and τ enter the exponent, not a reliability limit.

Worked MTBF examples, using τ = 44.29 ps, K = 303.6 ps, and Tres = Tclk − 50.83 ps. That 50.83 ps is the Liberty D-rise setup of dfxtp_1 at 0.01 ns edges in tt_025C_1v80 — a documented constraint, not re-measured at the 20 ps simulation edges. The last row is why the second flop exists. The same cell, the same τ and K, and the same 50 MHz clock give a model MTBF of a tenth of a second if only 400 ps of resolution is allowed, and a model MTBF of 10183 years if the full period (minus setup) is allowed. Adding 200 ps of resolution multiplies this model MTBF by exp(200 ps / 44.29 ps) ≈ 91.5. One extra FO4 of resolution, 72.15 ps from the delay-physics lesson, multiplies it by about 5.1. The exponent is unforgiving in both directions: a small loss of Tres, or a larger τ at a worse corner, moves the result by orders of magnitude.

3. A synchronizer can safely miss an event

Metastability containment and information delivery are separate requirements. A 2 ns pulse can fit completely between the sampling edges of a 100 MHz destination. No first-stage sampling edge sees it. Adding a second stage does not recover a pulse that was never captured.

InformationSuitable starting architectureWhat must still be checked
Persistent status levelSingle-bit synchronizerLatency, activity rate, resolution time, initialization
Occasional eventHeld request with acknowledgment, or a toggle protocolDo not overwrite an outstanding event; toggle changes must not outrun observation
Configuration wordStable data plus synchronized request/acknowledgmentData stability through capture and acknowledgment; reset consistency
Continuous word streamAsynchronous FIFO (first-in, first-out buffer)Pointer synchronization, full/empty logic, overflow/underflow, physical bus constraints

Stretching a pulse can work when the minimum width and maximum destination period are guaranteed with margin. An open-loop toggle also needs a proven event-rate bound. A handshake provides backpressure, at the cost of round-trip latency. An asynchronous FIFO typically transfers Gray-coded pointers so adjacent pointer states change one bit; its data storage and pointer protocol work together. Gray encoding alone does not make an arbitrary bus safe: route-delay/skew constraints and the architecture's assumptions must hold.

The original Cummings CDC paper (hosted copy) develops these distinctions and the corresponding verification techniques.

4. Never synchronize a changing binary word bit by bit

Consider a 4-bit counter changing from 0111 to 1000. Four independently synchronized bits can represent different source instants. The destination might temporarily see 0000, 1111, or another incoherent combination even when every bit has resolved to a valid voltage. A two-flop chain per bit addresses the wrong problem.

For a configuration update, the source can latch the complete word, hold it stable, and send a request through a synchronizer. The destination captures the stable word only after the protocol permits it, then returns an acknowledgment through a source-domain synchronizer. The physical data path must settle before the capture allowed by that protocol. Include this timing requirement in constraints; the handshake is not permission to ignore routing delays.

5. Reset release is another asynchronous crossing

An asynchronous reset can assert without a running clock, but releasing it near an active clock edge can violate recovery or removal. Recovery is analogous to the required time before an active edge; removal is analogous to the required time after it. Their characterized values are signed timing constraints, not universal positive constants. Reset minimum pulse width is a separate check.

A common architecture asynchronously asserts a small per-domain reset synchronizer and shifts a logic one through it after the external reset is released. Its final output releases the domain reset following destination-clock edges. Nominally, a two-stage chain releases after two edges; an arrival near an edge can delay this further. The reset tree still needs timing analysis so its loads meet recovery/removal and pulse-width requirements. Use one controlled release for related logic in each domain rather than independently synchronizing resets into every register.

The SKY130 HD dfxtp used in the timing lesson has no asynchronous reset. The dfrtp cell has an active-low RESET_B clear input and publishes recovery, removal, and minimum-pulse-width tables. At the typical corner tt_025C_1v80, the first cells of those tables (clock slew and reset slew both 0.01 ns — table cells, not an interpolation) are:

Constraint on dfrtp_1 · RESET_BValue at 0.01 ns / 0.01 ns
Recovery, rising clock (rise_constraint)−0.2287 ns
Removal, rising clock (rise_constraint)0.3011 ns

Recovery is negative at this cell and this corner: the characterized “must-be-stable-before-the-edge” time can have either sign, which is why these are signed constraints and not universal positive constants. The raw first cells are recovery −0.2287129 ns, removal 0.3010723 ns, and minimum pulse width 0.1807697 ns. Check the actual Liberty data for any other corner or slew; do not substitute ordinary D setup/hold values for reset timing.

Synchronous-reset architectures are also valid when their clock and pulse-capture requirements are satisfied. Choose deliberately. Hold release until supplies and required clocks are valid; a stopped destination clock cannot complete a synchronous release. Resetting one side of a live handshake or FIFO can destroy protocol state, so define how traffic is drained, flushed, or restarted. See Cummings, Mills, and Golson's reset paper.

6. The analog-to-digital boundary

A comparator's output is a voltage transition with propagation delay, noise sensitivity, and possibly repeated transitions near threshold. Check logic levels, rise/fall slew, powered-off behavior, and whether translation or isolation is required. Hysteresis or analog filtering may reduce chatter, but neither establishes a synchronous clock relationship.

If the output represents a lasting alarm, synchronize the level. If it represents a short zero-crossing event, design event capture and specify timestamp uncertainty. A synchronizer cannot preserve an exact analog crossing time or count arbitrarily fast chatter. A sampled-data converter's data-ready signal and result word need a coordinated word-transfer protocol; see converter interfaces.

7. Verification and a solved checkpoint

Review CDC structure, protocol behavior, reset-domain crossings, and physical constraints. Simulate different clock ratios, phases, stalls, and reset sequences; assertions or formal checks can prove protocol properties under stated assumptions. Place timing exceptions narrowly: the asynchronous launch into the first stage may be excepted as appropriate, but the destination-clock path between synchronizer stages must remain timed and physically controlled. Neither a blanket false path nor an RTL simulation proves a metastability MTBF (mean time between failures).

Checkpoint: a 4-bit ADC result and a 2 ns ready pulse cross into a 100 MHz controller. Are five two-flop chains sufficient?

No. The ready pulse can be missed between 10 ns sampling edges, and independent bit synchronization does not preserve the word. Latch the result in its source domain, hold a request until a synchronized acknowledgment returns, and hold the data throughout the capture protocol. Use an asynchronous FIFO if throughput or buffering requires it. Separately verify voltage compatibility, reset behavior, and synchronizer reliability.

Check your understanding — why must an asynchronous reset be released synchronously in each clock domain?

Reset release is itself an asynchronous event. If it lands near a clock edge, some flops can leave reset a cycle before others, or go metastable, so the block starts in an inconsistent state. A reset synchronizer in each domain still asserts reset immediately but releases it on a defined clock edge.

In the open SKY130 flow. Ordinary simulation rarely exposes crossing bugs, because it does not model metastability. Commercial flows add structural checkers — Synopsys SpyGlass CDC, Siemens Questa CDC, Cadence Conformal — that find every crossing and compare it with known-safe structures. Open flows rely on design review, lint, and simulations that vary the phase between clocks.

Key takeaways

  • Metastability is an analog property of storage elements; extra settling time reduces its propagation probability.
  • On schematic dfxtp_1 at tt, 25 °C, 1.80 V, a Method A fit gives τ = 44.29 ps and K = 303.6 ps (R² = 0.999). That is not a silicon MTBF.
  • In that model a two-flop chain at 50 MHz has an enormous MTBF; the same parameters with only 400 ps of resolution give 0.11 s. The exponent is the design lever.
  • A synchronizer's reliability and a protocol's ability to deliver information are different questions.
  • Use coherent protocols for multi-bit data and bounded-rate or acknowledged protocols for events.
  • Asynchronous reset release requires recovery/removal analysis and a deliberate per-domain architecture.
  • Comparator conditioning, voltage translation, CDC, and reset sequencing each solve different interface requirements.
