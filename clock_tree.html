Lesson 35 / 84 · Part V, Digital implementation

The Clock Tree

Synchronous flip-flops need a controlled relationship between their clock edges. A large sink load usually needs a clock distribution network. A tree of clock buffers does, and this library has cells built for that tree.

What you will learn

  • Explain why a large set of flip-flops needs a tree of clock buffers rather than one driver.
  • Describe what clock-tree synthesis does, and how timing changes from an ideal to a propagated clock.
  • Separate insertion delay from skew, see how skew moves setup and hold slack, and estimate clock power.
  • Read a real LibreLane CTS result: buffer count and type, insertion delay, network skew, and post-CTS hold repair.

Why it matters
The clock reaches every flip-flop, so its skew, delay and power affect every path.

Physical sizes and typical pin capacitances below were checked against the pinned SKY130 HD LEF and Liberty files. The configuration example is the current open_pdks LibreLane HD config, read on 6 November 2026; flow defaults can be overridden by the design.

1. One clock source, many clock pins

A flip-flop samples around its active edge. Define skew as capture clock arrival − launch clock arrival. Positive skew helps a single-cycle setup check and hurts the corresponding hold check. Clock-tree synthesis distributes the clock while controlling load, transition, insertion delay and skew; the goal is a timing budget that closes, not mathematically identical arrival at every pin.

The cells are still one row tall. They sit on unithd like inv_1. They are not a new row height. What changes is the input capacitance and how much load the output is allowed to drive (lesson 11, lesson 21).

2. clkbuf is a buffer. clkinv is an inverter.

clkbuf has output X with function (A); clkinv has output Y with function (!A). The checked LibreLane config selects CTS_ROOT_BUFFER = sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_16 and CTS_CLK_BUFFERS = sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_*. That wildcard does not exclude the root size. These are defaults, not mandatory process rules.

The library’s clkbuf_16 output maximum is 1.531687 pF. The checked config instead lists a project-overridable MAX_CAPACITANCE_CONSTRAINT of 0.2 pF, MAX_FANOUT_CONSTRAINT of 10, and MAX_TRANSITION_CONSTRAINT of 0.75 ns. The effective rules depend on how the flow applies these constraints; a large cell-level maximum is not permission to ignore tighter design constraints or slew.

3. The leaf is a clock pin, 1.794 fF

Using only the published lumped pin cap, 853 × 1.794 fF = 1.530282 pF, just below the clkbuf_16 library maximum; 854 exceeds it. This is a unit-checking exercise, not a legal fanout recommendation. Wire capacitance, slew, fanout policy, skew, power and the design’s tighter capacitance limits can require much smaller branches. Real CTS characterizes buffer behavior and interconnect rather than choosing a sink count by capacitance alone.

Areas at the typical corner match width times 2.72: clkbuf_16 is 25.024 µm², clkinv_16 is 30.0288 µm². Leakage of clkbuf_16 is 0.01232311 nW. clkinv_16 is 0.0114109 nW. Their delay and slew tables are in the same Liberty files. A clock buffer still has slew and load, the same two axes as lesson 23.

The cell SPICE netlists show how the strengths are built. Every clkbuf is two inverters in series. In clkbuf_2 to clkbuf_16 each device is a 0.42 µm NMOS or a 1.0 µm pfet_01v8_hvt, all at L = 0.15 µm, and the strength is the number of parallel copies. clkbuf_16 has 40 transistors: a first stage of 4 parallel pairs driving an output stage of 16 parallel pairs, a fan-up of 4 inside the cell. clkbuf_1 is the exception, with one 0.52/0.79 µm pair per stage.

The clkdlybuf4s family is four inverters in a row. The first and last stages are normal 0.15 µm devices. The two inner stages use the channel length in the cell name: 0.15, 0.18, 0.25 or 0.50 µm. A longer channel gives less current for the same width, so the delay grows while the area, the input pin and the output stage stay the same. That is why these cells are useful for adding a controlled delay to a clock branch or a short data path. Hold fixing with them is covered in lesson 42.

Analog readers should treat the tree as a set of large inverters and buffers switching together, on a net that runs past quiet signals. The per-micrometer capacitance in lesson 28 is a wire alone. A clock neighbor is extra. Digital readers should treat skew as a piece of the period, and the max capacitance as a limit, not a target.

4. Skew compares locations. Jitter compares edge timing.

Skew is two clock pins seeing one edge at two times. The path between those flops then has a period that is short or long by that difference. Jitter is one clock pin seeing its own edge move from cycle to cycle. A setup check that already spent the whole period feels jitter as a smaller period.

Skew compares the arrivals of the same logical edge at different locations. Jitter is timing variation relative to an ideal reference; period jitter and cycle-to-cycle jitter are different measurements. Common jitter can correlate between launch and capture, so timing uncertainty must reflect the actual clock relationship. An ideal H-tree illustrates equal geometric lengths, but unequal loads, process variation and obstacles still affect delay. Figure 1 is a generic buffer-tree sketch, not evidence of an implemented topology.

5. The clock also draws analog current

For an illustrative 1 pF total switched clock load at 1.8 V and 100 MHz, P ≈ CV²f = 0.324 mW, excluding buffer internal and leakage power. A clock has one 0→1 transition per cycle; this convention avoids a factor-of-two error. Current arrives in short edge pulses and flows through the shared supply and return network. Shielding, return paths, local decoupling and placement matter to nearby references and sampling circuits.

Checkpoint: can a clock gate be an arbitrary AND gate?

No. Changing enable while the clock is active can create a runt pulse. Use a characterized clock-gating structure and verify its enable timing, test bypass, minimum pulse widths and CTS treatment.

6. Why a tree: one buffer cannot drive every flip-flop

Each dfxtp_1 clock pin is 1.794 fF. The configuration’s root buffer, clkbuf_16, is limited to 1.531687 pF of output load, which is about 853 bare clock pins with no wire at all. A design with 4,096 flip-flops has 7.35 pF on its clock pins alone, before any wire. Even if one buffer could drive it, the edge would be slow, and a slow clock edge stretches every clock-to-Q delay and setup time (lesson 25).

A tree divides the load. Each buffer drives only a few dozen sinks or further buffers, so every edge stays sharp. With a fanout of f per buffer, N sinks need ⌈logf N⌉ levels: 4,096 sinks at a fanout of 8 need four levels (8⁴ = 4,096); at a fanout of 16, three (16³ = 4,096).

7. Clock-tree synthesis builds and balances the tree

Clock-tree synthesis runs after placement, once the flip-flops have positions. Its inputs are the clock definitions from the timing constraints (lesson 34), the sink locations, the cells it may use — the clkbuf and clkinv families here — and targets for maximum slew, capacitance and skew. It clusters nearby sinks, inserts buffers, and adjusts branches so that every sink sees nearly the same delay. The H-tree in Figure 2 is the geometric ideal it approximates; in the open SKY130 flow, OpenROAD’s clock-tree synthesis does this work.

Before this step the timer uses an ideal clock: every flop receives the edge at the same instant. Afterward it uses a propagated clock, with the real buffer and wire delays to each sink, which is when skew starts to affect slack.

In the open SKY130 flow. LibreLane’s CTS step calls OpenROAD’s clock_tree_synthesis with the buffer list from the PDK configuration; ResizerTimingPostCTS then repairs setup and hold using propagated clocks. Innovus (CCOpt) and IC Compiler II do the same job in commercial flows.

clock_tree_synthesis -root_buf sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_16 \
    -buf_list {sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_8 sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_4 sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_2} \
    -sink_clustering_enable -apply_ndr half
set_propagated_clock [all_clocks]
repair_timing -hold -hold_margin 0.10 -setup_margin 0.05

That is the command LibreLane 3.0.17 actually issued on the run in section 8, including the 0.10 ns hold margin on the post-CTS repair. The buffer list is taken from the PDK/flow configuration and can change with the LibreLane version; the section 8 result shows which cells were really inserted.

8. A measured result: 64 sinks, nine clkbuf_16 cells

On 11 November 2026 this course ran LibreLane 3.0.17 on the bundled 32-bit serial/parallel multiplier example (spm), with PDK sky130A (ciel revision 8afc8346a57fe1ab7934ba5a6056ea8b43078e71), library sky130_fd_sc_hd, and a 10 ns clock period, stopping after OpenROAD.ResizerTimingPostCTS. The reports are in labs/analysis_examples/cts_spm/. This is mid-place-and-route: placement-based estimated parasitics, not routed, and not a signoff result.

TritonCTS built an H-tree of three levels for one clock net with 64 sinks. It was allowed clkbuf_2, clkbuf_4 and clkbuf_8 as intermediate buffers and clkbuf_16 as the root, and it inserted nine sky130_fd_sc_hd__clkbuf_16 cells — the root plus eight leaves — and nothing from the smaller list. Every clock path has the same depth: two buffers. The eight leaf nets each have fanout 8. Average sink wire length was 103.41 µm. Clock-buffer area is 225.216 µm². No leaf-level extra buffers and no dummy loads were added.

Two different “skew” numbers appear in the same STA view and must not be mixed up. The network skew, 38.8 ps rise, is the difference between the fastest and slowest clock arrivals — the tree imbalance. The report’s “setup skew” of 0.268 ns is that network term plus the 0.250 ns clock uncertainty from the SDC, minus 21 ps of CRPR (clock reconvergence pessimism removal). Uncertainty and CRPR belong to the timing check, not to the tree’s geometry. The 10 ns period is an easy target for this small block, which is why setup slack is several nanoseconds; the useful facts here are the insertion delay, the network skew, and that a balanced two-level tree of clkbuf_16 was enough for 64 sinks.

9. Insertion delay, skew and hold fixing

Insertion delay (clock latency) is the time from the clock source to a sink; skew is the difference between two sinks. On the section 8 run those were 0.335–0.374 ns of rise insertion and 38.8 ps of rise network skew, with two clkbuf_16 cells on every path — a measured pair, not an assumed 0.15 ns per level. Insertion delay is shared by the launching and capturing flops of a same-clock path, so it largely cancels. Skew does not.

If the capturing flop’s clock arrives 30 ps later than the launching flop’s, setup slack improves by 30 ps and hold slack worsens by 30 ps (lesson 26). Short paths that fail hold after clock-tree synthesis are fixed by adding delay to the data path — hold fixing — not by slowing the clock. Where a clock leaves the chip or meets another domain, insertion delay no longer cancels and must be budgeted (lesson 39).

10. The clock tree’s power is C·V²·f

The clock toggles every cycle, which makes its network the most active capacitance on the chip. Its switching power is P = C·V²·f. For the 4,096-flop example: 4,096 × 1.794 fF = 7.35 pF of clock pins; assume 5 pF more for wires and buffer inputs, about 12.4 pF in total. At 1.8 V and 50 MHz, P = 12.4 pF × (1.8 V)² × 50 MHz ≈ 2.0 mW — before the flops’ own internal clock power. That is why clock gating targets the tree (lesson 37).

Check your understanding — a design has 2,000 flip-flops, and clock-tree synthesis limits each buffer to a fanout of 10. What is the minimum number of buffer levels?

10³ = 1,000 is too few and 10⁴ = 10,000 is enough, so at least four levels. Real trees can differ, because wire load and placement, not only sink count, limit each buffer.

Check your understanding — timing was clean with an ideal clock; after clock-tree synthesis, hold violations appear. Why?

With an ideal clock every flop sees the edge at the same instant. After clock-tree synthesis real skew appears; where a capturing flop’s clock arrives later than its launching flop’s, a short data path can change the captured value too early. The fix is delay on those data paths, or rebalancing the tree.

Lesson 42 shows what insertion delay and skew do to the setup and hold slack of one register-to-register path.

The source of the edge — a pad, a ring oscillator, a DCO, or a PLL — is lesson 36. This lesson is the tree that distributes it.

Key takeaways

  • A clock tree is buffers between one source and many CLK pins, so the edge arrives together and the load stays legal.
  • clkbuf is function (A). clkinv is function (!A). Both are one row tall.
  • The checked config’s root default is clkbuf_16, 20 sites, input 7.397 fF, output max 1.531687 pF.
  • 853 dfxtp_1 clock pins are 1.530 pF as arithmetic only. Fanout, slew, wire capacitance, and design constraints impose much tighter practical limits.
  • Input cap is not a clean step with the drive number. clkbuf_2 is 2.180 fF and clkbuf_4 is 2.106 fF.
  • Skew compares clock arrival at two sinks. Jitter describes edge-time variation relative to a specified timing reference. The implemented topology must be checked in the design.
  • One clkbuf_16 can drive at most about 853 bare dfxtp_1 clock pins; a real tree needs ⌈logf N⌉ levels.
  • Clock-tree synthesis builds and balances the tree after placement; timing then uses propagated clock arrivals.
  • On the course’s LibreLane 3.0.17 spm run (64 sinks, 10 ns period, mid-PnR), TritonCTS inserted nine clkbuf_16 cells at path depth 2; rise insertion was 0.335–0.374 ns and rise network skew was 38.8 ps. That is not a signoff result.
  • Skew moves setup and hold slack in opposite directions; hold is fixed with data-path delay, not a slower clock.
  • Clock power is C·V²·f on the most active net: about 2 mW for 4,096 flops at 50 MHz in the worked example.
