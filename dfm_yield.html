DFM, Variation & Yield: A Digital View

A correct GDS can still yield poorly. Systematic variation, random variation, lithography hotspots and via opens are different problems, and they enter the cost of a chip in different ways.

Where this sits. Lesson 2 already has yield arithmetic (die per wafer, a simple yield model). Lesson 75 is analog mismatch (Pelgrom, with a PDK Vth slope). Lesson 45 is digital corners. This lesson is the digital-view companion: when corners are enough, when Monte Carlo is the right question, what a litho hotspot and a redundant via are for, and how yield shows up in cost. Figures are teaching unless a number is tied to a file we already cited.

What you will learn

  • Separate systematic from random variation, and corners from Monte Carlo, for digital logic.
  • Say what a lithography hotspot and a redundant via are doing for yield.
  • Connect yield to die cost without inventing a SKY130 yield percentage.

1. Systematic and random are not the same knob

Systematic variation is correlated across a region or a layout pattern: a dense-versus-sparse lithography bias, a CMP dishing in a wide metal, a gradient across the wafer, a well-proximity effect. You fight it with design rules, dummy fill (lesson 61), matching orientation, and by not asking a 1 µm device to sit next to a 100 µm empty field and expect the same printed width.

Random variation is uncorrelated from device to device: dopant fluctuation, line-edge roughness, a via that does or does not open. You fight it with area (Pelgrom, lesson 75), with redundancy, and with statistical margin.

Digital STA’s usual answer is corners: a slow cell library and a worst RC, or a fast library and a hold-flavoured RC, as in lesson 45. A corner is a pessimistic simultaneous extreme. It is good at “does any path still close if everything is slow,” and bad at “what fraction of dice fail,” because real chips are not all-slow at once on every device. Monte Carlo draws from a statistical model and counts failures. It is the right question for an analog offset or a yield target; it is expensive to run on a full digital STA, and this course has not run a digital MC on the SPM.

Check your understanding — a path fails ss 125 °C by 5 ps. Is the yield zero?

Not automatically. ss 125 °C is a simultaneous slow-cell, high-temperature extreme. Many dice will be closer to typical. The fail does mean you do not have corner closure, which is the usual digital signoff contract. Yield is a different, statistical claim, and this course has not measured one on the SPM.

2. Litho hotspots and redundant vias

A lithography hotspot is a layout pattern that the optical process prints poorly: a pinch, a bridge, a line-end that pulls back. Foundry recommended rules and, at advanced nodes, litho-simulation checks, exist to catch these before tapeout. SKY130’s DRC deck already encodes a large part of that experience as spacings and densities; a “passed DRC” is necessary and not always a complete litho signoff on a leading-edge node. This course’s Lab 7 is DRC/LVS practice, not a litho-simulator run.

A redundant via is a second (or third) cut on the same connection, so that a single via open does not kill the net. Yield models often treat via opens as a dominant random fail. The cost is a little extra metal and congestion; the benefit is on every via in the design, which is why routers have a “multi-cut via” effort. It is a DFM knob, not a timing knob — though a double via also lowers resistance, which is a small IR and delay gift.

3. Yield is a cost number

Lesson 2’s arithmetic still holds: cost per good die is (wafer cost) / (dice per wafer × yield). A DFM rule that costs 2 % area and buys 5 % yield can be a bargain; one that costs 10 % area for a negligible fail rate is not. You cannot do that sum without a yield model and a fail-rate for the rule, and this course does not invent a SKY130 foundry yield. What you can say is that via redundancy, fill, recommended rules, and not packing a litho-hostile pattern are the usual cheap knobs, and that a shuttle’s “it DRCed” is the start of that conversation, not the end.

Check your understanding — why isn’t a 100 % DRC-clean GDS a 100 % yield?

DRC is a rule check, not a process. Random via opens, particles, and parametric tails (a Vth in the far end of the distribution) are not all written as DRC errors. Systematic litho issues at the edge of the rules can still print badly. Packaging and test then remove more dice. Yield is measured on silicon, and improved by DFM, margin, and process control together.

Key takeaways

  • Systematic variation is patterned or graded; random variation is device-to-device. Corners ask a simultaneous extreme; Monte Carlo asks a distribution. Digital signoff is usually corners; yield is a count.
  • Litho hotspots and via opens are classic DFM targets. Redundant vias are a standard, cheap defence. This course has not run a litho simulator.
  • Yield enters cost through lesson 2’s arithmetic. No SKY130 yield percentage is quoted here.
