A Timed Logic Path: Registers, Clocks, Scan & Speed/Power

One SKY130 register-to-register path, timed by hand the way a static timer does it: clock latency at both flops, a scan multiplexer in front of each, slew carried stage to stage, then setup and hold. The second half asks what it costs in power to make that path faster.

Where the numbers come from. Every cell delay, slew and constraint is a lookup in the HD tt_025C_1v80 Liberty file with the slew propagated from stage to stage (labs/analysis_examples/delay_physics/liberty_path.py). The clock period, clock slew, the two clock latencies and the 20 µm of met1 on every net are illustrative assumptions, chosen to look like a small block after clock-tree synthesis; they do not come from a routed design. Energy, delay and leakage against supply voltage and size are ngspice runs of the bundled SKY130 tt models, as in lesson 29.

What you will learn

  • Draw a complete register-to-register path with its clock tree, scan multiplexers and scan chain, and say which pieces belong to which timing check.
  • Propagate arrival and slew through a SKY130 path and compute setup and hold slack from the Liberty tables.
  • Explain how clock skew moves setup and hold in opposite directions, and why the scan shift path is the first to fail hold.
  • Write power as αCV²f plus leakage and estimate each for a SKY130 gate.
  • Compare the speed and power cost of supply voltage, sizing, threshold voltage, library choice and pipelining.

1. The path, drawn completely

Lesson 26 timed a flop, one inverter and a flop with ideal clocks. A real path has three more things around it. The clocks arrive late, and not equally late, because they come through a buffer tree (lesson 35). Each flop is a scan flop with a multiplexer in front of its D input (lesson 40). And the flops are also wired into a scan chain that bypasses the logic entirely. Figure 1 draws all three.

2. Clocks: latency, insertion delay and skew

The clock leaves its pin at time 0 by definition. The clock latency to a flop, also called insertion delay, is the time it takes to get through the clock tree to that flop’s CLK pin. Here the launch flop sees its edge at 0.420 ns and the capture flop at 0.470 ns. The difference, capture minus launch, is the clock skew of this pair: +50 ps.

Latency itself does not hurt a path between two flops on the same tree, because it shifts both ends. Only the difference matters. That is why clock-tree synthesis aims to balance latencies rather than minimize them, and why a timer reports “clock network delay (propagated)” once real buffers exist. Before CTS the timer uses ideal clocks, so hold slack before CTS means very little.

The clock slew matters separately. The flop’s clock-to-Q delay and its setup and hold constraints are all tables indexed by the CLK transition. A clock net with a slow edge makes every flop on it slower and less predictable.

3. Arrival and slew, stage by stage

A static timer walks the path from the launch clock pin, adding one cell delay at a time. Each lookup needs two inputs: the load on the cell’s output (pins plus wire) and the transition on its input, which is the previous stage’s output slew. Three things stand out. Clock-to-Q is the largest delay in the path by far: 330 ps against 257 ps for all four logic gates together. The same cell can be quick or slow depending on the edge: nor2_1 takes 42 ps falling and 97 ps rising, because its rising output goes through two series PMOS devices. And buf_2 is slower than the single-stage gates even with a light load: it is two inverters inside one cell. The rising nor2 output also hands buf_2 a 89 ps edge, which is the slew propagation that lesson 26 warned about.

4. The setup check

Setup asks whether data launched by one clock edge reaches the capture flop early enough before the next edge. With the notation of lesson 26:

setup slack = (T + Tcapture − Tsetup − U) − (Tlaunch + TCQ + Tlogic)

For the Q-rising case: the data arrives at 0.420 + 0.330 + 0.257 = 1.007 ns. The capture flop needs a falling D to be stable 0.187 ns before its edge, which is at 1.0 + 0.470 = 1.470 ns. The required time is 1.470 − 0.187 = 1.283 ns, and the slack is 1.283 − 1.007 = +0.276 ns. The Q-falling case arrives later (1.041 ns) but needs less setup for a rising D (0.091 ns), and its slack is +0.338 ns. The timer reports the worse one.

Setting the slack to zero gives the shortest period this one path allows: Tmin = Tlaunch + TCQ + Tlogic + Tsetup − Tcapture = 0.724 ns for the Q-rising case and 0.662 ns for Q falling, so 0.724 ns. That is nominal toy-path arithmetic only: one corner, no clock uncertainty, no on-chip variation, four gates and 20 µm wires. A real SKY130 block is timed at the slow corner with derates and uncertainty, and its worst path has many more levels of logic, so its clock is several times slower.

Check your understanding — the capture latency rises from 0.470 to 0.520 ns. What happens to both slacks?

Skew grows by 50 ps. Setup slack improves by 50 ps, to +0.326 ns, because the capture edge is later. Hold slack worsens by 50 ps, to +0.598 ns, because the same later edge must now be held against. The cell delays do not change.

5. The hold check

Hold asks the opposite question about the same edge. When the clock edge reaches the capture flop at 0.470 ns, the flop is still sampling the old data. New data launched by that same edge at the launch flop must not arrive before the capture flop has finished:

hold slack = (Tlaunch + TCQ + Tlogic) − (Tcapture + Thold + U)

The sdfxtp_1 hold constraint for a falling D is -0.111 ns: negative, so the data may change slightly before the edge. Hold slack is 1.007 − (0.470 − 0.111) = +0.648 ns. The period does not appear. A slower clock cannot fix a hold violation; only more delay in the data path, or less skew, can.

Note which numbers each check uses. Setup wants the latest data and the earliest capture clock; hold wants the earliest data and the latest capture clock. With one corner and no variation they are the same numbers here. In signoff they come from different corners and different derates, and the clock path that launch and capture share is credited back by clock reconvergence pessimism removal.

6. Scan: the same flops, a second path

Each sdfxtp_1 has a 2:1 multiplexer in front of its storage: next_state = (D & !SCE) | (SCD & SCE). In functional and capture mode SE is 0 and the flop takes D from the logic. In shift mode SE is 1 and every flop takes SCD from the previous flop’s Q, so the flops form one long shift register from SI to SO. Production test shifts a pattern in, pulses one capture clock with SE low, and shifts the response out (lesson 40).

The multiplexer sits in the data path, so scan costs setup time: about 50 to 100 ps at this corner, roughly the delay of one more gate. Clock-to-Q is unchanged, because the output side of the flop is the same. That 0.05–0.1 ns is paid on every path in a scanned design, which is why the setup numbers in section 4 used the sdfxtp_1 tables.

SE itself must meet timing. Its setup to the clock is 0.217–0.246 ns for a 0.05 ns SE edge and grows to 0.384 ns for a 0.5 ns edge. SE drives every scan flop, so its net is huge and slow; test clocks are therefore much slower than the functional clock, and SE is often pipelined or given a whole cycle to settle.

The shift path has a hold problem the functional path does not. Q of one flop goes straight to SCD of the next with only a short wire and no logic. Its hold slack is the launch latency plus clock-to-Q against the capture latency plus the SCD hold requirement: 0.420 + 0.326 − (0.470 − 0.094) = +0.370 ns for a rising Q. That is positive at this skew, but it is 0.278 ns smaller than the functional hold slack, and it vanishes if the skew between neighbors in the chain grows by about 370 ps. Scan stitching orders the chain by placement to keep neighbors close in the clock tree, and the hold fixer adds delay buffers on the scan nets where needed.

7. What skew does to all three checks

Every check above is a straight line in skew. Positive skew (capture later than launch) adds one for one to setup slack and subtracts one for one from hold slack. Figure 5 plots the three lines of this example.

Two design habits follow. Useful skew deliberately delays a capture clock to borrow time for a slow path, at the cost of hold margin on that flop and setup margin on the path that it launches. And hold fixing, which adds buffers to fast paths, is done after CTS, when the real skews are known, because before then the timer has no idea which paths are at risk.

Check your understanding — a scan chain is stitched in alphabetical order of flop names, not in placement order. Why does hold get harder?

Neighbors in the chain are now far apart on the die and on different branches of the clock tree. The skew between them can be hundreds of picoseconds in either direction, and the shift path has no logic delay to absorb it. Some links will fail hold and need delay buffers, and the long scan wires add capacitance and power. Placement-aware stitching keeps each Q → SCD link short and on nearby clock branches.

8. Where the power goes: αCV²f plus leakage

Every time a node rises from 0 to VDD the supply delivers charge CVDD, at a cost of CVDD²; half is stored on the capacitor and half is dissipated in the PMOS. When the node falls, the stored half is dissipated in the NMOS. So one full cycle of a node costs CVDD² from the supply, and the dynamic power of a design is

Pdyn = α C VDD² f

where α is the activity factor, the probability that the node makes a 0 → 1 transition in a given cycle. Clock nets have α = 1; typical logic is far lower. Two smaller terms sit beside it: short-circuit current, which flows while both transistors are partly on during a slow input edge, and leakage power, which flows all the time.

The simulated inv_1 FO4 stage draws 31.5 fJ from its supply per full cycle at 1.8 V, an effective switched capacitance of 9.74 fF: the four input gates it drives plus its own drain. At α = 0.1 and f = 100 MHz, Pdyn = 0.1 × 31.5 fJ × 100 MHz = 0.315 µW. The Liberty leakage of inv_1 at tt, 25 °C is 5.33 pW, about 59,217 times smaller. At room temperature and at this activity, a SKY130 HD gate is almost entirely dynamic power. Leakage grows steeply with temperature and at the fast corner, and it matters in blocks that sit idle most of the time, but the knob that moves most power here is α C V².

9. Supply voltage: the strongest lever

Lowering VDD cuts energy as V² and slows the gate, because the transistor overdrive VDD − Vt shrinks. Figure 6 is the simulated inv_1 FO4 stage from 1.0 to 1.8 V.

From 1.8 to 1.4 V the energy per cycle falls by 43% and the delay rises by 83%. Below about 1.2 V the delay explodes, because VDD approaches the 0.78 V NMOS threshold and the PMOS’s higher one. The energy-delay product, E × t, rewards both and has a broad minimum: 2.24 fJ·ns at 1.6 V against 2.28 at 1.8 V. E/V² is not quite constant: it rises from 8.2 to 9.7 fF because MOS capacitance varies with voltage and short-circuit current grows with supply. Leakage also falls with VDD, here by four times from 1.8 to 1.0 V.

10. Sizing: speed for energy, until the load is the next gate

Upsizing a driver lowers its effective resistance, but it adds its own drain capacitance and presents a larger input to the gate before it. Figure 7 drives a fixed 50 fF load, a long wire or a big fanout, with 1, 2, 4 and 8 inv_1 device pairs in parallel.

Into a large fixed load, speed is cheap: the 50 fF costs 162 fJ per cycle whatever drives it, and the 8× driver is 4.6 times faster for 42% more total energy. When the load is itself a gate of similar size, the picture reverses: every upsized gate raises the load on its predecessor, and timing-driven sizing tools stop upsizing once a path meets timing, then downsize everything off the critical path to recover power and leakage.

11. Threshold voltage, libraries and leakage

Below threshold, drain current falls exponentially with gate voltage. For the SKY130 NMOS the slope is about 88 mV per decade (lesson 29, Figure 4). A device with a 100 mV higher threshold therefore leaks about 14 times less, while its on current at full drive changes by only about 10%. 12. Pipelining and the meaning of fmax

Every register stage costs a fixed overhead: clock-to-Q plus setup, here 0.330 + 0.187 = 0.517 ns at tt for sdfxtp_1, plus whatever skew and uncertainty the design budgets. Take a block whose logic takes 2.0 ns (an illustrative number). Unpipelined, its period is at least 2.0 + 0.52 = 2.52 ns. Split into two 1.0 ns halves with a register between them, the period drops to 1.52 ns: 1.66 times the throughput, not 2, because the overhead is paid twice. Latency grows from one cycle (2.52 ns) to two (3.03 ns).

The power side of that trade is the extra flops and their clock. Each new sdfxtp_1 adds 1.776 fF of clock pin that switches every cycle, 5.8 fJ per cycle at 1.8 V before its internal clock energy (listed separately in Liberty’s internal_power), and 9.6 pW of leakage. A pipelined design can also spend its timing slack another way: keep the original throughput and lower VDD, trading the extra speed back for V² energy.

Finally, a caution on any “maximum frequency”. The 0.724 ns of section 4 is one path, one corner, no uncertainty. Signoff takes the worst path of millions, at the slow corner and the temperature extremes, with on-chip variation derates and clock uncertainty for jitter, and with hold met at the fast corner at the same time. The number that matters is the slack the timer reports under the constraints you actually wrote.

Check your understanding — should the example block run at 1.6 V instead of 1.8 V?

If the path still meets setup at the slower corner with the slower gates, yes for energy: the FO4 data says energy per cycle falls by 23% while gate delay rises by 28%. The flop overhead scales too, so the 0.276 ns setup slack would shrink. It needs Liberty characterized at 1.6 V and an I/O and level-shifting plan (lesson 54) if other blocks stay at 1.8 V.

Key takeaways

  • A path is launch clock latency + clock-to-Q + logic, checked against the capture clock latency + period − setup (setup) and against the same edge + hold (hold).
  • In the SKY130 example the setup slack is +0.276 ns and the hold slack +0.648 ns; clock-to-Q (330 ps) is larger than all four logic gates together.
  • Skew helps setup and hurts hold one for one. The scan shift path, Q straight to SCD, has the least hold margin (+0.370 ns here).
  • The scan mux costs about 50–100 ps of setup on every path; clock-to-Q is unchanged.
  • P = αCV²f + short-circuit + leakage. An inv_1 FO4 node at α = 0.1 and 100 MHz is 0.32 µW dynamic against 5 pW of leakage at 25 °C.
  • VDD from 1.8 to 1.4 V saves 43% of the energy for 83% more delay; the energy-delay product has a broad minimum near 1.6 V.
  • 100 mV of threshold is about 14× in subthreshold leakage and about 10% in drive.
  • Pipelining buys throughput less than linearly because every stage pays clock-to-Q plus setup.
