Feedback, Loading & Stability

Why a stable-looking DC result can ring, oscillate or miss its settling deadline.

What you will learn
  • Distinguish open-loop gain, loop gain, closed-loop gain and phase margin.
  • Calculate how an added pole changes crossover and phase margin.
  • Plan a meaningful loop-gain measurement and connect it to extracted and transient behavior.

Why it matters
An unstable loop rings or oscillates, whatever the DC simulation shows.

1. Negative feedback has a frequency limit

Feedback compares an output with a desired value and acts on the error. It can make gain precise, reduce distortion and reject disturbances, but the amplifier, load, routing and feedback network all introduce delay or phase shift. A loop that corrects a slow error can reinforce a faster one.

T(s) = A(s)β(s)   ·   H(s) = VOUT/VIN = A(s)/(1 + T(s))

When |T| is large and feedback has the intended sign, H approaches 1/β for this noninverting topology. “Gain of ten” does not tell us whether the loop has adequate stability margin. A feedback divider and the amplifier's open-loop response must be considered together. The TI note on overshoot and phase margin derives these feedback relationships and compares loop gain with transient response.

2. Read phase margin with its assumptions

For the usual single-loop, open-loop-stable negative-feedback system with one relevant unity crossing, a phase of −135° at |T| = 1 gives 45° phase margin. Small phase margin often means peaking and ringing; a negative margin at the ordinary crossing can indicate instability. Designers often target roughly 45–60° or more, according to settling, load and uncertainty requirements. A target number is a design choice, not a universal pass rule.

Check all crossings, gain margin and the complete loop shape. Open-loop right-half-plane poles, multiple crossings, nested loops and sampled systems require the appropriate Nyquist or discrete-time analysis. A scalar phase margin alone cannot prove every such circuit stable. Also, phase margin describes the linearized loop near one bias point; it does not establish startup, slew-rate behavior or recovery from clipping.

3. Worked example: an added load pole

Use an illustrative amplifier with A0 = 10,000 V/V, poles at 1 kHz and 5 MHz, and β = 0.1. Frequencies below are in hertz:

T(jf) = 1000 / [(1 + j f/1000)(1 + j f/5,000,000)]

The DC closed-loop gain is 10,000/(1 + 1000) = 9.990, close to the intended 10. Numerically solving |T| = 1 gives fc = 981 kHz. The loop phase there is −101.0°, so PM = 79.0°. Now multiply T by 1/(1 + j f/200,000) to represent an additional load-related pole. Crossover falls to 425 kHz, but phase falls to −159.5°: only 20.5° margin.

An output resistance Rout and capacitance CL can introduce a pole near 1/(2πRoutCL). Rout itself may depend on frequency, bias and feedback, so this is a planning estimate. ADI's capacitive-loading discussion explains why additional output capacitance can make an amplifier less stable and compares compensation choices.

4. Measure a return ratio, not just an output waveform

First establish the correct DC operating point with the intended load and feedback connected. In a simple voltage-feedback loop, a zero-DC-voltage series test source can preserve the DC connection while introducing a small-signal AC stimulus. Set other independent signal sources to AC zero without discarding their DC bias values.

For the ideal unilateral block model in Figure 3, Vr = −AβVd, hence T = −Vr/Vd. In a real circuit, the convenient voltage-injection estimate needs a suitable impedance separation: the return side should drive the forward-side input with low source impedance relative to its input impedance over the range of interest, and unintended reverse paths must be negligible. If those conditions fail, use a documented simulator stability probe or a general Middlebrook/Tian-style return-ratio method that accounts for loading and bidirectional transmission. Do not assume the simple ratio works at every wire in a schematic.

An AC magnitude of 1 is a normalization for a linearized solution; it does not mean physically swinging a 1.8 V transistor circuit by 1 V around its bias. A bench injection must be small enough to stay linear yet large enough for signal-to-noise, with injection hardware characterized. See ADI's loop-gain measurement lab for practical injection limits. Always confirm the sign convention at low frequency before calculating margin.

Solved checkpoint — A gain-of-ten stage has −159.5° loop phase at crossover. What is its phase margin?

The margin is 180° − 159.5° = 20.5°, even though the low-frequency signal gain is almost ten. The signal gain and phase margin are different measurements. If a simple additional transport delay of 100 ns were inserted at 425 kHz without changing the magnitude response, it would contribute about −360° × 425 kHz × 100 ns = −15.3°, reducing the estimated margin to about 5.2°. A sampled controller needs a fuller discrete-time model.

5. Stability is only one part of settling

Consider an ideal first-order closed-loop response with a 1 MHz pole. Its time constant is τ = 1/(2π × 1 MHz) = 159 ns. Settling a full-scale step to within half an LSB of a 10-bit range requires relative error below 1/2048, so t > τ ln(2048) ≈ 1.21 µs. A ringing response does not obey this single-exponential estimate.

Large signals can be limited by available current before small-signal settling begins. A 20 µA current charging a 10 pF load gives dV/dt = I/C = 2 V/µs. Moving 0.8 V therefore takes at least 0.4 µs if that current limit applies throughout. Device saturation, asymmetry, recovery and the final linear settling can add time. A good AC margin does not remove these limits.

6. Connect schematic stability to the real ASIC

For a SKY130 implementation, use the chosen transistor models to establish the forward gain and poles; the generic numbers above are not a recommendation for device sizes or compensation capacitance. Recheck the loop after extraction, across supply, temperature, process, relevant load states and operating modes. Include the capacitance and resistance of the actual pad, ESD network, routing, package and measurement fixture when the loop reaches off chip. A buffer stable before routing may not be stable after those elements are present.

ChangePossible benefitCost or check
Dominant-pole compensationMoves crossover away from later polesLess bandwidth; slew and settling still need transient checks
Series output isolation resistorReduces direct capacitive loading of the feedback nodeLoad-dependent voltage drop, extra noise and altered settling; the feedback pickup location matters
Stronger output stageCan reduce output resistance and improve slewMore power and capacitance; internal poles can also move
Extra capacitor at a reference outputMay reduce high-frequency disturbanceMay create a destabilizing load pole; use the reference's validated load range
Clocked load / switched capacitorImplements sampling or charge transferThe loop is periodically disturbed; evaluate the actual sampling sequence, not only an average capacitor

The analog/digital handoff: specify when the analog result has settled to its required error band, which loads may switch during that interval, and how “ready” is qualified. Digital logic should not consume an oscillating or still-settling reference merely because reset has ended. Analog verification should include the digital activity and enable sequence that the system will actually produce. Continue with sampling and aliasing and mixed-signal verification.

Check your understanding — an amplifier with open-loop gain 1000 has feedback factor β = 0.1. What are the loop gain and the closed-loop gain?

Loop gain Aβ = 100. Closed-loop gain A/(1 + Aβ) = 1000/101 ≈ 9.90, close to the ideal 1/β = 10; the 1% shortfall is 1/(1 + Aβ).

Key takeaways

  • Loop gain is Aβ; closed-loop signal gain is A/(1 + Aβ) for the stated negative-feedback topology.
  • Extra loading can lower crossover and still reduce phase margin.
  • A loop-gain measurement must preserve the operating point and account for the injection point's loading.
  • Use AC stability, large-signal transient behavior and final error-band settling together.
  • Extracted interconnect, load states and digital switching can change the loop that the schematic designer thought they had.
