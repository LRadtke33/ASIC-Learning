Lesson 36 / 84 · Part V, Digital implementation

Clock Generation: Oscillators, DCO, PLL & Jitter

An 11-stage ring of inv_1, simulated in ngspice 44.2 with this course’s models at tt 25 °C, runs at 1.849 GHz at 1.80 V (stage delay 24.58 ps). That is 0.341 of the lesson 27 FO4 delay recorded under the same conditions (72.15 ps).

Where the numbers come from. The ring frequencies and stage delays were measured on 11 November 2026, America/Chicago, with ngspice 44.2. Each stage is the inv_1 device pair of lesson 29 (NMOS sky130_fd_pr__nfet_01v8 W=0.65 µm L=0.15 µm, HVT PMOS W=1.0 µm L=0.15 µm), schematic only, no cell-layout parasitics. Decks, logs and ring_osc.json are in labs/analysis_examples/ring_osc/. FO4 values quoted for comparison are that lesson’s existing record (same models, same simulator, same day), not a new FO4 run. Caravel’s DCO trim and frequency range are from the official housekeeping documentation; this page does not simulate or measure that oscillator. PLL and jitter figures are teaching diagrams.

What you will learn

  • Write a ring oscillator’s frequency as f ≈ 1/(2 N td) and measure it on a SKY130 inv_1 ring.
  • Relate that stage delay to the course’s FO4 number, and see how both move with VDD.
  • Read Caravel’s 26-bit thermometer DCO trim from the housekeeping documents, and place a PLL around a VCO.
  • Name the usual jitter types and connect them to the sampling formula in lesson 79.

1. A ring of inverters is a clock if N is odd

An odd-length loop of inverting stages has no stable DC solution: the only consistent behaviour is oscillation. If each stage has an average delay td (the mean of a rising and a falling transition), the signal travels around the loop once in N td, and a full period — high then low at any node — takes two trips, so

f ≈ 1 / (2 N td)

Once f is measured, this equation defines the average stage delay. The delay itself depends on the load (here, one identical inverter — a fanout of one, plus the stage’s own drain capacitance), on VDD, and on the input slew, which in a ring is produced by the previous stage. An even-length loop can sit in a static state and is not used this way.

2. A recorded ring: frequency and stage delay versus VDD

The ring was run at six supplies, tt 25 °C. After discarding the first quarter of each transient, the period is the mean interval between successive VDD/2 rising crossings on n1. Stage delay is td = T / (2 × 11). At 1.80 V the period is 540.79 ps, so

f = 1 / 540.79 ps = 1.849 GHz,  td = 24.58 ps

Lesson 29’s fanout-of-1 delay for the same inverter, under a prescribed input slew rather than the ring’s self-consistent slew, was 25.05 ps. The two numbers sit within 0.5 ps. The same lesson’s FO4 delay at 1.80 V is 72.15 ps, so this ring’s stage is 24.58 / 72.15 = 0.341 FO4. A ring of fanout-1 inverters is not an FO4 path, and this ratio should not be used as a new FO4 measurement.

Check your understanding — if you double the number of stages to 22, what happens?

22 is even, so the loop can sit in a static, consistent state (half the nodes high, half low) and may never oscillate. The formula 1/(2 N td) is for an oscillating odd-length ring. If you want a slower clock from a ring, use a larger odd N (for example 21), accepting that td itself is almost unchanged because each stage still sees one similar load. A divider after a shorter ring is the more usual way to get a lower frequency with a single edge to distribute.

3. From a free-running ring to a DCO

A digitally controlled oscillator (DCO) is a ring (or another VCO topology) whose delay is steered by a digital code: extra loading, extra current, or extra delay stages switched in. Caravel, the SKY130 harness of lesson 56, includes a ring used both as a free-running DCO and as the oscillator inside a digital PLL. The housekeeping SPI documentation is the source for the following; this course has not measured that oscillator on silicon or in simulation.

The trim is a 26-bit thermometer code, in registers 0x0D through the lower two bits of 0x10. The same page says each set bit adds approximately 250 ps of delay on top of a fixed 4.67 ns, that 0x0 is fastest and 0x3ffffff is slowest, and that the approximate range is 215 MHz down to 90 MHz. A neighbouring sentence on the PLL-DCO-enable bit describes the same oscillator as “approximately 90 to 200 MHz.” This lesson quotes both wordings and does not pick a winner.

Worked arithmetic on the documentation’s own model (not a measurement): a period of 4.67 ns is 214 MHz, matching the “approximately 215 MHz” end. With all 26 trim bits set, the period is 4.67 ns + 26 × 0.250 ns = 11.17 ns, which is 89.5 MHz — the “approximately 90 MHz” end. The default trim is given as 0x3ffefff (slow, minus one). Thermometer codes and trim-range thinking are the subject of lesson 82.

Housekeeping SPI can be used even when the management CPU is held in reset — which is why it appears in the bring-up order of lesson 71. On startup the CPU clock is the external pin, not this DCO; firmware (or SPI) has to enable DCO or PLL mode deliberately.

Check your understanding — is Caravel’s 215 MHz the same kind of number as this lesson’s 1.849 GHz?

No. 1.849 GHz is a schematic inv_1 ring of 11 unloaded stages at tt 25 °C, with no layout parasitics. Caravel’s 90–215 MHz (or 90–200 MHz) is a documented range for a trimmed on-chip oscillator in the harness, with a completely different stage count, loading, and trim network, stated as approximate. They share a topology class, not a frequency.

4. A PLL wraps a VCO in a feedback loop

A phase-locked loop compares a reference (often a board crystal, or Caravel’s external clock on pin C9) to a divided copy of a VCO, and steers the VCO until the edges line up. In lock, the VCO frequency is Nfb × fref, and the output may be divided again for the core. Caravel’s digital PLL does this with a frequency-locked loop around the same trimmed ring: a feedback divider (5 bits, register 0x12) and two 3-bit output dividers (register 0x11) — one for the core, one for the user-project secondary clock. Values 0 and 1 on an output divider pass the undivided PLL clock; the documentation warns that the processor is not intended to run at that rate. The PLL-enable bit should be set before bypass is cleared, so the loop can settle.

5. Jitter is more than one number

A clock’s edges do not land at exact multiples of a period. The usual names:

NameWhat is measuredTypical question
Period jitterVariation of one period, Tn = tn+1 − tnWill this cycle’s setup path still close?
Cycle-to-cycle jitterVariation of Tn+1 − TnHow abruptly does the period change?
Phase (long-term) jitterDeviation of edge n from n × TidealHow far has the clock wandered over many cycles?
Deterministic vs randomBounded (spurs, supply ripple) vs unbounded (thermal, 1/f)Which part of the budget is a peak, and which is an RMS?

For a sampled signal, random jitter on the sampling edge limits SNR as in lesson 79: SNRjitter = −20 log10(2π fin σt). That formula uses an RMS time σt and the input frequency, not the clock frequency. A PLL can reject some close-in VCO jitter and can pass (or amplify) reference jitter, depending on the loop bandwidth — which is why “the VCO’s jitter” is not automatically “the core clock’s jitter.”

The clock tree of lesson 35 then adds its own power-supply-induced jitter as the edges travel. A clean generator is necessary; it is not sufficient.

Check your understanding — does a 10 ps RMS period jitter at 100 MHz and at 1 GHz have the same effect on a 10 MHz sampled sine?

The sampling formula depends on fin and σt, not on the sampling clock’s frequency. If σt is really 10 ps RMS in both cases, the SNR is the same. In practice the two clocks will not have the same σt, and a 1 GHz clock’s 10 ps is a much larger fraction of its period, which matters for digital setup — a different budget.

Key takeaways

  • A ring of N odd inverters oscillates at f ≈ 1/(2 N td). This 11-stage inv_1 ring was measured at 1.849 GHz at 1.80 V, td = 24.58 ps, which is 0.341 of the course FO4 (72.15 ps) under the same models — a correlation, not a new FO4.
  • Frequency is a strong function of VDD (185 MHz at 1.00 V to 2.130 GHz at 1.95 V in this schematic). A free-running ring is rarely a system clock.
  • Caravel’s DCO is a trimmed ring documented as a 26-bit thermometer code, approximately 90–215 MHz (the same page also says 90–200 MHz). Those figures are from the housekeeping docs, not from a run on this page.
  • A PLL locks a VCO to a reference through dividers. Jitter has several names; the sampling SNR formula uses RMS edge uncertainty and the input frequency.
