Metal Layers, Vias & the Stackup

How wires are built in layers above the transistors, how vias connect them, and how to read a top-down layout view.

What you will learn

  • Read the SKY130 routing stack (li1, met1–met5) and the job of each layer.
  • Apply the via rules: adjacent layers only, enclosure on both sides, and via arrays for current.
  • Read a top-down layout view and relate it to a cross-section.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
Used by step 4, floorplan and power grid for power, step 7, routing for signals and step 8, extraction and timing for extraction.
Tools

Why it matters
The layer a wire uses sets its resistance, capacitance and current limit.

1. The stackup

Lesson 3 showed how a SKY130 die is built in layers, with the official stack diagram and thicknesses. This lesson reads the same stack the way routing and layout tools do. In this course li1 and met1–met5 are the SKY130 layer names; generic teaching diagrams label the same idea M1, M2 and so on. This ordered list of layers — how many metals, how thick each one is, what the minimum width and spacing are on each — is called the stackup. It is defined by the foundry process; a design picks one of the offered metal options (for example, a "6-metal stackup") and every tool, from the router to the extractor, works from that definition.

A common pattern is that upper routing layers are thicker and permit wider wires. Adjacent layers can have the same thickness, and width is still a design choice within the rules. The lowest metals are thin and packed at a fine pitch — perfect for the millions of short, local connections between neighboring cells. The top metals are much thicker, so they have far lower resistance — that's where long routes, clocks, and the power grid live. It's a highway system: many small streets at the bottom, a few wide freeways on top.

2. What each layer is used for

Remember from lesson 14 that each layer also has a preferred routing direction, alternating horizontal/vertical as you go up, and its own routing grid. The coarser pitch of the upper layers means wider track spacing. Preferred directions are routing conventions in the technology file, not a physical ban on a legal jog in the other direction. This lesson uses the HD routing LEF's li1 plus met1–met5; other process options and PDK files can describe additional structures. [HD technology LEF]

3. Vias: the vertical connections

A via is a small hole etched in the dielectric between two metal layers and filled with conductor. This generic diagram calls the adjacent-metal cuts V1 and V2; the corresponding SKY130 layer names are via and via2. V1 connects M1 to M2, V2 connects M2 to M3, and so on. In this SKY130 stack the bottom path is more explicit: licon joins diffusion or poly to li1, then mcon joins li1 to met1. Three rules cover most of what matters:

  • These process vias connect adjacent conductors. To get from M1 to M3 you need a stacked via: V1, a small landing pad on M2, then V2. You pass through every layer on the way — there is no express elevator.
  • A via must be enclosed by metal on both layers. Each layer needs a shape (a wire or a landing pad) overlapping the via, with a required margin of metal around it — the enclosure rule.
  • Vias are resistive. A single via is a narrow constriction, so power nets and other high-current connections use via arrays — many vias in parallel — like the dark squares in lesson 14's power grid.

4. Looking down: how layout is viewed and drawn

Layout editors usually show the layout from directly above, looking down through the whole stack as if every layer were transparent. Each layer gets its own color and fill pattern, and all layers are drawn overlapping in one 2-D picture — every figure in lessons 13 and 14 was exactly this kind of view. You never see depth; you read it from the layer colors. A teal wire crossing an orange wire isn't touching it — they're on different floors, and they only connect where a via is drawn.

5. The routing abstraction stops at met5

Figure 1 and the high-density tech LEF use the same routing stack: local interconnect plus five metals: li1, then met1 through met5.

Direction and pitch are the routing grid. On a vertical layer the first pitch number is the spacing of the vertical tracks. li1 is PITCH 0.46 0.34, so its track pitch is the same 0.46 µm as the site width. Minimum width is the WIDTH line. Thickness is in the same layer block, and it is why the upper metals are less resistive per square (lesson 28).

6. Worked example: a vertical connection has a budget

Suppose an extracted cut is assumed to be 2 Ω. Four identical cuts in parallel have an ideal resistance of 2/4 = 0.5 Ω. At 1 mA they drop 0.5 mV. A stack of three such four-cut arrays has 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 Ω, before the landing metals. The 2 Ω value is a teaching assumption, not a SKY130 via specification. SkyWater’s published extraction table (Table 91 of the PDK documentation) lists 4.5 Ω per via cut, 3.41 Ω for via2 and via3, 0.38 Ω for via4 and 152 Ω for mcon; lesson 29 compares those values with the ones OpenROAD uses for estimates. Actual arrays need legal enclosure, spacing, current sharing, and extraction.

Analog ↔ digital: a signal via affects delay and coupling; a supply via affects reference error, rail droop, and lifetime. Count every vertical transition, including the path from a local-interconnect pin up to the routed metal.

Check your understanding — Can a met1–met3 connection omit met2?

Not with the adjacent-layer via stack shown here. It needs met2 landing metal plus both via and via2. The intermediate layer is electrically and geometrically present even when no long route uses it.

Check your understanding — why do power straps usually run on met4 and met5 rather than met1?

The upper metals are thicker and have lower sheet resistance: 0.047 Ω/□ on met3 and met4 and 0.0285 Ω/□ on met5, against 0.125 Ω/□ on met1 in the HD tech LEF. Long straps therefore lose less voltage, and the lower layers stay free for dense local routing and pin access.

Key takeaways

  • The stackup is the foundry-defined ladder of metal + via + dielectric layers above the silicon; the design picks one and every tool works from it.
  • Lower metals: thin, fine pitch, for dense local wiring. Upper metals: thick, low resistance, for long routes, clock, and power.
  • A via connects only adjacent layers; going up several layers takes a stacked via with a landing pad on every layer in between.
  • Vias need metal enclosure on both layers and are resistive — power uses via arrays.
  • The usual layout editor view is top-down with layers overlaid; color = layer, and depth is read, not seen. Crossing wires on different layers don't connect unless a via joins them.
  • The HD stack is li1 plus met1 through met5. Additional process/package layers are outside this routing abstraction; see RDL routing.
