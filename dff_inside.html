A Flip-Flop From the Inside

The teaching flip-flop from lesson 13, opened up, and the SkyWater cell beside it: 16 sites, 24 transistors.

What you will learn

  • Trace a master–slave flip-flop through its layout from left to right.
  • Read clock-phase generation and gated feedback in polygons.
  • Relate the teaching flop to SKY130’s dfxtp_1: 16 sites and 24 transistors.

1. The widest cell in the row

The DFF_X1 in lesson 13 is an illustrative 11-site cell. Here its chosen topology contains two 8T inverting latches, 4T of clock generation and a 4T noninverting output buffer: 24 transistors. A fixed row height encourages a wider layout, but neither gate count nor poly-finger count determines its site width. The actual SKY130 dfxtp_1 discussed below uses a different 24-device netlist and occupies 16 sites.

2. The floorplan: a story told left to right

A library designer floorplans the cell to control internal wire length, diffusion sharing, gate placement and access. Figure 2 is a conceptual zone map with an illustrative 11-site outline. Gate count does not determine site count: folding, diffusion breaks, tap strategy and local routing all contribute. The real dfxtp_1 below uses sixteen HD sites.

3. Under the microscope: the master latch in polygons

Here is the master latch — 8 of the 24 transistors — in the drawing style of lessons 16 and 18, with three things you haven't seen in polygons yet:

  • Split fingers. In an inverter or NAND, one poly finger crosses both diffusions because P and N share a gate signal. In a t-gate or tristate the P and N gates carry different signals (clk vs clk̄), so the finger is split: an upper half-finger over the p-diffusion and a lower one over the n-diffusion, each with its own connection.
  • Un-strapped diffusion. The t-gate's little diffusion segments touch no rail — no VDD or VSS strap anywhere. That's lesson 12's netlist signature ("devices not anchored to a supply"), now visible as geometry.
  • An M2 hop in the conceptual drawing. Here mb uses a short M2 jumper to cross m’s M1. It consumes routing area and a spacing halo; the particular drawing places the vias on a nominal track. A real router may use other legal local access patterns. The actual dfxtp_1 abstract discussed below declares no M2 obstruction.

4. How to read a flop in the wild

Faced with a real DFF layout or netlist — often 25–30+ transistors once scan or reset features appear — decode it in this order:

  1. Find the clock network. Trace every device controlled by the external clock and its derived phases. Some flip-flop architectures drive pass/clocked devices directly; do not assume the clock pin sees only inverters.
  2. Look for pass devices and clocked stacks. Split gate fingers and signal-to-signal diffusion can be useful clues in this drawing, but confirm each device’s gates, sources, drains and body connections from the real views.
  3. Find regenerative storage structures. The chosen master–slave topology has two gated feedback loops; pulsed or other flip-flop architectures need a different analysis.
  4. Follow D to Q in Figure 1: D → input t-gate → m → inverter → slave t-gate → s → inverter → noninverting output buffer → Q. Check the alternating latch phases and the total forward-path polarity.
  5. Identify the output stage. Its devices must drive the external Q load, but “biggest transistor” alone is not a reliable identification rule. Trace the Q net.

Scan and reset functions can be implemented by adding input selection or altering latch/control devices, but the exact architecture varies. A scan cell need not use the illustrated 2:1 transmission-gate mux, and reset need not be a simple inverter-to-NAND/NOR substitution. Trace the selected cell’s netlist and use its characterized functional and timing models; see lesson 40.

5. The library flip-flop is 16 sites and 24 transistors

sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1 is a positive-edge D flip-flop. Its checked LEF outline is 7.36 × 2.72 µm, or sixteen 0.46 µm sites, and its Liberty area is 20.0192 µm². Its SPICE netlist contains exactly 24 devices: twelve pfet_01v8_hvt and twelve nfet_01v8, all with drawn L = 0.15 µm. The teaching schematic also counts 24 devices, but its conceptual 11-site zone map is not a reconstruction of this cell.

The output pair is the same device as inv_1: the PMOS that drives Q is 1.0 µm, the NMOS that drives Q is 0.65 µm. Q's ANTENNADIFFAREA is 0.429 µm², the same number as inv_1 pin Y. The clock pair is smaller. The NMOS with its gate on CLK is 0.42 µm, and the PMOS with its gate on CLK is 0.64 µm. Section 2's picture, a bigger driver at Q and a smaller clock stage, is what this netlist does. The keepers and the pass devices are smaller still: several NMOS are 0.36 µm.

Pins D, CLK, and Q are on li1. CLK is marked USE CLOCK. The power rails are the same 0.48 µm met1 stripes as the inverter, run across all 7.36 µm. Setup, hold, and clock-to-Q for this cell start in lesson 25. The full tables, the slack arithmetic, and the minimum pulse width are lesson 26. At a 0.01 ns edge the falling-D setup is 0.103 ns and the rising-D hold is −0.029 ns.

PMOS widths, twelve devices: 0.42 µm seven times, 0.75 µm once, 1.0 µm twice (one of those is the Q pull-up), 0.64 µm twice (one of those has its gate on CLK). NMOS widths, twelve devices: 0.65 µm twice (one is the Q pull-down), 0.64 µm once, 0.42 µm five times (one has its gate on CLK, one has its gate on D), 0.36 µm four times. The other input device on D is a 0.42 µm PMOS.

Antenna areas check those gates. D is 0.126 µm², and (0.42 + 0.42) × 0.15 = 0.126. CLK is 0.159 µm², and (0.42 + 0.64) × 0.15 = 0.159. The LEF input antenna is the gate area of the transistors that pin touches, the same identity as inv_1.

The OBS block is long, and it is only li1, mcon, and met1. Six mcon cuts are listed, plus met1 straps a few micrometers long. There is no met2 shape in the obstruction. Figure 3 uses a met2 hop to teach the rule for a cell that needs one. This library cell's abstract does not declare one. Pin caps at the typical corner: CLK 1.794 fF, D 1.678 fF. Q max_capacitance is 0.162 pF.

6. The master is open while the clock is low

In the usual rising-edge master–slave arrangement drawn here, the master is transparent during the clock’s low phase and the slave during its high phase. Those phases need not have equal duration. Around the rising transition, the master closes and the slave transfers the stored value, subject to the cell’s timing and clock-shape requirements.

That is the conceptual arrangement in section 1; it was not traced device by device from the SkyWater SPICE. The library specifies the external positive-edge behavior: Q takes the sampled D value after a finite clock-to-Q delay when setup, hold, clock pulse-width and other operating requirements are satisfied (lesson 26).

Worked example — setup pushout of dfxtp_1, from ngspice

When D arrives with plenty of setup, clock-to-Q is a constant. As D is walked toward the capturing edge, the master latch has less time to resolve, and clock-to-Q stretches — pushout. The curve below is an ngspice 44.2 transient sweep of the same published sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1 schematic netlist, models, 1.678 fF load (the Liberty D-pin capacitance, standing in for a following stage) and 20 ps 0-to-100% edges used in the metastability record of lesson 39. Conditions: tt, 25 °C, 1.80 V, 11 November 2026, America/Chicago. There are no layout parasitics. The offset is D 50% minus CLK 50% (0.90 V); both edges rise. Q starts at 0. Every point on this sweep captured (Q ended above 1.5 V).

The teaching use of the curve is: a path that “meets setup” with only a few tens of picoseconds to spare is already eating into clock-to-Q of the next path, even if Q still reaches a legal 1. That is why the library’s setup time is not the point at which the flop first fails to capture.

Worked checkpoint — How much clock-pin load does an eight-flop bank add?

For eight identical dfxtp_1 cells at the cited nominal corner, the summed clock-pin capacitance is 8 × 1.794 = 14.352 fF. This excludes clock-wire capacitance, buffer inputs elsewhere on the branch and condition-dependent changes. Their unpadded placement outlines span 8 × 7.36 = 58.88 µm, or 128 HD sites. Clock-tree design must account for both the physical distribution and the characterized electrical loads.

Check your understanding — in dfxtp_1, which latch is transparent while the clock is low, and when does Q change?

The master is transparent while the clock is low, so it follows D; the slave holds the old value. At the rising edge the master closes and the slave opens, and Q takes the captured value after the clock-to-Q delay.

Key takeaways

  • The corrected teaching flip-flop uses two 8T inverting latches, 4T clock generation and a 4T noninverting output buffer: 24T. Many other flip-flop topologies and counts exist.
  • A fixed row height encourages lateral growth. The 11-site illustration is conceptual; gate/finger count is not equal to site count. The actual dfxtp_1 is 16 sites.
  • Big cells are floorplanned for routing and diffusion sharing. Gated feedback is disabled during write; strength ratios are topology-dependent.
  • New polygon patterns: split fingers where P and N gates carry different signals, and un-strapped diffusion marking t-gates.
  • Internal crossovers can use upper layers and consume routing capacity. The checked dfxtp_1 LEF has li1/mcon/met1 obstructions and no met2 obstruction.
  • The stored bit depends on physical storage nodes and regenerative feedback. Scan and reset versions may add a mux or alter feedback/control devices; check the actual cell topology and timing rather than assuming every variant shares one skeleton.
  • dfxtp_1 is 16 sites and 24 transistors. The Q pair matches inv_1. The LEF obstruction has no met2.
  • In the usual rising-edge pair the master is transparent while the clock is low and the slave is transparent while the clock is high.
