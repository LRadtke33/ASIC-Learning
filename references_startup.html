References, Temperature & Startup

Why the voltage scale, initial state and trim sequence matter

What you will learn
  • Derive a first-order bandgap sum and distinguish PTAT, CTAT (complementary to absolute temperature) and residual curvature.
  • Separate initial error, temperature behavior, load response and calibration resolution.
  • Recognize unintended bias equilibria and design informative power-ramp experiments.
  • Connect reference readiness, trim, routing and digital conversion accuracy.

Why it matters
Every converter, regulator and bias circuit depends on a reference that must start reliably and stay accurate.

1. A reference sets a scale that the rest of the chip trusts

An ADC compares its input with a reference; a DAC converts codes into a fraction of a reference; a regulator compares a divided supply with a reference. A bias network can also convert a reference voltage into current. The reference therefore connects transistor behavior to digital meaning. A perfectly monotonic converter can still report the wrong physical voltage if its scale drifts.

For an ideal unipolar N-bit ADC, the approximate code is 2NVIN/VREF, away from clipping and code-boundary details. A small reference error produces Δcode/code ≈ −ΔVREF/VREF for a fixed input. A +0.1% reference error therefore gives approximately −0.1% gain error. In a ratiometric sensor, where excitation and conversion use the same reference, part of that error can cancel; timing, wiring drops and unequal filtering can prevent perfect cancellation.

Specify the operating conditions before declaring a reference “accurate.” Initial voltage, temperature variation, supply sensitivity, load regulation, noise and startup are separate behaviors. ADI AN-82 provides useful definitions for comparing them; its historical product examples are not predictions for a new SKY130 circuit.

2. Why a weighted junction difference helps

A forward-active bipolar transistor approximately follows IC = ISexp(VBE/VT), with VT = kT/q. For matched junctions at a controlled current-density ratio N, subtraction removes the common saturation-current term: ΔVBE = VT ln N. Absolute temperature T is in kelvin. This difference is proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT), provided the ratio remains controlled and nonidealities are small.

At a chosen bias, VBE usually decreases with temperature; it is often called a complementary-to-absolute-temperature, or CTAT, term. The name describes its trend, not an exact negative straight line. Adding a suitable amount of the PTAT term can cancel the local first-order slope. ADI’s university reference chapter develops this principle. Actual implementations must establish the currents, account for base current and provide stable feedback.

Worked example: at 300 K, kT/q ≈ 25.852 mV. For N = 8, ΔVBE ≈ 53.758 mV and its slope is (k/q)ln 8 ≈ 179.19 µV/K. Assume VBE = 0.650 V and a local slope of −1.8 mV/K. The required weight is K = 1.8/0.17919 ≈ 10.045. The weighted term is 0.540 V, giving VREF ≈ 1.190 V. This calculation deliberately exposes the assumptions; “bandgap” does not imply an exact universal 1.200 V output.

The cancellation is local. Junction curvature, current variation, resistor temperature coefficients, finite amplifier gain and mismatch remain. Low-voltage references can scale or combine CTAT/PTAT currents to produce a smaller output, but reducing the output voltage does not automatically remove the internal headroom requirements.

3. SKY130 gives you devices, not a finished reference

The SKY130 device documentation describes sky130_fd_pr__pnp_05v5 as a substrate-collector PNP, with published emitter geometries and required guard-ring considerations. Its collector is the substrate; it is not an independently isolated ideal transistor that can be placed at an arbitrary collector potential. The documentation also disallows reverse-active use. The correct primitive, topology and physical isolation must agree.

For a nominal 1.8 V domain and an illustrative 1.2 V output, only 0.6 V remains between the output and upper rail. That arithmetic is a first warning about stacked devices, amplifier output swing and startup current paths. It does not prove any particular circuit works or fails. Check every relevant terminal voltage using the selected device family and model revision; model-validity ranges are not a substitute for reliability or layout rules.

A reference needs matching as well as nominal device behavior. Unit junction arrays, resistor orientation, equal surroundings and symmetric routing can reduce systematic differences. Guard rings and substrate connections affect isolation. Heating from a nearby digital or output block can create a gradient even when the schematic temperature is uniform. A Monte Carlo model of independent random mismatch does not automatically represent that physical gradient. These choices belong in the layout plan before drawing a large trim range around an avoidable error.

4. Trim the error you actually measured

Offset trim can move the voltage at one temperature. Slope trim can alter its first-order temperature dependence. Curvature correction changes the remaining shape. These controls may interact in a real circuit: changing a resistor ratio that weights ΔVBE changes both output voltage and slope. Calibration therefore needs a model of the trim sensitivities, sufficient range, monotonic behavior and a stated measurement uncertainty.

In the earlier example, a +1% error in K adds about 5.4 mV at 300 K and approximately +18 µV/K of residual slope. The latter is about 15 ppm/K relative to 1.190 V. Removing 5.4 mV with an independent offset adjustment at one temperature leaves that slope error. A second temperature point can estimate a linear correction, but it cannot identify an arbitrary curve from two samples.

Real trim switches add parasitics and leakage, and a code update can inject a transient. Store trim values with the intended reset behavior, establish them before declaring the analog function ready, and define what happens for an invalid or unprogrammed code.

Solved check — Why are temperature endpoints insufficient?

After ideal offset and slope trim in Figure 2, let V(T) = 1.200 V − 0.2 µV/K² × (T − 25 °C)² over −40 to 125 °C. The maximum is 1.200 V at the interior point 25 °C; the minimum is 1.198 V at 125 °C. The box coefficient is (2 mV)/(1.2 V × 165 K) × 106 ≈ 10.10 ppm/K. Subtracting only the two endpoints gives 1.155 mV and underestimates the full range. A box coefficient bounds total spread over an interval; it is not the derivative at every temperature.

5. Self bias introduces an initial-state problem

A self-biased loop derives the current that keeps its own amplifier and reference core operating. Such a loop can admit an unwanted zero-current equilibrium as well as an intended operating point. Solving the intended DC equations shows that the desired state exists; it does not show how an unpowered circuit gets there. An initial-condition command that places the simulation near the desired state can conceal this distinction.

A startup circuit supplies a temporary imbalance or current, then becomes sufficiently inactive that it does not corrupt normal operation. Its effectiveness depends on supply ramp, temperature, leakage and stored charge. ADI’s discussion of reference startup illustrates why an output’s previous voltage and external loading can matter. A fixed power-on pulse and a state-sensitive startup path solve different problems: the pulse can expire before a slow supply becomes usable, or fail to reappear after a brownout.

In the behavioral model of Figure 3, τdx/dt = x(x − 0.3)(1 − x) at full supply without a kick. For 0 < x < 0.3 the derivative is negative; for 0.3 < x < 1 it is positive. A startup action must move the state into the intended basin. The lab adds supply qualification, a low-supply discharge path and a separate kick. These are constructed dynamics for learning initial-state behavior, not extracted transistor poles, startup currents or reference accuracy.

6. Reference distribution is a dynamic load problem

The reference core, output buffer, routing and consumer form a system. A switched-capacitor ADC may draw short charge packets even when its average current is small. A high-impedance core that is accurate in a DC sweep can move during those packets. Place and size local storage using the actual charge demand, then check buffer stability, settling, package/interconnect impedance and available recharge time.

Charge example: suppose a sampling event requires 5 pC, corresponding to charging an illustrative 10 pF capacitance by 0.5 V. If a local 1 nF capacitor alone supplies that event, its immediate ideal droop is Q/C = 5 mV. A 12-bit converter with a 1.2 V reference has an ideal LSB of 292.97 µV; 5 mV is about 17.1 of those reference-voltage LSBs. This is a charge-budget calculation, not the final ADC code error: the actual error depends on switching sequence, recovery and where the reference is sampled.

More capacitance reduces immediate droop but consumes area and can alter the driving amplifier’s poles. A series isolation resistor changes both damping and DC/load error. Shared routes can carry converter pulses into another analog block. Keep the current return path explicit, and measure the reference at the consumer as well as at the core. The sampling lesson and the acquisition lab extend this discussion.

7. Turn “ready” into an interface contract

Digital logic needs a usable event, not an optimistic delay copied from one nominal waveform. Define the supply range, trim validity, voltage error band, persistence time and response to loss of regulation. A monitor itself needs valid bias and thresholds. An asynchronous analog-ready signal must enter synchronous logic through an appropriate synchronization/reset-release scheme; its analog meaning and its digital timing are separate requirements.

A useful verification matrix varies initial conditions, ramp rates, interruptions, load and temperature. Observe startup current as well as output voltage: a reference can reach an acceptable voltage while a startup branch remains partially on and adds error or noise. Test re-enable and brownout, not only the first power application. Explain any initialized nodes and ideal sources in the testbench so that another designer can reproduce the intended experiment.

Solved check — Does a 99% crossing prove a reference is ready?

No. For a 1.2 V target, 99% is 1.188 V: still 12 mV, or 1%, low. A system requiring 0.1% error needs a much tighter band, with an agreed persistence and loading condition. A first threshold crossing can precede overshoot or a later dip. Lab 2 reports first 99% crossings only to compare its behavioral trajectories, and labels them accordingly.

8. Connect the reference to the rest of the course

Use Bias, Mirrors & Headroom to understand current sources, Feedback & Stability to reason about the regulation loop, and Noise & Mismatch to separate noise from device spread. Lab 2 measures real SKY130 MOS bias and amplifier behavior, then deliberately switches to a labeled behavioral model for startup experiments. The separation makes clear which conclusions are supported by transistor models and which come from a constructed teaching model.

Key takeaways

  • A reference error changes the meaning of a digital code; a stable supply alone does not establish accuracy.
  • PTAT/CTAT weighting cancels a local slope under stated assumptions. Curvature, mismatch, loading and noise remain.
  • Offset, slope and curvature calibration solve different problems. Measure the full temperature range.
  • A correct operating point and a forced initial state do not prove power-on or brownout recovery.
  • Reference routing, decoupling, readiness and trim sequencing belong in the analog/digital interface specification.
  • A StrongARM comparator, an LDO that uses a reference like this one, and a power-on reset are lesson 77.
