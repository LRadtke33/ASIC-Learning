Lesson 30 / 84 · Part IV, Loads, timing and interconnect

Electromigration

How current, geometry, temperature, and time affect a wire’s lifetime. The published routing-LEF limits are an example of a reliability check, separate from voltage drop.

Where the numbers come from. The limits are the DCCURRENTDENSITY and ACCURRENTDENSITY lines in sky130_fd_sc_hd.tlef. Each comment says the number is at a junction temperature of 90°C. The UNITS block has no current unit. The source comments explicitly identify mA per micrometer of width, or mA per via. [pinned technology LEF] These 90°C entries are not a complete reliability deck for arbitrary temperatures or product lifetimes.

What you will learn

  • Apply current-density limits per micrometer of width, separately for average and RMS current.
  • Keep electromigration, voltage drop and metal-density rules distinct.
  • Use Black’s equation to reason about how lifetime depends on current density and temperature.

Why it matters
A wire that passes every timing check can still wear out within the product’s lifetime.

1. The limit is current per width, not current alone

Electromigration is metal atoms drifting under a flow of electrons until the wire opens or a neighbor shorts. The underlying stress is current density through the conductor cross-section. Because a layer has a nominal thickness, this LEF expresses a limit per unit width. For identical cuts with equal current sharing, two parallel vias have twice the tabulated single-cut budget. Real arrays can share unequally, especially near a narrow feed or corner.

met1 and met2 are both AVERAGE 2.8 mA/µm and RMS 6.1 mA/µm. Minimum width is 0.14 µm, so a minimum wire is allowed 0.14 × 2.8 = 0.39 mA average, and 0.14 × 6.1 = 0.85 mA RMS. The standard-cell rail is the same metal, drawn 0.48 µm tall (lesson 17). Its average budget is 0.48 × 2.8 = 1.344 mA. Its RMS budget is 0.48 × 6.1 = 2.928 mA.

2. Average and RMS are two different checks

DCCURRENTDENSITY AVERAGE is the long-term current, the one that walks metal down the wire over years. A capacitive signal wire has charging and discharging pulses of opposite sign. Logic duty cycle alone does not specify those currents. A nearly zero signed average does not establish reliability: use the foundry’s treatment of each direction, duty cycle, recovery, and self-heating. Heating follows the square of the current, so the file also gives ACCURRENTDENSITY RMS on the routing metals. RMS is larger than the average on every metal in this file: 6.1 against 2.8 on met1, 14.9 against 6.8 on met3 and met4, 22.34 against 10.17 on met5.

A signal net can pass the average check and fail the RMS check. A DC bias current does the opposite kind of harm: the average is the whole current, so the average limit is the one that binds. The via layers in this file publish only the average. There is no RMS number on mcon, via, via2, via3, or via4, and there is no peak-current line on any layer.

None of these have been scaled away from 90°C. The file does not give a second temperature.

3. The cell rail is one shared stripe

Every high-density cell draws the met1 ground rail from y = −0.24 µm to y = 0.24 µm, and the power rail from y = 2.48 µm to y = 2.96 µm. Both rectangles are 0.48 µm tall, centered on the row boundary. The flipped row next door draws the same rectangle on that shared edge. The two drawings occupy one stripe. The current limit is the limit of that one 0.48 µm stripe, 1.344 mA average.

The via that feeds the stripe is often the tighter object. One via (met1 to met2) is 0.29 mA average. The rail's 1.344 mA needs 1.344 / 0.29 = 4.6 of them, so an ideal minimum of five equally loaded cuts, totaling 1.45 mA. The cut from local interconnect up to the rail is a different via, mcon, at 0.36 mA. A single contact inside a cell is not the rail's budget, and the rail's budget is not a license for one contact to carry 1.34 mA.

via width in the same file is 0.15 µm and the spacing is 0.17 µm, so neighboring cuts want about 0.32 µm center to center. Five cuts in a row are a couple of micrometers of rail, before enclosure. That is only a count of current. It is not a via array from the design manual, and it says nothing about the grid the strap has to hit (lesson 14).

RMS on the same rail is 2.93 mA. A clocked rail current that averages 1.0 mA can still fail RMS if the pulses are tall. Average and RMS both have to be computed from the current waveform. The LEF does not do that arithmetic. It only publishes the two ceilings.

4. This is not IR drop, and it is not the density rule

IR drop is about voltage: current times resistance (lesson 28) tells you how many millivolts the far end of a power wire has lost. Electromigration is about lifetime. A short, narrow wire can lose almost no voltage, because it has almost no resistance, and still exceed the current-per-micrometer limit; a long, wide wire can do the reverse, with a legal current density and too little voltage at its far end. Fixing one does not automatically fix the other. Widening a wire helps both, while shortening it reduces only the voltage drop: the width-only LEF calculation gives no extra current budget for a short wire, although a process-qualified short-wire (Blech) rule may.

met1 through met4 also carry MAXIMUMDENSITY 70, checked in a window of 700 µm by 700 µm, stepping 70 µm. That caps how much of the window may be covered by the layer. It is a fill rule, a maximum of 70%, not a current. This file does not put that line on li1, on any via, or on met5. It also does not publish a minimum density. Do not read 70% as "fill until you reach 70." Metal fill, as distinct from cell fill, is lesson 61.

5. The routing LEF gives no li1 current limit

li1 is the resistive layer, 71.8 Ω/µm (lesson 28). It is the layer a person most wants a current limit for, and the tech LEF used here does not have a DCCURRENTDENSITY line on it. The metals and the vias above do. A number borrowed from another process, or from a slide that says it was scaled from somewhere else, is not this limit. If a design needs a local-interconnect current limit, it has to come from a source this file is not.

6. Black's equation is the lifetime model

A common empirical form of Black’s lifetime model for the mean time to failure (MTTF) is MTTF = A j−n exp(Ea / kT). j is current density. T is absolute temperature. A, n, and Ea are fit to the process. This tech LEF does not publish those fit parameters. It publishes current limits at 90 °C; it does not let you infer years of service or extrapolate a 125 °C limit. [NIST reliability model]

Foundry decks usually split that limit three ways. Average current is the lifetime of a net that flows one direction, such as a rail. RMS current is the heating of a wire that swings both ways. Peak current is a short pulse. This file has DCCURRENTDENSITY AVERAGE and ACCURRENTDENSITY RMS. It has no peak line. Under qualifying boundary conditions, stress buildup can suppress EM below a critical current-density × length product (the Blech effect). This is not a blanket exemption for every short wire; the relevant threshold and connected interconnect structure must be covered by the reliability methodology.

7. Worked example: an average-current pass can hide an RMS failure

Assume a unidirectional rectangular supply-current pulse of 10 mA for 10% of each period and zero otherwise. For duty factor D = 0.10, Iavg = Ipk D = 1.0 mA and Irms = Ipk √D = 3.16 mA. Compare that waveform with the 0.48 µm met1 rail’s quoted 90°C limits:

CheckCalculated currentLEF example budgetResult
Average1.00 mA1.344 mAPass
RMS3.16 mA2.928 mAFail
Peak10.0 mANot provided hereUnresolved

Analog ↔ digital: a steady bias current and a switching bank with the same average current can stress the interconnect differently. Both teams need the actual waveform, local temperature, via path, and lifetime target. Decoupling changes where transient current flows; it does not erase average current through the supply network.

Check your understanding — Does a 90°C pass establish a 125°C lifetime?

No. The local metal temperature, activity, lifetime target, and process-qualified derating model are required. Black’s equation describes a trend but cannot manufacture missing process fit parameters.

Check your understanding — a 0.14 µm met1 wire carries 0.30 mA average and 0.90 mA RMS. Does it pass the tech LEF limits?

The limits are 2.8 mA/µm average and 6.1 mA/µm RMS, so a 0.14 µm wire allows 0.39 mA average and 0.85 mA RMS. The average passes, but the RMS current is too high: widen the wire or reduce the current.

At a macro ring the via is often the limit. A 1.6 µm met5 strap crossing a 1.74 µm met4 ring side has room for a single 0.8 µm via4 cut, rated at 2.49 mA. The wires on either side of it carry far more, so count cuts at every feed (lesson 50, section 7).

EM on the power grid, and the fact that LibreLane Classic’s IR script does not pass -enable_em, is taken up in lesson 63.

Key takeaways

  • Routing-layer limits in this tech LEF are mA per micrometer of width, at 90°C. Via limits are mA per cut.
  • met1 and met2: 2.8 mA/µm average, 6.1 mA/µm RMS. A 0.14 µm wire is 0.39 mA and 0.85 mA.
  • The shared cell rail is one 0.48 µm met1 stripe: 1.344 mA average, 2.928 mA RMS. One met1–met2 via is 0.29 mA, so the via count binds before the stripe does.
  • Average is the long-term metal movement. RMS is the heating. This file gives RMS on the metals only.
  • IR drop is voltage. Electromigration is lifetime. MAXIMUMDENSITY 70 is neither. li1 has no current-density line in this file.
  • Black's equation is how lifetime depends on current density and temperature. This LEF gives average and RMS limits at 90 °C, and no peak and no Blech length.
  • HCI, BTI, TDDB and self-heating — aging mechanisms that are not electromigration — are lesson 31. The digital-yield view is lesson 32.
