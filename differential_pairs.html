Differential Pairs, Common Mode & CMFB

What a pair rejects, what it converts, and why two outputs need an average-voltage controller.

What you will learn
  • Decompose two-wire signals into differential and common-mode coordinates.
  • Derive gain and current steering while keeping input/output normalization explicit.
  • Distinguish common-mode output motion from differential error, and plan CMFB verification.

Why it matters
The differential pair is the input stage of most amplifiers, comparators and converters.

1. Define the signal before measuring the gain

A differential pair compares two voltages. It does not remove the need for a valid voltage on either pin. The useful coordinates are vd = v1 − v2 and vcm = (v1 + v2)/2. Conversely, v1 = vcm + vd/2 and v2 = vcm − vd/2. The same decomposition applies to two outputs. These definitions are central to MIT’s differential-amplifier analysis.

For example, 1.005 V and 0.995 V represent a 10 mV differential signal at a 1.000 V input common mode. Adding 0.300 V to both inputs preserves the 10 mV difference but moves common mode to 1.300 V. The circuit may respond differently because device headroom, body effect and bias currents have changed. “The difference is unchanged” is a statement about the stimulus, not proof that the hardware is still operating correctly.

Pure differentialAdd +vd/2 to one input and −vd/2 to the otherOutput difference and each output’s headroom
Pure common modeAdd the same vcm to both inputsOutput average and any unwanted output difference

Solved check — Is a 10 mV step on one input purely differential?

No. It changes vd by 10 mV and vcm by 5 mV. A pure 10 mV differential step instead moves the two pins by +5 mV and −5 mV around the same common mode. Specify this normalization when reporting a gain; otherwise a factor-of-two disagreement is easy to create.

2. The pair steers a finite tail current

Two matched input devices share a source node and a tail-current source. With equal inputs and symmetric loads, the tail current divides equally. Raising v1 relative to v2 makes branch 1 conduct more and branch 2 less. Resistive loads then lower vo1 and raise vo2. An active load can provide higher resistance and may combine the branch currents into a single-ended output; that changes gain, poles and output-common-mode behavior.

Assume, for a hand example, IT = 40 µA, RD = 45 kΩ, equal 20 µA branch currents, and input-device overdrive VOV = 0.20 V. Then gm ≈ 2ID/VOV = 0.20 mS, and the drain bias is 1.8 − 20 µA × 45 kΩ = 0.90 V. These are illustrative strong-inversion assumptions, not transistor sizes or measured SKY130 values. A BSIM operating-point report, rather than a universal threshold constant, must establish a real design’s gm and region.

The tail source also needs voltage to maintain current. Its finite output resistance allows the total current to vary as the common-source node moves. Its capacitances can inject noise and change the response at high frequency. An ideal current symbol is therefore a useful first step, but it hides exactly the effects a physical mixed-signal design must check.

3. Small-signal gain and large-signal steering are different limits

For a symmetric pair, a pure differential perturbation leaves the source node approximately stationary to first order. Each gate sees half of vd. Neglecting body effect and transistor output conductance, the left output changes by −gmRDvd/2; the right changes by the opposite amount. With vo,d = vo1 − vo2:

Add = vo,d/vd ≈ −gmRD = −9 V/V
Ads = vo1/vd ≈ −gmRD/2 = −4.5 V/V

A 1 mV differential input therefore predicts a −9 mV output difference, produced by about −4.5 mV and +4.5 mV individual output changes. For actual devices, replace the simple load resistance with the appropriate incremental network, including finite ro, feedback and loading. At high frequency, use the full transfer function rather than one constant gain.

In the ideal square-law pair, with β = μCoxW/L and both devices conducting, I1 − I2 = βvd√(IT/β − vd²/4). Complete steering occurs at |vd| = √(2IT/β), about 0.283 V for Figure 2. This model requires sufficient drain and tail compliance. A resistor load may drive a drain too low before that endpoint, and short-channel behavior, subthreshold conduction and mismatch alter the curve. A comparator deliberately exploits large-signal regeneration; a linear amplifier normally operates in a much smaller region.

4. Common-mode rejection requires a stated output definition

Suppose the tail source has incremental resistance rt. With matched branches, negligible transistor ro/body effect, and the same resistor loads, a common input change moves both outputs together with approximately Acs = vo1/vcm = −gmRD/(1 + 2gmrt). For rt = 500 kΩ, this is −9/201 ≈ −0.0448 V/V. A 20 mV common input step produces about −0.896 mV at each output.

In perfect symmetry those equal output changes cancel when subtracted. Thus finite tail resistance alone does not create a nonzero differential-output common-mode gain. Mismatch, unequal parasitics or asymmetrical loading convert common mode into differential error. This distinction prevents confusing a change in output average with the error that a differential receiver actually observes. ADI MT-042 discusses CMRR as the relationship between differential signal gain and unwanted response to a common input.

A single-ended reading instead uses |Ads/Acs|. With the definitions above it is 100.5, or about 40.0 dB. A textbook that normalizes its differential gain to the half-input vd/2 reports a different numerical ratio. State the input normalization and measured output before comparing numbers.

Mismatch example: change the resistor loads by +1% and −1%, so their difference is 900 Ω. In this restricted model, the common-mode branch-current change from a 20 mV input step is about 19.9 nA. Multiplying by the load difference gives about 17.9 µV of unwanted output difference. The same mismatch also creates a nominal DC output offset of 20 µA × 900 Ω = 18 mV. Offset and CMRR are separate specifications: a calibration that removes one does not automatically fix the other.

5. Input range and output swing share the voltage budget

For an NMOS pair above a tail sink, a first estimate is VCM,min ≈ VSS + VDS,sat,tail + VGS,input. At the upper end, each input device must retain sufficient drain-source voltage. In the long-channel approximation, VCM,max ≲ VD + VTH,input at the relevant drain bias. An active PMOS load imposes additional upper-rail headroom on the outputs.

Assume VGS,input = 0.65 V, tail saturation requirement 0.20 V, drain bias 0.90 V and threshold 0.45 V, with body-effect changes ignored for this estimate. The resulting input-common-mode interval is approximately 0.85–1.35 V. An input centered at 1.0 V fits; centering the same small differential signal at 0.5 V does not. Even inside that estimate, a large output excursion can violate saturation on one branch. Model-validity ranges and reliability limits are additional constraints, not substitutes for circuit headroom.

For SKY130, use the actual device family, four-terminal connections and model revision. A source-coupled NMOS pair generally has a moving source node while its substrate/body remains at its assigned bias, so body effect matters. The official device documentation identifies model families and validity conditions. The arithmetic in this lesson does not select a legal layout, prove a common-mode range or establish a reliability rating.

6. Why fully differential amplifiers need common-mode feedback

Two high-resistance output nodes can have the correct difference while their average drifts toward a rail. Differential feedback does not necessarily correct a load-current imbalance that moves both outputs together. A common-mode feedback circuit measures the output average, compares it with VCM,REF, and changes a suitable load or tail bias. TI’s fully differential amplifier report explains the role of the output-common-mode control alongside the differential signal path.

The detector may use resistors, transistors or switched capacitors. Each choice adds loading, noise, nonlinearities and operating-range constraints. Imperfect averaging can feed some differential signal into the common-mode controller. The actuator also changes device operating points, so the differential and common-mode loops can interact even though the ideal coordinate decomposition looks independent.

Check both loops’ stability with their correct DC bias, loads and operating modes. Good differential-loop phase margin does not establish common-mode-loop stability. A slow or ringing common-mode loop can spoil ADC acquisition even when the difference waveform looks settled. Conversely, an aggressive common-mode controller can couple clock feedthrough or detector noise back into both outputs. See lesson 73 before treating a common-mode reference pin as a perfect clamp.

7. Matching, routing and the digital boundary

Input-device mismatch creates offset and imbalance; load, contact and routing mismatch can convert common interference into differential error. Matching orientation and surroundings, appropriate common-centroid/interdigitated placement, equal well conditions and symmetric routing can reduce selected systematic errors. They do not remove random mismatch or guarantee cancellation of an arbitrary gradient. Include extraction, model-supported statistical analysis and the actual coupled aggressors when the specification depends on small errors.

Digital designers should provide the input/output common-mode levels, differential swing, sampling sequence, kickback load and invalid intervals. Analog designers should state the allowed pin voltages, source impedance, settling requirement and ready behavior. A differential ADC input does not grant permission to drive either pin outside its permitted range. A reset signal also cannot guarantee that a common-mode feedback loop has reached its final bias.

Solved check — Convert an 80 dB CMRR specification into an error

For the specified measurement configuration, 80 dB corresponds to a ratio of 10,000. A 200 mV common-mode disturbance therefore represents about 20 µV input-referred differential error. At differential gain 9 V/V, that is about 180 µV output error. This estimate applies at the frequency, bias and common-mode range where the CMRR was established; clipping or transient recovery can produce much larger errors.

8. A verification sequence that exposes the hidden assumptions

Start with equal inputs and inspect branch currents, all terminal voltages and the output average. Sweep input common mode while holding the differential input at zero. Then apply normalized differential and common-mode AC excitations separately and record both output coordinates. Repeat with realistic loading, mismatched elements where justified, and frequency-dependent parasitics. Finally, use transient tests for common-mode steps, differential steps, enable/startup and sampled-load activity; AC analysis alone cannot prove recovery from an invalid state.

Lab 2 runs actual SKY130 mirror and common-source experiments that establish the bias, output-resistance and loading concepts used here. Its startup experiment is explicitly behavioral. Neither that lab nor the illustrative curves above constitute a completed differential-pair or common-mode-feedback design; those are natural next experiments once the required bias and measurement definitions are clear.

Key takeaways

  • State differential and common-mode coordinates, including the factor of two in each input excitation.
  • A differential pair steers a finite tail current and requires headroom on every relevant device.
  • Output-common-mode gain and common-mode-to-differential error are different measurements.
  • Mismatch and asymmetric parasitics turn shared disturbances into differential error.
  • Common-mode feedback needs its own range, startup, noise and stability analysis.
