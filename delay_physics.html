Delay from Physics: Transistor, Contact, Via, Wire & Gate

A gate delay is a transistor current charging a capacitor through a chain of resistors. This lesson follows one SKY130 net from the drain diffusion, up through the contacts and vias, along a met3 route and back down into a gate oxide, and puts a number on every piece.

Where the numbers come from. Device curves are ngspice runs of the bundled SKY130 tt models at 25 °C and 1.80 V (nfet_01v8, pfet_01v8_hvt, the device sizes inside sky130_fd_sc_hd__inv_1). Cell numbers are the HD tt_025C_1v80 Liberty file. Wire R and C come from the HD tech LEF, as in lesson 28. Contact and via resistances come from two published SkyWater sources that disagree; both are shown. Route lengths, fanouts and the 1 mm example are illustrative choices, not values from a design. Simulations use schematic netlists without cell layout parasitics, so they are teaching estimates, not signoff. Every script and result is in labs/analysis_examples/delay_physics/.

What you will learn

  • Turn a transistor’s I–V curve into an effective resistance, and see why SKY130’s short-channel NMOS is nearly linear rather than square-law.
  • Read gm, output resistance and intrinsic gain from the same curves, and see which of them matter for delay.
  • Build the gate capacitance from Cox, W, L and overlap, and compare it with the Liberty pin capacitance.
  • Add contact, via and wire resistance and capacitance into one Elmore estimate, and check it against simulation.
  • Explain fanout, FO4, logical effort, buffer trees, the L² wire term and repeaters with SKY130 numbers.

Why it matters
When a path is slow you need to know which physical piece to change: the driver, the layer, the via count, the fanout or the wire length.

1. One net, five physical pieces

Lesson 21 treated a load as one capacitor, and lesson 28 added the wire’s resistance. Here we go one level lower and ask where each resistance and capacitance physically lives. Figure 1 is a cross-section of one net: an inv_4 drives a 1 mm route on met3 that ends at an inv_1 input.

The current starts in the driver’s channel and leaves through the n+ (or p+) drain diffusion. A licon contact lifts it into the cell’s li1 output pin. To reach met3 the router stacks three more cuts: mcon from li1 to met1, via from met1 to met2 and via2 from met2 to met3. The route runs along met3 and comes back down the same stack at the receiver, ending on a licon that sits on the poly gate. Under that gate, a 4.15 nm oxide makes the capacitor that the whole chain is charging.

Every element in Figure 1 is either a resistor in series with the current or a capacitor to an AC ground. Figure 2 redraws it as a circuit. The driver is an effective resistance Reff with its own drain capacitance Cself. Each cut stack is a series resistor. The route is a distributed RC line, drawn as four sections. The gate is Cg.

The rest of the lesson fills in each element from physics and then adds them up. The single equation we are working toward is the Elmore sum of Figure 2, with the usual 0.69 and 0.38 factors for the 50% point:

t50 ≈ 0.69 Reff(Cself + Cw + Cg) + 0.69 Rup(Cw + Cg) + 0.38 RwCw + 0.69 RwCg + 0.69 RdownCg

Each resistor is multiplied by all the capacitance downstream of it. Section 9 derives the factors.

2. The transistor is a current source first

A switching transistor spends most of an output transition with its gate fully on and its drain moving from one rail toward the other. Figure 3 is the SKY130 I–V family for the two devices inside inv_1: an NMOS 0.65 µm wide and a high-threshold PMOS 1.0 µm wide, both 0.15 µm long. The shaded band is the half of the swing that sets the 50% delay: the drain moving from VDD down to VDD/2.

The textbook long-channel model is the square law. In saturation,

ID = ½ µCox(W/L)(VGS − Vt)²(1 + λVDS)

It assumes carrier velocity grows in proportion to the lateral field. In a 0.15 µm channel at 1.8 V the field is high enough that electrons reach a saturated velocity near the drain, and the current becomes closer to linear in overdrive: ID ≈ W·vsat·Cox(VGS − Vt). The alpha-power law of Sakurai and Newton bridges the two with one fitted exponent, ID = K(VGS − Vt)α, where α = 2 is square law and α = 1 is fully velocity-saturated.

Fitting the simulated saturation current between 1.0 and 1.8 V gives α = 1.09 for the NMOS and α = 1.70 for the PMOS, with fitted Vt of 0.78 and 0.80 V. Holes are slower and saturate less, so the PMOS sits closer to square law. The right half of Figure 4 shows what that means in practice: a square law anchored at the NMOS’s 1.0 V current (64 µA) predicts 1377 µA at 1.8 V. The model gives 336 µA, four times less.

3. gm, output resistance and gain: what they do and do not do to delay

Analog design lives on the small-signal slopes of Figure 3. The transconductance gm = ∂ID/∂VGS says how much current a small gate change buys; the output resistance rout = 1/gds = 1/(∂ID/∂VDS) says how flat the saturated curve is, which is channel-length modulation. Their product gmrout is the most voltage gain one transistor can give. For the inv_1 NMOS at full drive it is 9.2; lesson 72 uses the same quantities at analog bias points.

An inverter is two such devices fighting at the switching point, so its gain there is roughly (gmn + gmp)(rout,n ∥ rout,p). In the Lab 1 DC sweep the inv_1 slope peaks at −18.6 at Vin = 0.766 V. That gain is why a chain of gates restores logic levels: a slightly degraded input still produces a full-swing output.

For delay, the big-signal current matters, not the slopes. rout enters only through λ: between VDS = 1.8 and 0.9 V the saturated NMOS current falls by roughly λ × 0.9 V ≈ 10%, which slightly lengthens the discharge. gm matters indirectly, through how fast the gate voltage rises during a slow input edge. That is why Liberty indexes delay by input slew as well as by load (lesson 23).

Check your understanding — why does the NMOS’s alpha of 1.09 matter for a designer who never writes a device equation?

Because it says the current is nearly proportional to overdrive, not to its square. Lowering VDD from 1.8 to 1.4 V cuts the NMOS overdrive from about 1.02 to 0.62 V. A square law would predict the current falls to (0.62/1.02)² ≈ 37%; the alpha-power fit predicts (0.62/1.02)1.09 ≈ 58%. Delay estimates from the square law overstate both the benefit of higher supply and the penalty of lower supply. The simulated inv_1 FO4 delay rises from 72 ps at 1.8 V to 132 ps at 1.4 V (see lesson 42).

4. From current to an effective resistance

To discharge a load C from VDD to VDD/2 through a current I(V) that depends on the output voltage, integrate:

t50 = C ∫VDD/2VDD dV / I(V)

Two shortcuts follow. If the current were constant at Ion, t50 = C(VDD/2)/Ion. If instead we want an equivalent resistor, so that the same circuit can be treated as an RC with t50 = ln 2 · RC = 0.69 RC, the resistor is the average of V/I over the half swing. A common approximation for that average is Reff ≈ (3/4)VDD/Ion, which assumes saturation with modest λ.

5. The gate is a capacitor: Cox, W, L and overlap

The receiving end of the net is a MOS capacitor. The gate oxide thickness in the bundled models is toxe = 4.148 nm for the NMOS and 4.23 nm for the PMOS. With εox = 3.9 ε0,

Cox = εox/tox = (3.9 × 8.854×10−12 F/m) / 4.148 nm = 8.32 fF/µm² (NMOS), 8.16 fF/µm² (PMOS)

The channel term is CoxWL: 8.32 × 0.65 × 0.15 = 0.81 fF for the NMOS and 8.16 × 1.0 × 0.15 = 1.22 fF for the PMOS. The gate also overlaps the source and drain diffusions at both edges. The model’s overlap parameters are 0.245 fF/µm (NMOS) and 0.0593 fF/µm (PMOS) per edge, so two edges add 2 × (0.245 × 0.65 + 0.0593 × 1.0) = 0.44 fF.

The estimate is 7% above Liberty, and the gap is physics, not arithmetic. CoxWL is the full channel capacitance only when the channel is inverted and connected to the source and drain. In saturation the drain end is pinched off, and the gate sees roughly two-thirds of the channel; when the device is off, the channel term is the much smaller depletion capacitance. The Liberty number is an average over a real input edge. During that edge the output moves the opposite way, so the gate-to-drain overlap capacitance sees up to twice the voltage change: the Miller effect. It shows in Figure 12 as the small bump above 1.8 V at the start of each falling output.

6. FO4: the first delay you can predict from physics

The fan-out-of-four inverter delay, FO4, is an inverter driving four copies of itself. It is the yardstick for a process because it combines the device current, the gate capacitance and the self-loading in one number. For inv_1 the load is 4 × 2.302 = 9.208 fF and the falling output also carries its own 4.05 fF.

Check your understanding — if the PMOS in inv_1 were made twice as wide, what happens to the FO4 rise delay?

The PMOS resistance halves to about 4.9 kΩ, but the PMOS gate capacitance doubles from 1.22 to about 2.45 fF of channel plus its overlap, and so does its drain capacitance. Each of the four loads grows from about 2.3 to about 3.6 fF, and the driver’s self-load grows too. The rise improves by much less than half, and the fall gets slower because the NMOS now drives more capacitance. That is why cells are sized for the path, not for one edge.

7. Contacts and vias: small resistors in series

A contact or via is a short vertical plug of conductor through the dielectric between two layers. Its resistance is set by its cross-section, height and the interfaces at each end, and it is quoted per cut rather than per square. Every layer change on the way up and back down adds one in series. Multiple cuts side by side are in parallel and divide the resistance by their number, as long as the current spreads evenly.

8. Wires: resistance by the square, capacitance by the micrometer

A uniform wire of resistivity ρ, length L, width W and thickness t has R = ρL/(Wt). Because the thickness is fixed by the process, the PDK quotes the sheet resistance Rs = ρ/t in ohms per square, and R = Rs × (L/W): count how many W × W squares fit along the wire. Capacitance per micrometer is a plate term to the layer below plus two edge terms, and on narrow wires the edges dominate (lesson 28 derives these from the LEF).

9. Distributed RC and the Elmore delay

For a tree of resistors and capacitors driven by a step, the Elmore delay to a node is the sum, over every resistor on the path from the source, of that resistance times all the capacitance downstream of it. It is the first moment of the impulse response, an upper bound on the 50% delay for RC trees, and simple enough to do by hand. Applied to an N-section ladder of total R and C it gives RC(N + 1)/(2N): 1.0 RC for one section, 0.75 RC for two, 0.55 RC for ten, and RC/2 in the limit of a continuous wire.

The Elmore value is a time constant, not a 50% point. For a single lumped RC the 50% point is ln 2 × τ = 0.69 τ. For a distributed wire the response starts late and then rises steeply, and the 50% point is 0.380 RC against an Elmore value of 0.5 RC. So the practical recipe, used throughout this lesson, is: multiply lumped terms by 0.69, and the wire’s own distributed term by 0.38. The Elmore sum itself, without those factors, is a safe upper bound. OpenROAD’s placement-stage estimate_parasitics builds exactly these RC trees from per-layer values; OpenSTA’s delay calculator then reduces each tree to a driver-load model that is more accurate than a bare Elmore sum.

12. Logical effort and buffer trees

Logical effort writes the delay of a stage as d = gh + p in units of τ. h = Cout/Cin is the electrical effort, g is how much worse than an inverter the gate is at producing current from a given input capacitance (1 for an inverter, 4/3 for a textbook NAND2), and p is the parasitic delay (about 1 for an inverter). Lesson 11 introduced it; here we use the SKY130 τ of 14.4 ps from section 6. Its main result is that a path of total effort F is fastest with N = log4F stages of equal effort, each about 4. That is why “fanout of 4” is the design rule of thumb.

Take one inv_1 that must drive 64 inv_1 inputs, 147 fF. The total effort is 64, so log464 = 3 stages is the predicted optimum. Figure 14 draws four ways to do it and compares Liberty delays.

Use logical effort to choose a structure, then use the timer for the number. Clock-tree synthesis (lesson 35) builds exactly these trees, with the additional goal of equal delay to every leaf.

Check your understanding — why not use one enormous inv_16 to drive the 64 loads directly?

Its own input is about 16 × 2.3 ≈ 37 fF, so the inv_1 before it now sees an electrical effort of 16 and becomes the slow stage. Logical effort balances the ratio across stages: two stages of about 8 each (option B) or three of about 4 each (option C) beat one stage of 64 or a 1-then-16 split.

13. Wire length: linear, then quadratic, then repeaters

Figure 15 sweeps the route length from 10 µm to 2 mm for met1 and met3, driven by one or four inv_1 device pairs, with the Table 91 stacks at both ends. On a short wire the delay grows linearly with length, because the wire is just more capacitance for the driver: the 0.69ReffCw term. The wire’s own 0.38RwCw term grows as L² and eventually dominates.

A useful marker is the length where the wire term alone equals one FO4 delay: 0.38 r c L² = 72 ps, or L = 1.58 mm for minimum met1 and 3.75 mm for minimum met3. Beyond it, the remedy is to cut the wire into k segments and drive each with a repeater. Each segment’s RC falls as 1/k², so the wire term falls as 1/k, while the repeaters add k gate delays. Bakoglu’s classic optimum, for a repeater h times an inverter of resistance R0 and input capacitance C0, is

kopt = √(0.4 RwCw / 0.7 R0C0)    hopt = √(R0Cw / RwC0)

For 6 mm of minimum met1 with inv_1 as the unit (R0 = 6.53 kΩ, the average of the Liberty rise and fall fits, C0 = 2.302 fF): Rw = 5357 Ω, Cw = 508 fF, kopt = 10.2 and hopt = 16.4. Including the repeater’s own drain capacitance, kopt drops to 6.2.

The simulation agrees with the shape of the theory and corrects its numbers. With inv16 repeaters (close to hopt), 6 mm drops from 1192 ps unrepeated to 825 ps with four segments, and the minimum is flat from four to six. With inv4 repeaters, too small for this wire, the best gain is only 9%. At 3 mm, which is below twice the met1 critical length, every repeater makes the wire slower. In LibreLane, repeater insertion is the repair_design step of OpenROAD’s resizer, run after global placement, driven by maximum wire length, slew and capacitance limits; it then sizes the buffers against the same RC estimates.

In the open SKY130 flow. Before routing, OpenROAD estimates every net from placement with per-layer R and C set by set_layer_rc and set_wire_rc (the ORFS setRC.tcl values above). After routing, OpenRCX extracts the real shapes, including vias, into SPEF, and OpenSTA reports delay per stage with the wire’s share separated from the cell’s. LibreLane runs these as its resizer, RCX and STA steps.

The driver. With inv_1 the driver term is about 95% of the estimate, so inv_4 cuts the delay by more than half. met3 and doubled cuts each change it by a few picoseconds. The inv_4 does present four times the input capacitance to the gate before it, which the timer will charge to that stage.

An 11-stage inv_1 ring, measured in ngspice 44.2 under the same models, has a 24.58 ps stage delay at 1.80 V — 0.341 of this lesson’s FO4 — in lesson 36.

Key takeaways

  • A gate delay is a current charging a capacitor. For SKY130 inv_1 at tt, 1.8 V, the NMOS carries 336 µA and the PMOS 148 µA, and their effective resistances over the half swing are 4.2 and 9.8 kΩ.
  • Short-channel SKY130 NMOS is nearly linear in overdrive (α ≈ 1.09). The square law overstates its current by four times at 1.8 V.
  • gmrout ≈ 9 per device and an inverter gain of about −19 restore logic levels; they barely enter delay.
  • Cox is 8.3 fF/µm²; CoxWL plus overlap estimates the inv_1 input as 2.47 fF against Liberty’s 2.30 fF.
  • FO4 is 72 ps (τ ≈ 14 ps). A resistor model gets the load term right; the input slew adds the rest.
  • Cuts range from 0.38 Ω (via4) to 152 Ω (mcon) each in Table 91; the two sources disagree by 16 times on mcon. They matter for strong drivers and for current.
  • Elmore: each R times all C downstream. Use 0.69 for lumped terms and 0.38 for the wire’s own RC.
  • Delay is linear in fanout (about 10 ps per inv_1 load for inv_1) and in short wire length. The wire term grows as L² and passes one FO4 at about 1.6 mm of minimum met1; here repeaters paid off at 6 mm but not at 3 mm.
