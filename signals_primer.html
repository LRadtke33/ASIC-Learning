Signals Primer: DC, AC, RMS, Decibels & the FFT

Analog and mixed-signal lessons describe voltages as levels, waveforms and spectra. This lesson defines the units and measures they use, from the RMS value of a sine wave to what a fast Fourier transform actually computes.

What you will learn

  • Use SI prefixes and the course’s units without conversion mistakes.
  • Describe a signal by its DC level, amplitude, frequency and RMS value, and express ratios in decibels.
  • Explain a time constant, a −3 dB bandwidth, and what the DFT and FFT compute — bins, Nyquist frequency, leakage and windows.

Four relations cover most of the arithmetic in later lessons: Ohm’s law V = I·R; a capacitor stores charge Q = C·V and passes current I = C·dV/dt; an inductor develops V = L·di/dt; and power is P = V·I. Units multiply too: 1 kΩ × 1 pF = 1 ns, and 1 kΩ × 1 fF = 1 ps.

2. DC, AC and the sine wave

DC (direct current) originally meant current that flows in one direction. In circuit language a DC value is a constant: a supply voltage, a bias current, or the average level of a signal. AC (alternating current) is the part that changes. Any waveform can be split into its DC (average) value plus an AC part.

The basic AC signal is the sine wave, v(t) = VDC + A·sin(2πft + φ). Its amplitude A is the peak deviation from the DC level, so the peak-to-peak swing is 2A. Its frequency f, in hertz (Hz, cycles per second), sets the period T = 1/f. Its phase φ says where in the cycle it starts. Engineers often write ω = 2πf, the angular frequency in radians per second.

Circuit simulators use the same words. A DC analysis finds the steady operating point. An AC analysis linearizes the circuit around that point and sweeps a small sine wave across frequency (lesson 73). A transient analysis computes waveforms step by step in time.

3. RMS: the value that matches power

RMS (root mean square) is computed exactly as the name says: square the waveform, take the mean over time, then the square root. It is the DC value that would deliver the same power to a resistor. For a sine of amplitude A the RMS value is A/√2 ≈ 0.707A. For a signal with both parts, VRMS,total = √(VDC² + VAC,RMS²).

Noise has no single amplitude, so it is described by its RMS value (lesson 75). Independent RMS quantities add as the square root of the sum of squares, not directly.

Worked example. The 0.8 V-amplitude sine in Figure 1 has an AC RMS value of 0.8/√2 = 0.566 V. Including its 0.9 V DC level, the total RMS is √(0.81 + 0.32) = 1.063 V. Two independent noise sources of 200 µV and 150 µV RMS together give √(200² + 150²) = 250 µV RMS, not 350 µV.

4. Decibels

A decibel (dB) expresses a ratio on a logarithmic scale. For power, dB = 10·log10(P2/P1). For voltage or current amplitudes, dB = 20·log10(V2/V1), because power goes as the square of amplitude. Gains in series multiply, so their decibel values add.

A suffix names the reference. dBc is relative to the carrier — the main tone of a measurement. dBFS is relative to the full scale of a converter. dBV is relative to 1 V RMS. A harmonic at −60 dBc has one-thousandth of the fundamental’s amplitude (lesson 81).

5. Time constants and bandwidth

When a resistor R charges a capacitor C after a step, the voltage approaches its final value exponentially: v(t) = Vfinal·(1 − e−t/τ), with time constant τ = RC. After one τ the output has covered 63.2% of the step; after three, 95.0%; after five, 99.3%. Settling to a tighter error takes more time constants: to within a fraction ε needs t = τ·ln(1/ε) (lesson 79).

The same circuit, viewed in frequency, is a low-pass filter. Its gain is 1/√(1 + (f/fc)²), with corner frequency fc = 1/(2πRC). At fc the output power has halved: −3 dB. That frequency is the circuit’s bandwidth. Above it the gain falls by 20 dB per decade of frequency. The frequency where a response bends like this is called a pole, and a plot of gain in dB and phase against log frequency is a Bode plot (lesson 73).

6. Time domain and frequency domain

A waveform plotted against time is the time domain. The same information can be shown as how much of the signal lies at each frequency: its spectrum, in the frequency domain. Any repeating waveform is a sum of sines at its fundamental frequency and at integer multiples of it, called harmonics. A pure sine has one line. A square wave has lines at the odd harmonics.

That is why spectra are so useful for analog testing. Drive a circuit with a pure sine; any harmonic in its output was created by the circuit’s nonlinearity — distortion. Lines at other frequencies point to other sources, such as a clock leaking into the signal path (lesson 83).

7. Sampling, bins and the FFT

An analog-to-digital converter, an oscilloscope or a simulator captures a signal as samples taken every 1/fs seconds; fs is the sample rate. Only frequencies below fs/2 — the Nyquist frequency — can be represented unambiguously. A tone above it produces exactly the same samples as one below it; it aliases (lesson 79).

The DFT (discrete Fourier transform) takes a record of N samples and returns N complex numbers, one for each frequency bin. Bin m represents frequency m·fs/N, so the bins are spaced fs/N apart — the reciprocal of the record’s duration. The magnitude of each result is how much of the signal lies in that bin; its angle is the phase. For a real signal only bins 0 to N/2 are independent, covering DC up to the Nyquist frequency.

Computed directly, the DFT needs about N² multiplications. The FFT (fast Fourier transform) is a family of algorithms, popularized by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, that computes exactly the same result in about N·log2N operations. For N = 4096 that is roughly 49,000 instead of 16.8 million. “Take an FFT” therefore means “compute the DFT, quickly”. Whether the result means anything depends on the measurement choices: record length, sample rate, scaling, and the window (lesson 81).

Try it: a 64-point DFT of one tone

The demonstration shows the two central ideas. If a tone completes a whole number of cycles in the record — coherent sampling — all of its energy lands in one bin. If not, the DFT, which treats the record as one period of an endlessly repeating signal, sees a jump at the joint and spreads energy into other bins: spectral leakage. A window such as the Hann window tapers both ends of the record to remove the jump; it lowers distant leakage at the cost of a wider peak and a known correction factor.

Worked example. The FFT lesson uses N = 4096 samples at fs = 1 MHz. The bins are 1 MHz / 4096 = 244.14 Hz apart, the Nyquist frequency is 500 kHz, and a tone completing 67 cycles in the record sits at 67 × 244.14 Hz = 16,357.42 Hz, exactly on bin 67.

Check your understanding — what are the time constant and the 99.3% settling time of 1 kΩ and 2 pF?

τ = 1 kΩ × 2 pF = 2 ns. Five time constants, 10 ns, reach 99.3% of the final value.

Check your understanding — a record has N = 1024 samples at 10 MHz. What is the bin spacing, and how long is the record?

Bin spacing = 10 MHz / 1024 = 9.766 kHz. Record length = 1024 / 10 MHz = 102.4 µs. They are reciprocals: finer frequency resolution needs a longer record.

Key takeaways

  • Keep prefixes straight: 1 kΩ × 1 pF = 1 ns; 1 kΩ × 1 fF = 1 ps.
  • A signal is a DC level plus an AC part; a sine’s RMS value is its amplitude divided by √2.
  • Use 10·log₁₀ for power ratios and 20·log₁₀ for amplitude ratios; dBc and dBFS name the reference.
  • τ = RC sets both settling (63% per τ) and bandwidth (fc = 1/2πRC, −3 dB).
  • The DFT turns N samples into bins spaced fs/N; the FFT computes it quickly; non-integer cycles cause leakage.
