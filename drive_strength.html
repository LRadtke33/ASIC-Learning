Drive Strength & Transistor Sizing

What the X1 / X4 / X16 on cell names physically means, why PMOS are drawn wider, and how big drivers are built.

What you will learn

  • Estimate switching delay with an effective-resistance model of the driver.
  • Explain why PMOS devices are drawn wider and how large drivers are folded into fingers.
  • Read SKY130 drive suffixes (inv_1 to inv_8) as parallel devices, and weigh speed against input load.

Why it matters

1. An effective resistance helps estimate switching delay

Lesson 10 showed a gate sourcing and sinking current into a load. For a first delay estimate, replace the switching transistor by an effective resistance and represent the wire and receiver inputs by capacitance. A real MOSFET changes operating region during the edge, so this resistance is a fitted approximation rather than a constant physical resistor. The familiar 50% RC estimate is:

delay ≈ 0.7 · Rdriver · Cload

For a fixed device type, length, terminal voltages and operating regime, increasing total channel width usually increases drive and input capacitance. An X4 suffix describes a library strength option; it does not universally guarantee four times the width, current, or speed. The ideal resistor model below deliberately assumes R scales as 1/W and ignores internal parasitics. Section 5 checks what the SKY130 inverter family actually implements.

2. Why the PMOS is wider than the NMOS

Electrons and holes have different mobility, so comparable bulk-CMOS NMOS and PMOS devices generally do not carry equal current at the same geometry. A PMOS-to-NMOS width ratio around two is a useful classroom starting point, not a process constant. Threshold options, velocity saturation, bias, layout and required rise/fall balance all affect the chosen ratio.

3. How big transistors are drawn: fingers

The solution is folding: connect several device fingers in parallel and tie their gates, sources and drains appropriately. Four equal fingers give approximately four times the total channel width, but shared diffusion changes junction capacitance and resistance. More fingers often cost width in sites; spare room can allow two strengths to share an outline, as the SKY130 inv_1/inv_2 example shows.

4. Choosing sizes: nothing is free

Why not make every cell large? The input of each cell also loads its predecessor. Increasing width usually increases pin and internal capacitance, area, dynamic energy and peak rail current. Upsizing can improve a heavily loaded output while making the preceding stage slower. The best size depends on the entire path and routing, not only one gate:

  • Small drives (X1, X2) for short local nets and non-critical paths — cheap in area, power, and upstream loading.
  • Large drives (X8, X16…) for long wires, high fanout, and critical paths.
  • Swapping is easy by design: all strengths of a cell share the same logical footprint and pin names, so synthesis and place-and-route freely upsize/downsize cells during timing and power optimization — one reason placements leave the ECO (engineering change order) space described in lesson 53.
  • Very large loads often need a buffer chain. A stage-effort target around 3–4 is a useful first estimate for simple paths. Required polarity, branch loads, wire resistance, allowed slew and characterized intrinsic delay change the optimum. Tools select discrete cells and may build a branching buffer tree.

5. These SkyWater inverter strengths use parallel devices, and inv_2 is not wider

This library names the strengths inv_1, inv_2, inv_4, and inv_8. The suffix is how many copies of the inv_1 transistor the cell uses, not how many sites it occupies, and not the X1 / X4 label in the figures above. The SPICE netlists of inv_1, inv_2, and inv_4 are one, two, and four of each transistor. Every finger is the same device: NMOS 0.65 µm, high-Vt PMOS 1.0 µm, length 0.15 µm. inv_8's input antenna area is 1.980 µm², eight times inv_1's 0.2475 µm², which is eight copies of that same gate.

Folding does not always make the cell wider. inv_1 and inv_2 are both 1.38 µm, three sites. The second finger fits in the same outline. inv_4 is 2.30 µm, five sites. inv_8 is 4.14 µm, nine sites. Five and nine, rather than four and eight: in this inverter family the suffix matches the parallel-device count; the site count also includes layout overhead. Other cell families need not follow the same transistor-count rule.

The rise and fall columns are the same comparison: typical corner, 25°C, 1.80 V, input edge 0.01 ns, load 9.21 fF, which is four inv_1 inputs. Each number is a linear interpolation along the load axis of that cell's own table. It is not a raw table entry. The input column is the Liberty capacitance in femtofarads.

The table’s rising edges are slower than its falling edges at these conditions. Doubling the fingers does not halve the measured delay because intrinsic device/interconnect capacitances and internal delay remain. The larger input capacitance burdens the preceding stage; it is not a load that this inverter’s own output charges.

Input antenna gate area in these four inverter variants scales 1:2:4:8. Rise/fall pin capacitances need not be equal. The output max_capacitance is an electrical design-rule limit at the named corner; timing constraints and max_transition also apply. Table coverage and design-rule limits must both be checked rather than inferred from the strength suffix.

inv_2 being the same three sites as inv_1 matters to the placer. Swapping _1 for _2 does not push the neighbors sideways. Swapping in _4 does: the cell grows by two sites. The "same footprint, any strength" sentence in section 4 is the goal for pin names and the row height. On this inverter the outline is shared by _1 and _2 only.

6. Logical effort is the textbook size, in units of an inverter

Harris's logical-effort count writes delay as d = gh + p. Electrical effort h is Cout / Cin. Logical effort g is how much input capacitance a gate needs to drive as hard as an inverter. The inverter is defined as g = 1, p = 1, so d = h + 1. A fanout of 4 is h = 4, so d = 5. That 5 is in units of the process delay. It is not a picosecond count for SkyWater.

The count that produces g = 4/3 for a NAND2 and g = 5/3 for a NOR2 takes the PMOS as twice the NMOS, and widens a series device until the stack matches the inverter. Section 2 already measured this library: inv_1 is 1.00 µm over 0.65 µm, a ratio of 1.54, and the PMOS is high-Vt. The delay a path is signed off with is the Liberty table (lesson 23).

Worked example — a 3-stage path driving 100 fF

Logical effort writes a stage delay as d = gh + p, in units of a process’s basic inverter delay τ. For a path of N stages, the path electrical effort is H = Cout / Cin, the path logical effort is the product of the stage g values, and the branching effort B accounts for each stage’s off-path load (here B = 1). The stage effort that minimizes the path delay is ρ = (GBH)1/N, and the path delay is D = Nρ + P with P = Σ p.

Take three inverters (g = 1, p = 1 each) whose first input is an HD inv_1, Cin = 2.302 fF (this course’s nominal pin capacitance for pin A, the same number used in lesson 21). The load is 100 fF.

  • G = 1 × 1 × 1 = 1, B = 1, H = 100 / 2.302 = 43.440, so F = GBH = 43.440.
  • ρ = 43.4401/3 = 3.516.
  • Each stage’s input capacitance is the previous stage’s output: 2.302 fF, then 2.302 × 3.516 = 8.094 fF, then 8.094 × 3.516 = 28.46 fF, and 28.46 × 3.516 = 100.0 fF, which closes on the load.
  • Path delay D = 3 × 3.516 + 3 × 1 = 13.55 τ. An FO4 inverter has d = 1×4 + 1 = 5 τ, so this path is 13.55 / 5 ≈ 2.71 FO4.

Those capacitances are a sizing target, not a claim that the HD library has a cell at exactly 8.094 fF. In the library you would pick the nearest inv_n (lesson 11’s parallel-device strengths) and re-check H. Worked checkpoint — Does inv_4 give four times the speed?

For the earlier comparison’s same 10 ps input transition and 9.208 fF external load, nominal rising delays are about 71 ps for inv_1 and 37 ps for inv_4. The speed ratio is 71/37 ≈ 1.92, using rounded table values. Input capacitance rises from 2.302 to 9.004 fF, a factor of 3.91. The stronger output therefore adds substantially more load to its predecessor; a path-level timing check decides whether upsizing helps overall.

Check your understanding — replacing an inv_1 with an inv_4 speeds up that stage. Why can the whole path still get slower?

The inv_4 input is about four times heavier: 9.00 fF against 2.30 fF for inv_1 in the HD Liberty data. The gate driving it now sees more load and slows down. Upsizing helps only when the stage’s own load dominates; otherwise it moves the delay upstream. Buffer chains and logical effort manage that trade.

Lesson 29 derives these effective resistances from SKY130 device curves, compares them with values fitted to the Liberty tables, and works logical effort and a buffer tree through a fanout example.

Key takeaways

  • An effective RC model helps estimate delay. Increasing width usually improves drive but also adds capacitance; compare actual strength options using characterized timing and the surrounding path.
  • An X4 suffix is a library drive-strength choice. Fourfold current or speed is an idealized comparison, not a universal guarantee; use the characterized tables.
  • A PMOS width around twice the NMOS is a teaching approximation. Actual balanced sizing depends on the device models and operating point.
  • Fixed row height encourages folding into parallel fingers. More fingers may require more sites, although the checked inv_1 and inv_2 share an outline.
  • Input capacitance scales with W too — upsizing loads the previous stage; sizing is a trade of speed vs area, power, and upstream load.
  • Large loads often benefit from buffer chains or trees; choose stage sizes using the actual loads, slew constraints and timing models.
  • SkyWater's inv_1/2/4/8 are 1, 2, 4, and 8 fingers of 0.65 / 1.0 µm. inv_2 is still 3 sites. Into 9.21 fF the rise goes 71, 47, 37, 31 ps.
  • Logical effort compares a gate with an inverter in textbook units. inv_1 is ratio 1.54, and the sign-off delay is the Liberty table.
