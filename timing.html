Lesson 25 / 84 · Part IV, Loads, timing and interconnect

Timing

Delay, slew, and the setup and hold checks on a SkyWater flip-flop. The numbers are the Liberty tables from lesson 23, read at a real load.

Where the numbers come from. Unless a corner is named, the corner is tt_025C_1v80: 25°C, 1.80 V. A nanosecond is 1000 ps.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish delay (50% to 50%) from slew (20% to 80% in this library), and pass slew from stage to stage.
  • Read the setup and hold numbers of dfxtp_1, including what a negative hold means.
  • Add arcs along a path, and see how corners move both delays and constraints.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

Why it matters
Timing closure decides whether the chip runs at its target clock frequency.

Verified example. The numerical cell values are from inv_1 and dfxtp_1, HD revision ac7fb61f06e6. Waveforms are drawings consistent with the table crossings, not transistor-simulation traces.

1. Delay is when it arrives. Slew is how long the edge takes.

Take sky130_fd_sc_hd__inv_1 with a fast input edge (0.01 ns from 20% to 80%) and a load of 0.00952 pF, about four of its own inputs. The cell_rise table says the output reaches half of VDD 0.0728 ns after the input reached half of VDD. That interval is the delay. Half of 1.80 V is 0.90 V.

The edge is not a step. After the output crosses 0.90 V it still has to finish the swing. The rise_transition table says the climb from 20% to 80% (0.36 V to 1.44 V) takes 0.0877 ns. That interval is the slew. Delay and slew are neighbors in the file, and they are not the same question.

The fall at this same square is quicker, because the pull-down is the standard-Vt NMOS and the pull-up is a narrower-than-balanced high-Vt PMOS (lesson 21). cell_fall is 0.0455 ns and fall_transition is 0.0477 ns.

The published thresholds are slew_lower_threshold_pct_rise : 20, slew_upper_threshold_pct_rise : 80, with corresponding fall thresholds and slew_derate_from_library : 1.0. Therefore the table transition is the actual 20–80% interval (80–20% for a fall). Other libraries may apply a scaling factor; a value of 0.5 alone does not prove a 0–100% waveform convention. Match thresholds and scaling before comparing SPICE with Liberty.

2. The output slew becomes the next input slew

A path is a chain of these lookups. The load on gate 1 sets its delay and its output slew. That output slew is the input slew of gate 2, whose own load then sets the next delay. The fastest row in the table, 0.01 ns, is sharper than an edge a real gate produces. Using it for every stage makes the path look too fast.

Follow one rising step. Gate 1 rises in 0.0728 ns and hands gate 2 an input edge of 0.0877 ns. Gate 2 is another inv_1 with the same 9.52 fF load, so its output falls. Between the 0.053 ns and 0.122 ns input-slew rows, that fall delay is 0.074 ns, not the 0.0455 ns on the fastest row. The slower incoming edge cost about 29 ps.

The 0.074 ns figure is a straight-line read of cell_fall at input slew 0.08768 ns, which is the rise_transition just produced, and at the same load column 0.009521 pF. The two rows used are 0.05313 ns (fall delay 0.0616 ns) and 0.1225 ns (fall delay 0.0873 ns).

A timer does this for every pin-to-pin arc, adds the wire delay from the extracted resistance and capacitance, and compares the arrival time with the required time. The difference is the slack, and negative slack is a timing failure. Along a whole path the same addition simply repeats, arc after arc.

Clock-to-Q is the same kind of arc on a flip-flop. For sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1, with a 0.01 ns clock edge and a load of 0.00900 pF, cell_rise on Q is 0.328 ns and cell_fall is 0.304 ns. The arc is timing_type : rising_edge and timing_sense : non_unate, because Q can rise or fall depending on D. The cell's state description is clocked_on : "CLK", next_state : "D".

3. Setup and hold are checks on the flip-flop

sky130_fd_sc_hd__dfxtp_1 is a rising-edge D flip-flop, 16 sites (area 20.0192 µm²). Lesson 20 opened a flop up. These are the timing rules that flop has to satisfy. Both rules are tables, indexed by the clock slew and the data slew. The numbers below are the corner of that table where both edges are 0.01 ns.

Setup is how long D must already be valid before the clock edge. When D is falling the requirement is 0.103 ns. When D is rising it is 0.051 ns. The falling data is the harder check.

Hold constrains the earliest following data transition relative to the capturing clock reference crossing. Here it is −0.047 ns for a falling transition and −0.029 ns for a rising transition. A negative number permits that particular pin transition before the clock reference crossing, under the characterized slew and operating conditions. It does not remove the hold check. In a simple internal-delay picture, hold_external ≈ clock_delay + hold_internal − data_delay; a sufficiently slow data path can make external hold negative. The precise cell behavior is the characterized table.

The full 3×3 setup and hold tables, the straight-line read between data slews, the slack arithmetic, and the minimum pulse width are lesson 26.

Pin capacitance on this flop, typical corner: CLK is 1.79 fF, D is 1.68 fF. Q's max_capacitance is 0.162 pF. The clock pin is a small load electrically and a large load in count, because every flop in the block adds one.

4. PVT changes the path and the constraints

Stretch the transistors and the combinational delay grows, so data arrives later and setup gets harder. Speed the transistors up and data can race through a short path and change the next flop too soon, so hold gets harder. The flop's own tables move too. At the same 0.01 ns edges:

In these selected tables, setup and clock-to-Q grow in the slow case, while rising-data hold is closer to zero in the named fast case. Those observations do not establish a universal worst corner. Check both transition polarities and all project-approved PVT, interconnect and variation scenarios; clock skew, temperature dependence and constraint changes can alter which case limits the design.

The fast column is ff_n40C_1v76, not the ff_n40C_1v95 file that some flow configurations load. It is cold, but the supply is 1.76 V, a little under the typical 1.80 V. Lesson 22 showed a hotter, higher-voltage fast file beating this one on the inverter rise and losing on the fall. Pick the file by the corner the project signs off, then read the arc. Do not carry a single "fast factor" from one cell to another.

Hold slack is roughly the data delay — including the launching flop’s clock-to-Q — minus the capturing flop’s hold requirement, minus how much later the capturing clock arrives. A negative hold requirement on the cell adds margin to that sum, and a faster data path, which is what the fast corner produces, uses the margin up. Setup slack is the clock period plus any extra delay of the capturing clock, minus the data delay and the setup requirement. A longer period therefore fixes setup but not hold, because the period does not appear in the hold check at all.

5. A path is a sum of arcs

One timing path is a chain of the arcs from lesson 23. The launch flop contributes a rising_edge clock-to-Q. Each gate contributes the combinational arc for the edge it actually produces. The capture flop contributes setup_rising or hold_rising.

A clock-to-Q arc is non_unate relative to CLK: a rising clock can produce either Q polarity, depending on the stored data. The timer analyzes the applicable rising and falling Q arcs and propagates polarity through the combinational logic. Add clock-to-Q, gate delays and wire delays to obtain data arrival. The capture setup or hold value defines a constraint; it is not a propagation delay to be added to the data arrival.

In the open SKY130 flow. OpenSTA prints each path this way: one line per cell or wire with its delay and the running arrival time, ending with the required time and the slack. Reading a few of these reports from a real run is the quickest way to connect this lesson to a design. PrimeTime and Tempus print the same structure.

report_checks -path_delay max -fields {slew capacitance} -digits 3

6. A practical handoff check

Suppose an analog control output takes 0.20 ns to cross 20–80%, while a digital bench assumed 0.01 ns. Timing must use the 0.20 ns input transition and the proper receiving pin. If the signal is asynchronous, clean slew does not establish a clock relationship: it also needs a CDC strategy. A comparator’s noisy threshold crossing may produce repeated events.

Checkpoint: capture clock is delayed by 50 ps. What changes?

With the same data delays and clock pairing, setup slack improves by 50 ps and hold slack worsens by 50 ps. The sign follows directly from the capture-clock term in the equations in lesson 26.

Check your understanding — a second inv_1 follows the first. Why is its delay not the same as the first one’s?

Its input slew is the first inverter’s output slew, not the sharp 0.01 ns edge used for the first stage. A slower input edge gives a longer delay: in this lesson’s example the second inverter falls in about 0.074 ns, noticeably slower than the sharp-edge table value.

Lesson 42 follows one SKY130 register-to-register path through the clock tree, the logic and a scan mux, and adds up every arc by hand for both the setup and the hold check.

Key takeaways

  • Delay is 50% to 50%. Slew is 20% to 80%. For inv_1 at 9.52 fF and a 0.01 ns input edge, the rise delay is 0.0728 ns and the rise slew is 0.0877 ns.
  • That output slew is the next gate's input slew. A second inv_1 then falls in about 0.074 ns, not the 0.045 ns of the fastest table row.
  • On dfxtp_1, with fast edges, setup is 0.103 ns (D falling) or 0.051 ns (D rising). Hold is −0.047 ns and −0.029 ns.
  • Negative hold means the data may change slightly before the clock. Internal data and clock delays shift the external timing boundaries; use the characterized constraint.
  • In the selected examples, the slow corner stretches delay and setup and the fast corner moves hold toward zero. The limiting scenario still needs analysis. The clock period appears in the setup check only. The tables and the slack arithmetic are lesson 26.
  • Data arrival adds launch clock-to-Q, combinational delay and wire delay. The capture setup or hold check defines the required time. Unateness picks which edge each arc uses.
