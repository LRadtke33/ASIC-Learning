The Power Grid & Routing Grids

How power reaches every cell, how voltage is lost along the way, and how routing tracks help digital implementation.

What you will learn

  • Describe the ring, strap and rail structure of a power delivery network.
  • Distinguish routing tracks, preferred directions and the manufacturing grid.
  • Estimate the voltage drop along a rail or strap, and explain why a mesh reduces it.

Where this fits · the flow map is lesson 8

In the flow
The grid is built in step 4, floorplan and power grid, routing tracks are used in step 7, routing, and voltage drop is checked in step 8, extraction and timing.
Tools
OpenROAD: pdngen for the grid, global_route and detailed_route for signals, analyze_power_grid for voltage drop. Commercial: Innovus, Voltus, RedHawk.
Why it matters
A weak grid slows every cell through voltage drop; an over-built one leaves no room to route.

1. The power delivery network (PDN)

Every cell in every row needs VDD and VSS. The M1 rails from the site-row template deliver power along each row, but a thin M1 rail alone can't carry current for a whole chip — the resistance would cause the supply voltage to sag (IR drop) far from the source. So power is delivered through a grid (mesh) built on the thick upper metal layers, dropping down to the rails everywhere via stacked vias.

A PDN is usually planned during floorplanning and refined as placement, current demand, and package connections become known. One common topology is the following; rings and this exact construction order are not mandatory. OpenROAD PDN policies specify the actual layers, widths, domains, and connections.

  1. Core ring — wide VDD and VSS rings around the core area on top metals, fed from the pads/bumps.
  2. Straps (the mesh) — wide vertical straps on one upper layer and horizontal straps on the layer above, at a regular pitch, connected to the ring and to each other with via arrays.
  3. Follow-pin rails — the horizontal M1 rails drawn along every row boundary ("follow pin" because they overlap the rail pins of every cell placed later), with legal via stacks at selected same-net crossings. An M1-to-M4 connection needs intermediate metal landings and each intervening via layer.

The pad-ring buses that feed this grid, and the IR drop along them, are lesson 68.

The planned PDN consumes routing resources. Routers respect its shapes, while placement considers associated blockages, utilization, and power needs. Upper-metal straps can pass over standard cells; a metal obstruction is not automatically a cell-placement blockage. A current can then flow from a bump, around the ring, down a strap, and along an M1 rail into any cell — with many redundant parallel paths, which is exactly what keeps IR drop low.

In the open SKY130 flow. OpenROAD’s pdngen builds the grid from a short description: add_pdn_stripe adds the met1 rails that follow the cell pins and the wider straps on upper metals, add_pdn_ring adds rings, and add_pdn_connect places the vias between layers. LibreLane runs this as its GeneratePDN step and later estimates the voltage drop with analyze_power_grid (IRDropReport). Commercial flows build the grid in Innovus or IC Compiler II and analyze it in Voltus or RedHawk. The widths and pitches below are example values.

add_pdn_stripe -layer met1 -width 0.48 -followpins
add_pdn_stripe -layer met4 -width 1.6 -pitch 153.6
add_pdn_connect -layers {met1 met4}
pdngen

2. Routing grids: tracks

Signal routing is also grid-based. For each metal layer, the technology defines a routing grid — a set of evenly spaced tracks on which wire centerlines are supposed to run. The track spacing (routing pitch) is essentially minimum wire width + minimum spacing (or the via-to-via pitch, whichever is larger), so ordinary wires can often occupy adjacent tracks. Width-dependent spacing, end-of-line rules, via enclosures, neighboring pins, and other checks still apply; being on a track does not prove DRC cleanliness.

Each layer also has a preferred direction, alternating layer by layer so that any two points can be reached with simple L-shapes and vias:

LayerPreferred directionTypical use
M1horizontalinside cells: rails, pins, internal connections
M2verticalshort signal routes, pin access
M3horizontalsignal routing
M4, M5, …alternating V / Hlonger routes, clock, power straps

3. Routing tracks and the manufacturing grid are different

A routing track is a tool convention; the manufacturing grid is a geometry constraint. The HD technology LEF specifies a 0.005 µm manufacturing grid, a 0.34 µm M1 pitch, and a 0.46 µm M2 pitch. A coordinate may be on the manufacturing grid while being between routing tracks. Off-track shapes can be legal. They must still meet the complete process rules.

Standard-cell internal wiring, analog routes, power straps, and pin-access jogs may use several metal layers and may be off track. The router needs accurate pin and obstruction geometry. Keeping ordinary digital routes on the advertised tracks usually makes access and spacing more predictable. Detailed routers can also construct permitted off-track access segments; they do not need a DRC waiver merely because a segment is off track.

  • Capacity: an off-track wire can block neighboring tracks. How many depends on its width, spacing rule, pitch, and offset; “always two” is not a physical law.
  • Vias: a via must satisfy cut, enclosure, and surrounding-metal rules. It need not lie at an intersection of preferred routing tracks in every flow.
  • Abstraction: cell-internal M2 and higher metal must appear in the abstract as pins or obstructions when relevant to routing.

Figure 3 deliberately chooses geometry in which the off-track wire blocks two neighbors. It illustrates a congestion cost, not a universal legality test. See the HD technology LEF and OpenROAD detailed-routing documentation.

4. From netlist to wires: the routing flow

Knowing the tracks exist, how does the router actually use them? A modern design has millions of nets — no tool can draw final geometry for all of them in one pass. Routing is staged, coarse to fine:

  1. Global route. The core is divided into a coarse grid of gcells (each a few routing tracks tall and wide). Every gcell has a known capacity — how many tracks per layer pass through it. The global router assigns each net a corridor of gcells from pin to pin, spreading nets out so no gcell is asked to carry more wires than it has tracks. No real shapes are drawn yet — it's a plan.
  2. Track assignment. Within their corridors, the long straight pieces of each net are assigned to specific tracks on specific layers.
  3. Detail route. The exact wires and vias are drawn: on-track, in the preferred direction per layer, connecting down to the actual pin shapes, obeying every spacing/width/via rule.
  4. Search & repair. A detail pass may leave violations where nets fought for the same resources. The router iterates: rip up the offending wire, re-route it along another track or layer, re-check — toward a DRC-clean design. Unresolved violations must be reported; completion of routing is not proof that signoff checks pass.

Congestion is the number to watch: wherever demand for tracks exceeds capacity, nets take detours — longer wires, more vias, off-preferred jogs — and timing suffers. This is precisely why the placer holds utilization down and spreads cells out (lesson 58): whitespace in the rows is really track supply for the router. And the power grid from section 1, built first, is a fixed obstruction the whole flow simply routes around — its tracks were spent before the first signal net was planned.

In the open SKY130 flow. Global routing is OpenROAD’s global_route (the FastRoute engine): it divides the core into routing cells, gives each net a corridor and reports congestion. Detailed routing is detailed_route (TritonRoute): it draws the wires and vias on tracks and repairs spacing violations until none remain. The commercial counterparts are NanoRoute in Innovus and Zroute in IC Compiler II.

5. A mesh is several paths to the same rail

A single strap reaches a cell through one piece of metal. Every extra micron of that strap is more resistance, and every cell downstream of a break loses the rail.

A mesh crosses straps in both directions, the pattern section 1 already builds with met4 and met5 over the met1 rails. Current has a second path. Lesson 68 does the one numbered drop this course has, on the pad-ring sheet resistance. Section 6 adds a deliberately simplified resistor example. Its current and dimensions are teaching assumptions, not extracted performance.

6. Worked example: supply resistance becomes an analog error

Teaching assumptions: one uniform met4 segment is 500 µm long and 10 µm wide, carrying 2 mA at its far end. Using the HD LEF sheet resistance 0.047 Ω/square gives R = 0.047 × 500/10 = 2.35 Ω and ΔV = 2 mA × 2.35 Ω = 4.7 mV. Vias, temperature change, the ground return, and the upstream network are omitted. This is a resistance calculation, not an EM (electromigration) pass.

Assumed networkEquivalent resistanceDrop at 2 mA
One segment2.35 Ω4.7 mV
Two identical independent segments in parallel1.175 Ω2.35 mV
Same total current uniformly drawn along one segmentFar-end drop = ItotalR/22.35 mV

The last row is a distributed-load calculation: upstream metal carries more current than metal near the end. Real meshes require network analysis with source locations and current maps. OpenROAD PDNSim is a static IR analyzer; a DC solution does not include package L·di/dt or time-varying droop.

Across the boundary: for a digital path, rail loss changes cell delay and noise margin. For an analog circuit it can change bias, output headroom, or reference accuracy. Both sides need the voltage between the local supply and local return, not just an ideal schematic VDD.

Check your understanding: does doubling width halve every source of droop?

No. It halves this segment's resistance at fixed length and sheet resistance. Via resistance, package inductance, source impedance, and other segments remain. A droop budget must include those terms and their timing.

Check your understanding — global routing finds that one region needs 14 tracks on a layer that offers only 10. What happens, and what are the usual fixes?

The region is congested. Detailed routing must detour around it, adding wire length, delay and coupling, or it ends with violations. Typical fixes are spreading the cells there (lower local utilization or placement padding), moving straps or blockages, or making other layers available.

Macros in the grid. A hard macro such as an SRAM interrupts this mesh. Rows, met1 rails and met4 straps stop at its halo, while the met5 straps cross it and drop a via4 onto the macro’s own met4 ring. The SKY130 SRAM case, and how to size a ring you draw around a block, are in lesson 50, section 7.

A grid-level IR map of the Lab 6 SPM (132 µV worst VPWR drop, recorded in OpenROAD) and what LibreLane’s IR step actually runs are lesson 63.

Key takeaways

  • Plan the PDN during floorplanning, then refine it. A ring, mesh, and M1 rails are a common topology, connected only at legal same-net vias.
  • The mesh gives every cell many parallel paths to the supply, keeping IR drop low; it is a fixed obstruction the router works around.
  • Each routing layer has evenly spaced tracks (pitch ≈ wire width + spacing) and a preferred direction that alternates H/V by layer.
  • Routing tracks are distinct from the manufacturing grid. Custom shapes and pin-access jogs can be off track on any layer if their geometry and abstractions are legal.
  • Off-track shapes can consume additional routing capacity. On-track shapes still need spacing, enclosure, and end-of-line checks.
  • Routing runs coarse-to-fine: global route (gcell corridors, congestion-aware) → track assignment → detail route → search & repair iterations until DRC-clean.
  • Congestion = track demand exceeding gcell capacity; it forces detours and hurts timing — placement whitespace is really track supply for the router.
  • A mesh gives the rail a second path. Compute drops using the actual current distribution; a mesh reduces resistance but does not make the supply ideal.
